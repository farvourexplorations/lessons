Phase 1 · Wonder

⚗️ Why Does Chemistry Matter in Biology?

You are made of atoms. Every heartbeat, every thought, every cell division — all of it follows the rules of chemistry. This lesson is about the chemical toolkit that makes life possible.

Your body is about 60% water by mass. Water is just two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom. So why is it the single most critical molecule for life on Earth? What makes H₂O so special — and what would happen if life tried to use a different solvent?

✏️ Your Thinking — Before We Begin

The four elements that make up 96% of living matter are carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, and nitrogen — abbreviated CHON. Everything from your DNA to your cell membranes is built from these four elements arranged in specific molecular structures.

This lesson covers three sections: (1) atoms, isotopes, ions, and chemical bonds — the building blocks; (2) the unique properties of water that make life possible; and (3) why carbon is the backbone of all biological molecules.

Phase 2 · Atoms & Elements

⚛️ Atoms, Isotopes, and Ions

At its most fundamental level, life is made of matter — and matter is made of atoms. Understanding atomic structure tells you why elements behave the way they do in biological systems.

The four elements common to all living organisms are oxygen (O), carbon (C), hydrogen (H), and nitrogen (N). In humans, oxygen makes up 65% of body mass, carbon 18%, hydrogen 10%, and nitrogen 3%. Non-living matter has very different proportions.

Atom — The smallest unit of matter that retains all the chemical properties of an element. Composed of a nucleus (protons + neutrons) surrounded by electrons in orbitals. More than 99% of an atom's volume is empty space.
Protons (+1 charge), Neutrons (0 charge), Electrons (−1 charge) — Protons and neutrons are in the nucleus (~1 amu each). Electrons are in orbitals and have negligible mass. In a neutral atom, protons = electrons. The number of protons defines the element.
Isotope — Different forms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons. Example: Carbon-12 has 6 neutrons; Carbon-14 has 8 neutrons. Same element, different mass. Don't confuse isotope with ion!
Radioisotope — An unstable isotope that undergoes radioactive decay, releasing energy. Carbon-14 (half-life 5,730 years) is used in carbon dating to determine the age of formerly living organisms up to ~50,000 years old. Potassium-40 (half-life 1.25 billion years) dates older fossils.
Valence Electrons — Electrons in the outermost electron shell (valence shell). These are the electrons involved in chemical bonding. The number of valence electrons determines an element's chemical behavior. Carbon has 4 valence electrons — allowing up to 4 bonds.
Octet Rule — Atoms are most stable when their outermost shell contains 8 electrons (or 2, for the first shell). Atoms form bonds to fill their valence shells. This drives all chemical bonding. Noble gases (He, Ne, Ar) already have full shells — they almost never bond.
Ion — An atom that has gained or lost electrons, giving it a net charge. Cation (+) — lost electrons (e.g., Na⁺). Anion (−) — gained electrons (e.g., Cl⁻). Ions are crucial in biology: Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, and Cl⁻ are key electrolytes for nerve impulses and muscle contraction.
Atomic Number vs Atomic Mass — Atomic number = number of protons (defines the element, never changes). Atomic mass = protons + neutrons. To find neutrons: atomic mass − atomic number. Carbon's atomic number is 6; atomic mass is ~12 → 6 neutrons.

Phase 3 · Chemical Bonds

🔗 Chemical Bonds & Molecular Interactions

Atoms bond together to form molecules by sharing or transferring electrons to fill their valence shells. The type of bond determines the molecule's properties — and its biological role. Tap each bond type to expand it.

⚡ Ionic Bond
Formed when one atom donates an electron to another. The atom that loses an electron becomes a positive cation; the atom that gains becomes a negative anion. These opposite charges attract — forming an ionic bond.
📌 Classic example: NaCl (table salt). Na donates its 1 valence electron to Cl (which needs 1 to fill its shell). Result: Na⁺ and Cl⁻ attract. | AP note: Ionic bonds are weaker than covalent in biological systems — they break easily in water (dissociation).
🤝 Nonpolar Covalent Bond
Electrons are shared equally between two atoms of the same element or atoms with similar electronegativity. No partial charges develop — the molecule is nonpolar. Nonpolar molecules are hydrophobic (don't mix with water).
📌 Examples: O₂ (oxygen gas), N₂ (nitrogen gas), CH₄ (methane). The carbon-hydrogen bonds in lipids are nonpolar covalent — this is why fats don't dissolve in water.
⚖️ Polar Covalent Bond
Electrons are shared unequally because one atom is more electronegative than the other — it pulls electrons closer. This creates partial charges: δ+ on the less electronegative atom, δ− on the more electronegative. Polar molecules are hydrophilic.
📌 Water (H₂O): oxygen is much more electronegative than hydrogen. Electrons spend more time near oxygen → oxygen gets δ−, hydrogens get δ+. This polarity is responsible for ALL of water's special properties. CO₂ has polar bonds but is linear → the partial charges cancel → nonpolar overall.
🫧 Hydrogen Bond
A weak attractive interaction between a slightly positive hydrogen (δ+) in one polar molecule and a slightly negative atom (usually O or N) in another molecule — or in the same molecule. Individual hydrogen bonds are easily broken, but many together create significant force.
📌 Crucial examples: (1) Water molecules attract each other via H-bonds → gives water cohesion, high heat capacity, surface tension. (2) DNA double helix held together by H-bonds between base pairs. (3) Protein secondary structure (alpha helices, beta sheets) maintained by H-bonds.
🌫️ Van der Waals Interactions
Very weak, temporary attractive forces between any two molecules caused by momentary fluctuations in electron density. They require molecules to be very close together. Individually negligible — but cumulatively important for protein folding and DNA structure.
📌 Van der Waals forces contribute to the three-dimensional structure of proteins — alongside ionic, covalent, and hydrogen bonds. A gecko's ability to stick to walls is partly due to van der Waals forces between its toe hairs and surfaces.

Phase 4 · The Properties of Water

💧 Water — Life's Essential Solvent

Water's polarity and hydrogen bonding give it five critical properties that make life as we know it possible. The AP exam regularly tests all five. Tap each property card to explore.

The human body is 60–70% water. Life originally evolved in a watery environment, and most cellular chemistry occurs inside the watery cytoplasm of cells. Water's unique properties stem entirely from one fact: it is a polar molecule that forms hydrogen bonds.

🧂 Water as Universal Solvent

Because water is polar, it dissolves ionic compounds and other polar molecules by surrounding them with a sphere of hydration. The δ− oxygen attracts cations; the δ+ hydrogens attract anions. Biological reactions occur in aqueous solution — without water's solvent properties, biochemistry is impossible.

📌 When NaCl dissolves in water, Na⁺ is surrounded by the oxygen side of water molecules and Cl⁻ is surrounded by the hydrogen side. | Nonpolar molecules (lipids) do NOT dissolve in water — they are hydrophobic.

🌡️ High Heat Capacity

Water absorbs a large amount of heat before its temperature rises, and releases heat slowly. This is because breaking hydrogen bonds requires energy. Water's specific heat is 1 calorie/gram/°C — about 5× higher than sand. This stabilizes temperatures in organisms and aquatic environments.

📌 Warm-blooded animals use water's high heat capacity to distribute and buffer body heat. Coastal cities have milder temperatures than inland cities because the ocean absorbs and releases heat slowly.

💨 High Heat of Vaporization

It takes a lot of energy (586 cal/g) to convert liquid water to gas, because all hydrogen bonds must break. As water evaporates, it carries heat away — cooling the surface it leaves. This is the basis of evaporative cooling.

📌 Sweating cools your body: water evaporates from skin, removing heat. This is homeostatic temperature regulation in action. Plants use transpiration (water evaporating through leaves) for the same purpose.

🌊 Cohesion and Adhesion

Cohesion = water molecules attracted to each other via hydrogen bonds → creates surface tension. Adhesion = water attracted to other polar surfaces. Together, these properties drive capillary action — water climbing up narrow tubes (like the xylem of a 300-foot redwood tree).

📌 Surface tension allows water striders to walk on water. Capillary action transports water from roots to leaves in plants without requiring a pump. A water column "pulled" up a tree by evaporation at the leaves → cohesion + adhesion make this possible.

🧊 Ice is Less Dense Than Liquid Water

When water freezes, hydrogen bonds lock into a crystalline lattice that is more spread out than liquid water — making ice LESS dense than liquid water. This is unusual; most solids are denser than their liquid form. Ice floats, insulating the water below and allowing aquatic life to survive winter.

📌 If ice sank, lakes and ponds would freeze solid from the bottom up, killing all aquatic life. Ice forming on the surface insulates the water below, keeping it liquid at ~4°C. This property of water is essential for life in cold climates.

Phase 5 · pH, Acids & Bases

🧪 pH, Acids, Bases & Buffers

The pH of a solution measures its concentration of hydrogen ions (H⁺). Life is extremely sensitive to pH — most enzymes only work within a narrow range. Understanding pH and buffers is essential AP content.

Acid Base Buffer Logarithmic Scale Bicarbonate Buffer
Acid — A substance that donates H⁺ ions (protons) to a solution, increasing [H⁺] and lowering pH. Strong acids (like HCl) completely dissociate. Weak acids (like citric acid) partially dissociate. The stomach uses HCl (strong acid) to digest food and kill pathogens.
Base — A substance that accepts H⁺ ions (reducing [H⁺]) or releases OH⁻, thereby raising pH. Strong bases (NaOH) fully dissociate. Baking soda (weak base) is used as an antacid because it neutralizes stomach acid.
Buffer — A solution that resists changes in pH by absorbing excess H⁺ or OH⁻. Buffers contain both a weak acid and its conjugate base. Essential for maintaining homeostasis — most enzymes are pH-specific and denature outside their optimal range.
Logarithmic Scale — pH = −log[H⁺]. Each whole number change in pH represents a 10-fold change in H⁺ concentration. pH 5 has 10× more H⁺ than pH 6, and 100× more than pH 7. This is why small pH changes in blood can be life-threatening.
Bicarbonate Buffer System — The main buffer of human blood. CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻. When blood is too acidic (too much H⁺), bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) absorbs the H⁺, forming H₂CO₃, which breaks down to CO₂ exhaled by the lungs. This maintains blood pH at 7.35–7.45.

Phase 6 · Carbon — The Backbone of Life

🖤 Carbon — Why Life Is Carbon-Based

Carbon has four valence electrons, allowing it to form up to four covalent bonds with other atoms — making it the most versatile element for building biological molecules. All macromolecules are built on carbon skeletons.

With 4 valence electrons, carbon can bond to oxygen, hydrogen, nitrogen, sulfur, phosphorus — and to other carbon atoms. This creates chains, rings, and branched structures of endless variety. No other element can match carbon's molecular diversity.

⛓️ Carbon Skeletons

Carbon atoms bond to each other in chains (straight, branched) or rings. The length and shape of the carbon skeleton largely determines a molecule's properties. Single bonds (C−C), double bonds (C=C), and triple bonds (C≡C) are all possible. More bonds = stronger, shorter, less flexible.

📌 Glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆) has a 6-carbon ring. Fatty acids have long carbon chains. DNA has a backbone of alternating carbon-containing sugars and phosphate groups.

🔄 Isomers

Isomers are molecules with the same molecular formula but different structural arrangements — giving them different properties and functions. Don't confuse isomers with isotopes! Isotopes differ in neutrons; isomers differ in structure.

📌 Glucose and fructose are both C₆H₁₂O₆ (isomers) but have different structures and are metabolized differently. Structural isomers have different connectivity; stereoisomers have the same connectivity but different spatial arrangements.

⛽ Hydrocarbons

Molecules consisting only of carbon and hydrogen. The C−H bonds are nonpolar covalent, making hydrocarbons hydrophobic. They store a lot of energy because the electrons are at a high energy state relative to water. This is why fats (which have long hydrocarbon tails) store more energy per gram than carbohydrates.

📌 Methane (CH₄), propane (C₃H₈), and the fatty acid tails of lipids are all hydrocarbons or hydrocarbon-like. They don't mix with water but do mix with other nonpolar substances.

−OH Hydroxyl Group

Polar and hydrophilic. Makes molecules more soluble in water. Found in alcohols and sugars. The hydroxyl groups on glucose make it water-soluble. Also found on the sugar component of nucleotides.

C=O Carbonyl Group

Polar. Found in aldehydes (carbonyl at end of chain, e.g., glucose) and ketones (carbonyl in middle, e.g., fructose). Distinguishes glucose from fructose despite having the same molecular formula. Important in carbohydrate chemistry.

−COOH Carboxyl Group

Acts as an acid — can donate H⁺, making the molecule acidic. Found in amino acids and fatty acids. The carboxyl end of an amino acid is what forms peptide bonds during protein synthesis. Fatty acids are named for their carboxyl group.

−NH₂ Amino Group

Acts as a base — can accept H⁺. Found in amino acids (hence the name) and nucleotides. Amino acids have BOTH a carboxyl group (acidic) and an amino group (basic). The interaction between these drives peptide bond formation.

−PO₄ Phosphate Group

Negatively charged at physiological pH — acidic. Found in ATP (the energy currency), DNA and RNA backbones, and phospholipids. The high-energy bonds between phosphate groups in ATP store chemical energy used to power cellular work.

−SH Sulfhydryl Group

Found in the amino acid cysteine. Two −SH groups can bond to each other, forming a disulfide bond (−S−S−). Disulfide bonds help stabilize protein tertiary structure. The hair example: heat breaks H-bonds but not disulfide bonds; perms use chemicals to break disulfide bonds and reform them in new positions.
