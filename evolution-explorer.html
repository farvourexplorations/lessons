🌿 Why Do Species Change Over Time?

You are studying for the AP Biology exam — one of the hardest high-school science tests in the country. The Evolution unit alone covers speciation, natural selection, mutations, genetic drift, and the fossil record. Let's start with a big question.

A tiny Galapagos finch lands on an island where only large, hard-shelled nuts grow. Fast-forward 10,000 years. What do you predict has happened to the beak of that finch population? Why?

✏️ Your Prediction (before we dig in)

Here's the kicker: the same process that shaped finch beaks also shaped whale flippers, dog breeds, antibiotic-resistant bacteria, and the human hand. Evolution isn't a distant idea — it's happening right now.

🗝️ The Big Picture

Evolution is the process by which organisms change over time due to inherited changes to DNA. It operates through natural selection, artificial selection, sexual selection, and random processes like genetic drift. Two scientists — Charles Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace — independently developed similar ideas about how evolution works.

Evolution Natural Selection Artificial Selection Sexual Selection Mutation Speciation Homologous Structures Vestigial Structures Adaptation / Fitness Heredity Species Genetic Drift
Evolution — The process by which organisms change over time due to inherited changes to their DNA. This is the central theory of biology. Q126 on your worksheet.
Natural Selection — The process by which traits that help an organism survive and reproduce become more common in a population over generations. It acts on variation that already exists. Q118, 119, 116 on your worksheet.
Artificial Selection — Selection caused by humans choosing which individuals to breed. Responsible for dog breeds, farm animals, and crop vegetables like broccoli. Q110, 112, 122, 136.
Sexual Selection — A special type of natural selection driven by mate choice. Explains elaborate traits like the bright plumage of male birds. Q135.
Mutation — A random change in an organism's genetic sequence (DNA). Mutations are the ultimate source of all new genetic variation. They act randomly — not always helpful, not always harmful. Q113, 120, 130.
Speciation — The process by which one species splits into two or more new species, usually because populations become reproductively isolated (can no longer breed with each other). Creates new species. Q117, 121.
Homologous Structures — Structures in different organisms that developed from the same embryonic tissue but may now perform different functions (e.g. whale fin and human hand). Evidence for common ancestry. Q108, 129.
Vestigial Structures — Structures that are remnants of evolutionary history but no longer serve a function in the organism (e.g. human appendix, whale hip bones). Q124, 125, 128, 131.
Adaptation / Fitness — A trait that increases an organism's ability to survive and reproduce in its environment is called an adaptation. Fitness = reproductive success. Q127, 132.
Heredity — The transmission of genetic characteristics from parent to offspring. Natural selection only works because traits are heritable. Q134.
Species — A group of organisms that can interbreed and produce fertile offspring. Speciation creates new species when populations can no longer interbreed. Q117, 121.
Genetic Drift — Random changes in allele frequency in a population, due to chance rather than selection. Most important in small populations. The change in gene frequencies in a population over time is evolution. Q133.

🪨 Fossil Record

The fossil record shows organisms preserved in rock layers over millions of years. Older layers contain simpler, more primitive organisms; newer layers show more complex, modern species. Comparing structures in the fossil record provides evidence for evolution (Q111).

📌 Worksheet Q111: "Comparing organisms' structures in the fossil record provides evidence for ___." → evolution

🤝 Homologous Structures

Homologous structures develop from the same embryonic tissue but function differently in different species. This shows that different species descended from a common ancestor whose limb was then modified by evolution over millions of years (Q129).

📌 Q108: A whale fin and a human hand are examples of homologous structures.
📌 Q129: "What are structures that develop from the same embryonic tissue into different functioning structures?" → Homologous structures

🦴 Vestigial Structures

Vestigial structures are evolutionary remnants — structures that had a function in an ancestor but are no longer useful. They are "biological fossils" inside living bodies, proving that species have changed over time (Q131).

📌 Q124: The human appendix is an example of a vestigial structure.
📌 Q128: Whale hip bones are an example of a vestigial structure.

🥚 Similarities in Embryology

The embryos of very different vertebrates (fish, turtles, humans) look strikingly similar in early development — all have gill slits and tails. This suggests all vertebrates share a distant common ancestor (Q125).

📌 Q125: The inside of a turtle egg looks like a human embryo because turtles and humans share similarities in embryology.

🌿 Natural Selection

The environment acts as the "selector." Individuals with traits better suited to their environment survive and reproduce more. Over generations, the population becomes better adapted.

📌 Worksheet examples: Faster mice that outrun predator cats (Q116). Antibiotic-resistant bacteria from excessive antibiotic use (Q115). Birds with thicker beaks survive on hard-shelled nuts (Q118).

👨‍🌾 Artificial Selection

Humans choose which individuals to breed, selecting for traits humans desire. Also called selective breeding. This is what caused the incredible diversity of dog breeds and farm animals.

📌 Worksheet examples: Different dog breeds (Q122). Cows and chickens (Q112). Broccoli and other vegetables (Q110). Human-caused = artificial (Q136).

🦚 Sexual Selection

A form of natural selection where the "selector" is the opposite sex choosing mates. Leads to traits like bright colors, elaborate songs, or large antlers — even if those traits reduce survival (but increase mating success).

📌 Worksheet Q135: "What type of selection caused male birds to be bright colors?" → Sexual selection

🏭 Industrial Melanism (a type of Natural Selection)

During the Industrial Revolution, coal pollution darkened tree bark. Light peppered moths became highly visible to predators and were eaten, while darker moths survived and reproduced. The population shifted to become predominantly dark — natural selection in action.

📌 Worksheet Q114: Peppered moths became darker because of natural selection.
