Phase 1 · Wonder

🌿 What Is the Smallest Unit of Life?

Every living thing you have ever seen is made of cells. But what exactly is a cell, and why is it considered the fundamental unit of life?

A single Paramecium — barely visible to the naked eye — hunts for food, digests it, moves using cilia, responds to stimuli, excretes waste, and reproduces. It does everything a living thing must do, using just one cell. How is that possible?

Phase 2 · A2.2.1 — Cell Theory

🧪 Cell Theory SL

Cell theory is one of biology's great unifying principles. The IB exam tests all three statements, supporting evidence, and — critically — the limitations of cell theory with named examples.

The Three Statements of Cell Theory:
1. All living organisms are composed of one or more cells.
2. The cell is the basic structural and functional unit of life.
3. All cells come from pre-existing cells (Virchow, 1855 — Omnis cellula e cellula).

Robert Hooke (1665)

Used a compound light microscope to examine cork. Saw tiny box-like compartments and named them cells. He was seeing empty dead plant cell walls — not living cells. First documented use of "cell" in biology.

IB link: Hooke laid the foundation for cell theory but lacked the resolution to see living cell contents or organelles.

Antonie van Leeuwenhoek (1670s)

Built high-quality single-lens microscopes and was first to observe living microorganisms — bacteria and protozoa from pond water. He called them "animalcules." Proved the microscopic world teems with life.

IB link: Leeuwenhoek showed a single cell could be a complete living organism — a key step toward cell theory.

Schleiden & Schwann (1838–1839)

Schleiden (botanist) proposed all plants are made of cells. Schwann extended this to animals. Together they formulated the first two statements of cell theory: all life is made of cells; the cell is the basic unit of life.

IB link: Their cross-disciplinary collaboration is a Nature of Science example — sharing observations between fields led to a unifying biological principle.

Rudolf Virchow (1855)

Added the third and most important statement: "Omnis cellula e cellula" — all cells arise from pre-existing cells. Disproved spontaneous generation of cells. Every cell today traces an unbroken chain back ~3.8 billion years.

IB link: Virchow's principle connects directly to cancer biology (uncontrolled cell division) and to the concept of common ancestry in evolution.

⚠️ Limitations of Cell Theory — Tap Each

IB examiners regularly ask students to evaluate cell theory. Know these exceptions by name and be able to explain the challenge each poses.

Syncytia Fungal Hyphae Viruses Giant Algae Myelin Sheath
Syncytia — Multinucleate Structures: Skeletal muscle fibres form when many myoblast cells fuse, producing a long structure with multiple nuclei and no internal cell boundaries. This challenges the idea that all organisms are composed of discrete, bounded cells. Osteoclasts (bone-dissolving cells) are also syncytia.
Coenocytic Fungal Hyphae: Many fungi grow as hyphae — long filaments without cross-walls (septa). This creates a continuous tube of cytoplasm with many nuclei and no distinct cell boundaries. It is difficult to apply the concept of individual cells to coenocytic fungi.
Viruses — Acellular Life: Viruses are not made of cells — they are just nucleic acid in a protein coat. They can reproduce and evolve, but only inside a host cell. Cell theory states all life is cellular, but viruses blur the boundary between living and non-living — challenging the theory's universality.
Giant Unicellular Algae (Caulerpa): Caulerpa is technically a single cell that can grow to over one metre in length, with no internal cell division. A single "cell" that is metres long and has complex structure challenges the idea that cells are small, discrete units.
Myelinated Nerve Fibres: The myelin sheath is formed by Schwann cells that wrap around the axon and lose most of their cytoplasm. The resulting structure is largely just membrane layers with minimal cytoplasm — blurring the boundary between a cell and a non-cellular structure.

Phase 3 · A2.2.2 & A2.2.3 — Microscopy

🔬 Microscopy Skills SL

The IB requires you to calculate magnification and actual size, understand resolution, and know the differences between light and electron microscopes. These skills appear in every paper.

🔬 Microscope Magnification Simulator

Click a zoom level to see what becomes visible
🦠

Select Magnification

At x100 (light microscope, low power): Large cells and tissues visible. Nucleus appears as darker region. Resolution ~200 nm. Coloured stains needed for contrast. Living specimens possible.
Magnification = Image size ÷ Actual size
M = I ÷ A  |  Rearrange: A = I ÷ M  |  Always use same units  |  1 mm = 1000 µm = 1,000,000 nm

🧮 Practice Calculations

Calculation 1 — Find Magnification
A student draws a cell 40 mm wide. The actual cell is 50 µm wide. Calculate the magnification. Show your working.
Working:
Image = 40 mm = 40,000 µm
Actual = 50 µm
M = 40,000 ÷ 50 = x800
Always convert both measurements to the same unit before dividing.
Calculation 2 — Find Actual Size
An electron micrograph shows a mitochondrion 60 mm long. Magnification is x30,000. Calculate the actual length in µm.
Working:
A = I ÷ M = 60 mm ÷ 30,000 = 0.002 mm
Convert: 0.002 mm × 1000 = 2 µm
Realistic — mitochondria are typically 1–10 µm long.
Calculation 3 — Scale Bar
A micrograph has a scale bar 20 mm long on the printed image representing 2 µm. A nucleus measures 45 mm across. Calculate: (1) the magnification, (2) the actual diameter of the nucleus in µm.
(1) M = 20 mm ÷ 0.002 mm = x10,000
(2) A = 45 mm ÷ 10,000 = 0.0045 mm = 4.5 µm
Nuclei are typically 5–10 µm — this result is realistic.

⚡ Light vs Electron Microscope

IlluminationVisible lightBeam of electrons
Max resolution~200 nm~0.1 nm (TEM)
Max magnification~x1,500x500,000+

Resolution vs Magnification: Resolution is the ability to distinguish two adjacent points as separate. Magnification just makes things appear larger. A blurry photo can be enlarged, but resolution stays the same. Electron microscopes improved resolution — revealing ultrastructure light microscopes cannot show regardless of magnification.

Phase 4 · A2.2.4 & A2.2.5 — Prokaryotic Cells

🦠 Features Common to All Cells & Prokaryotic Structure SL

All cells share four universal features. Prokaryotic cells are defined by the absence of a membrane-bound nucleus and membrane-bound organelles — but they have been the dominant life form on Earth for 3.5 billion years.

🔗 Features Common to ALL Cells (A2.2.4) — Tap Each

Plasma Membrane Cytoplasm Ribosomes DNA
Plasma Membrane: A phospholipid bilayer with embedded proteins (fluid mosaic model). Controls what enters and leaves the cell. Maintains the cell as a distinct unit. Present in ALL cells — prokaryotic and eukaryotic.
Cytoplasm / Cytosol: The aqueous contents inside the plasma membrane (excluding the nucleus in eukaryotes). Site of many metabolic reactions. Contains dissolved substances, enzymes, and ribosomes. Present in ALL cells.
Ribosomes: Non-membrane-bound organelles that synthesize proteins by translating mRNA. Present in ALL living cells. Prokaryotic: 70S. Eukaryotic cytoplasmic: 80S. This difference is medically important — antibiotics like streptomycin target 70S ribosomes without harming human 80S ribosomes.
DNA: All cells contain DNA as their genetic material. In prokaryotes: single circular molecule in the nucleoid region (not membrane-bound). In eukaryotes: multiple linear chromosomes in the nucleus. DNA stores the information needed to build and maintain the cell.

Nucleoid Region

Region of prokaryotic cytoplasm where the single circular DNA molecule is located. Not membrane-bound — no nuclear envelope. DNA is in direct contact with cytoplasm, allowing simultaneous transcription and translation (coupled transcription-translation).

IB: "Why can prokaryotes undergo coupled transcription-translation?" Because there is no nuclear membrane separating DNA from ribosomes.

Cell Wall (Peptidoglycan)

Prokaryotic cell walls are made of peptidoglycan — a polymer of sugars and amino acids. Gives the cell rigidity and maintains shape. Antibiotics like penicillin target peptidoglycan synthesis. Gram-positive = thick layer. Gram-negative = thin layer + outer membrane.

IB: Prokaryote wall = peptidoglycan. Plant wall = cellulose. Fungal wall = chitin. Animal cells = no wall.

Pili (singular: pilus)

Short protein filaments on the cell surface. Functions: (1) attachment to surfaces and host cells (important for pathogenicity), (2) conjugation (sex pili) — transfer of plasmids between bacteria. Made of pilin protein.

E. coli uses pili to attach to the urinary tract lining. Gonorrhoea bacteria use pili to colonise host cells.

Flagellum (plural: flagella)

Long rotating tail that propels the cell. Made of flagellin protein. Rotates like a propeller driven by a proton gradient. Completely different from eukaryotic flagella, which use microtubules (9+2) and dynein motor proteins.

IB distinction: Prokaryotic flagella = solid protein rod, rotary motor. Eukaryotic flagella = hollow microtubule-based (9+2 arrangement), bending/whipping motion.

Plasmids

Small circular self-replicating DNA molecules separate from the main chromosome. Often carry genes for antibiotic resistance, virulence, or special metabolism. Can be transferred between bacteria by conjugation — key mechanism for the spread of antibiotic resistance.

IB application: Plasmids are used as vectors in genetic engineering (e.g. for insulin production in bacteria). The Ti plasmid from Agrobacterium is used for plant transformation.

Capsule (Slime Layer)

Thick gel-like polysaccharide layer surrounding the cell wall in some bacteria. Functions: (1) protection from phagocytosis by white blood cells, (2) resistance to desiccation, (3) attachment to surfaces. Encapsulated bacteria are typically more virulent.

Classic example: Streptococcus pneumoniae with capsule = virulent; without = non-virulent. This was key to Griffith's transformation experiment (1928).

Phase 5 · A2.2.6 — Eukaryotic Cells

🧬 Eukaryotic Cell Structure SL

Eukaryotic cells have a membrane-bound nucleus and membrane-bound organelles. The IB requires you to know the structure AND function of every major organelle and to link structure to function.

The key concept in eukaryote biology is compartmentalization — membrane-bound organelles divide the cell into functional zones with distinct internal chemistry. This allows incompatible reactions to occur simultaneously in the same cell.

Nucleus

Largest organelle. Bounded by a double membrane (nuclear envelope) with nuclear pores. Contains linear DNA wrapped around histones. Contains the nucleolus where rRNA is synthesized and ribosomal subunits assembled. Directs all cell activities through gene expression.

IB structure-function: Nuclear pores allow selective export of mRNA to cytoplasmic ribosomes and import of transcription factors. Double membrane protects DNA from cytoplasmic reactions.

Mitochondria

Double membrane: smooth outer, highly folded inner (cristae). Cristae increase surface area for the electron transport chain and ATP synthase. Interior matrix is the site of the Krebs cycle. Contains own circular DNA and 70S ribosomes (endosymbiosis evidence). Site of aerobic respiration.

IB: Cells with high energy demand (muscle, sperm midpiece, liver) have more mitochondria. "How does cristae structure relate to function?" More surface area = more ATP synthase = more ATP produced.

Rough Endoplasmic Reticulum

Membrane-bound sacs and tubules studded with ribosomes. Ribosomes synthesize proteins destined for secretion, membranes, or lysosomes. Proteins enter ER lumen for folding and initial modification (glycosylation). Continuous with the outer nuclear membrane.

IB: Cells secreting many proteins (pancreatic acinar cells, goblet cells, plasma cells) have abundant rough ER. Proteins follow the route: rough ER -> transport vesicle -> Golgi -> secretory vesicle -> exocytosis.

Smooth Endoplasmic Reticulum

ER without ribosomes. Functions: (1) lipid synthesis (phospholipids, steroids), (2) drug detoxification in liver, (3) calcium ion storage in muscle cells (Ca release triggers contraction).

IB: Liver hepatocytes are rich in smooth ER for detoxification. Steroid-secreting cells (adrenal cortex, Leydig cells in testes) have abundant smooth ER for steroid hormone synthesis.

Golgi Apparatus

Stack of flattened membrane sacs (cisternae). Receives vesicles from rough ER (cis face), modifies contents (adds/removes sugars, cleaves signal peptides), packages into vesicles, sends to destinations (trans face): cell membrane for secretion, or lysosomes. The cell's postal sorting office.

IB: "Describe the role of the Golgi." Receives proteins from rough ER -> modifies and sorts them -> packages into vesicles -> sends to cell membrane (exocytosis) or lysosomes. Produces glycoproteins and glycolipids.

Lysosomes

Membrane-bound vesicles containing hydrolytic enzymes. pH ~4.5 inside. Functions: (1) intracellular digestion of phagocytosed material, (2) autophagy (digesting old organelles), (3) apoptosis (programmed cell death). Acidic pH activates enzymes and protects cell if lysosomes leak.

IB: White blood cells use lysosomes to destroy engulfed pathogens. Lysosomal storage diseases (Tay-Sachs disease) result from missing lysosomal enzymes. Lysosomes fuse with phagosomes to form secondary lysosomes.

Chloroplasts (plant cells only)

Double membrane. Inside: thylakoids stacked into grana (light-dependent reactions — chlorophyll here). Surrounding: stroma (Calvin cycle — carbon fixation). Contains own circular DNA and 70S ribosomes (endosymbiosis evidence).

IB: "How does chloroplast structure relate to photosynthesis?" Thylakoid membranes hold chlorophyll and electron transport proteins (large surface area for light absorption). Stroma contains RuBisCO and Calvin cycle enzymes.

Vacuole

Plant cells: large central vacuole (up to 90% of volume) bounded by the tonoplast. Maintains turgor pressure — essential for plant support. Contains cell sap (water, salts, pigments, waste). Animal cells: small temporary vacuoles only, no permanent central vacuole.

IB: A turgid plant cell has its vacuole pushing the cytoplasm against the rigid cell wall, generating pressure that supports non-woody plants. Water loss -> vacuole shrinks -> cell becomes flaccid -> plant wilts.

Centrioles (animal cells only)

Pairs of cylindrical structures made of microtubule triplets (9 sets of 3). Located near the nucleus. During cell division, centrioles organise the mitotic spindle. Absent from plant cells, which still divide using a spindle formed by other mechanisms.

IB: Centrioles are absent from plant cells and most fungi. Despite this, plant cells divide normally — showing centrioles are not universally essential for spindle formation.

Phase 7 · IB-Style Exam Questions

📝 IB-Style Exam Questions

Written in the style of IB DP Biology Paper 1 and Paper 2. Write your answers then reveal the mark-scheme level responses.

Cell Theory · 3 marks
State the three principles of cell theory. [3]
Mark scheme (1 mark each):
  • All living organisms are composed of one or more cells [1]
  • The cell is the basic structural and functional unit of all living organisms [1]
  • All cells come from pre-existing cells [1]
Cell Theory Limitations · 4 marks
Explain two limitations of cell theory with reference to specific named examples. [4]
Mark scheme (2 marks per limitation):
  • Skeletal muscle fibres are syncytia — formed by fusion of many cells with multiple nuclei and no internal cell boundaries, challenging the idea that organisms are composed of discrete cells. [2]
  • Coenocytic fungal hyphae lack septa, forming tubes of cytoplasm with many nuclei and no distinct cell boundaries. [2]
  • Viruses are not composed of cells yet can reproduce and evolve, challenging the universality of cell theory. [2]
  • Caulerpa is a giant unicellular alga that can grow metres long, challenging the idea of cells as small discrete units. [2]
Microscopy Calculation · 3 marks
A diagram of a cell has magnification x15,000. The nucleus appears 45 mm in diameter. Calculate the actual size of the nucleus in µm. Show your working. [3]
Mark scheme:
A = I / M [1]
A = 45 mm / 15,000 = 0.003 mm [1]
0.003 mm x 1000 = 3 µm [1]
Prokaryotes vs Eukaryotes · 4 marks
Compare the structure of prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells. [4]
Mark scheme (any 4 true comparisons):
  • Prokaryotes have no membrane-bound nucleus; eukaryotes have a nucleus with a double membrane (nuclear envelope). [1]
  • Prokaryotes have 70S ribosomes; eukaryotes have 80S ribosomes in the cytoplasm. [1]
  • Prokaryotes lack membrane-bound organelles; eukaryotes have mitochondria, ER, Golgi, etc. [1]
  • Prokaryotic DNA is a single circular molecule not associated with histones; eukaryotic DNA is linear and wrapped around histones. [1]
  • Both have plasma membranes, ribosomes, cytoplasm, and DNA. [1]
Organelle Structure-Function · 3 marks
Explain how the structure of the mitochondrion is related to its function in ATP synthesis. [3]
Mark scheme (any 3):
  • Inner membrane is folded into cristae, increasing surface area for the electron transport chain and ATP synthase. [1]
  • The matrix is the site of the Krebs cycle and contains enzymes for pyruvate oxidation. [1]
  • The double membrane creates the intermembrane space where H+ accumulates to generate the proton gradient that drives ATP synthase. [1]
  • Mitochondria contain their own 70S ribosomes and circular DNA, allowing synthesis of some respiratory proteins. [1]
Resolution vs Magnification · 2 marks
Distinguish between resolution and magnification in the context of microscopy. [2]
Mark scheme:
  • Resolution is the ability to distinguish two adjacent points as separate / the minimum distance between two points that can be seen as distinct; magnification is how many times larger the image appears compared to the actual object. [1]
  • Electron microscopes have much greater resolution (~0.1 nm) than light microscopes (~200 nm), allowing ultrastructure to be seen that cannot be revealed by increased magnification alone. [1]
