Phase 1 · Wonder

🧈 The Molecules That Hate Water

Pour oil into water and it never mixes — it just sits on top. That same chemical "stubbornness" is exactly what makes lipids so essential to life: they form the barrier around every cell on Earth.

Your cell membrane is made of phospholipids — molecules with a water-loving head and a water-hating tail. If lipids are hydrophobic and water is everywhere inside and outside your cells, how does a membrane made of lipids actually hold together and function in a watery environment?

✏️ Your Prediction (before we dig in)

The answer is elegant: phospholipids are amphipathic — part hydrophilic, part hydrophobic. In water, they spontaneously arrange into a bilayer, with all the water-hating tails tucked away from water on the inside, and all the water-loving heads facing outward into the watery environment. No energy input required — it just happens. This chapter also covers proteins, the macromolecule responsible for nearly every job a cell needs done — from digesting your lunch to contracting your muscles.

🗝️ The Big Picture

Lipids (3.3) are a diverse, nonpolar group including fats/oils, waxes, phospholipids, and steroids — built from glycerol and fatty acids (or, for steroids, a fused 4-ring structure). Proteins (3.4) are the most functionally diverse macromolecule, built from 20 different amino acids and folded into four levels of structure that determine everything about how a protein works.

Phase 2 · 3.3 Lipids

📖 Lipids — Core Vocabulary

Tap each term to reveal its definition. Lipids are grouped together not because they're structurally similar, but because they all share one key property: hydrophobic.

4 Major Types Triglycerides Saturated vs. Unsaturated Trans Fats Omega-3 Fatty Acids Waxes Phospholipids Amphipathic & Micelles Steroids & Cholesterol
Four Major Types of Lipids — Fats & Oils (triglycerides — energy storage), Waxes (protective coatings), Phospholipids (membrane structure), and Steroids (signaling, membrane fluidity). All four are grouped together because they are nonpolar and hydrophobic, even though they have very different structures and functions.
Triglycerides (Triacylglycerols) — A fat molecule made of one glycerol backbone (3 carbons, each with an OH group) bonded to three fatty acid chains via dehydration synthesis (ester bonds), releasing three water molecules. Fatty acids range from 4–36 carbons (most commonly 12–18). Long straight saturated chains pack tightly and are solid at room temperature; bent unsaturated chains can't pack as tightly and are liquid (oils).
Saturated vs. Unsaturated Fatty Acids — A saturated fatty acid has only single bonds between carbons — it's "saturated" with the maximum number of hydrogens (e.g., stearic acid). An unsaturated fatty acid has at least one C=C double bond, which creates a "kink" or bend in the chain (e.g., oleic acid). One double bond = monounsaturated (olive oil); more than one = polyunsaturated (canola oil). Saturated fats tend to be solid at room temp; unsaturated fats/oils tend to be liquid.
Trans Fats — Made by artificially hydrogenating oils in food processing, converting some cis double bonds into trans double bonds. In a cis configuration, hydrogens are on the same side of the double bond (causing a kink); in trans, they're on opposite sides (chain stays straighter, behaves more like saturated fat). Trans fats raise LDL ("bad") cholesterol and contribute to heart disease — many restaurants have banned them.
Omega-3 Fatty Acids — Essential fatty acids (the body cannot synthesize them — must come from diet) that are polyunsaturated, with the first double bond at the 3rd carbon from the omega (tail) end. Examples: ALA, EPA, DHA — found in salmon, trout, tuna. Benefits: reduce risk of sudden cardiac death, lower triglycerides and blood pressure, inhibit blood clotting, reduce inflammation.
Waxes — Long fatty acid chains esterified to long-chain alcohols. Their hydrophobic nature makes them excellent waterproof coatings — found on bird feathers (waterproofing) and plant leaf surfaces (preventing water loss/sticking).
Phospholipids — The main structural component of all cell membranes. Like triglycerides, they have a glycerol backbone, but with only 2 fatty acid chains (forming a diacylglycerol) — the 3rd carbon position is occupied by a modified phosphate group instead of a third fatty acid. Common examples: phosphatidylcholine, phosphatidylserine.
Amphipathic Molecules & Micelles — "Amphipathic" means having both a hydrophilic part and a hydrophobic part. Phospholipids are amphipathic: the phosphate "head" is hydrophilic, the fatty acid "tails" are hydrophobic. In water, phospholipids spontaneously form a bilayer (heads facing outward toward water, tails facing inward away from water) — or, if dropped as droplets, a spherical micelle with tails clustered in the protected interior.
Steroids & Cholesterol — Structurally very different from triglycerides/phospholipids (four fused carbon rings instead of a glycerol + fatty acid structure), but still classified as lipids because they're hydrophobic. Cholesterol is the most common steroid — synthesized mainly in the liver, it's a precursor to steroid hormones (e.g., testosterone, estradiol), Vitamin D, and bile salts. It's also embedded in animal plasma membranes, where it helps maintain membrane fluidity.

✅ Reflect — Fatty Acid Structure & Health

Cell membrane "fluidity" depends partly on the ratio of saturated to unsaturated fatty acid tails in its phospholipids. Straight saturated tails pack tightly together (less fluid); kinked unsaturated tails create gaps that keep the membrane flexible (more fluid) — this concept will become critical when you study cell membranes in Unit 2!

🛠️ 7 Types & Functions of Proteins

Proteins serve more functions than any other macromolecule: Digestive Enzymes (amylase, lipase, pepsin — break down food), Transport (hemoglobin, albumin — carry substances in blood), Structural (actin, tubulin, keratin — build the cytoskeleton), Hormones (insulin, thyroxine — coordinate body systems), Defense (immunoglobulins/antibodies — fight pathogens), Contractile (actin, myosin — muscle contraction), and Storage (egg white albumin, legume storage proteins — nourish developing embryos).

📌 Enzymes are catalysts: catabolic enzymes break down substrates, anabolic enzymes build complex molecules, and ALL enzymes increase reaction rate (are "catalytic").

⚛️ Amino Acid Structure

Every amino acid shares the same core structure: a central alpha (α) carbon bonded to (1) an amino group (–NH₂), (2) a carboxyl group (–COOH), (3) a hydrogen atom, and (4) a variable R group (side chain) that gives each of the 20 amino acids its unique chemical identity (nonpolar, polar, positively charged, or negatively charged).

📌 Nonpolar/hydrophobic: valine, methionine, alanine. Polar/hydrophilic: serine, threonine, cysteine. Positively charged (basic): lysine, arginine. 9 amino acids are "essential" — the human body cannot synthesize them and must obtain them from diet.

🔗 The Peptide Bond

Amino acids link together via a peptide bond, formed by dehydration synthesis: the carboxyl group of one amino acid combines with the amino group of the next, releasing one water molecule. A chain of amino acids is called a polypeptide. Each polypeptide has a free amino group at one end (the N-terminus) and a free carboxyl group at the other (the C-terminus).

📌 "Polypeptide" and "protein" aren't quite synonyms: a polypeptide is just the amino acid polymer, while "protein" implies the final folded, functional molecule (sometimes multiple polypeptides combined, often with post-translational modifications).

🍳 Denaturation

When a protein is exposed to extreme temperature, pH change, or certain chemicals, it can lose its 3D shape — this is called denaturation. The primary sequence (order of amino acids) usually stays intact, but the protein loses its function because shape determines function. Denaturation is sometimes reversible (if the denaturing agent is removed), but often it's irreversible — like a fried egg, where the albumin protein in egg white permanently unfolds and can never refold.

📌 Not all proteins denature easily — bacteria in hot springs have proteins that function near boiling temperatures. Your stomach's digestive enzymes function fine in highly acidic conditions (low pH) that would denature most other proteins.

📝 Primary Structure

The unique, linear sequence of amino acids in a polypeptide chain — determined directly by the gene encoding the protein. Even a single amino acid substitution can drastically change a protein's structure and function. Example: in sickle cell anemia, a single substitution (glutamic acid → valine) in the hemoglobin beta chain — out of 600 total amino acids — causes red blood cells to sickle and clog blood vessels.

🌀 Secondary Structure

Local folding patterns held together by hydrogen bonds between the carbonyl and amino groups in the polypeptide's backbone. Two common forms: the α-helix (a coiled spring shape; H-bonds form every 4th amino acid) and the β-pleated sheet (a folded, ribbon-like zigzag; H-bonds form between adjacent strands). Found in most globular and fibrous proteins.

🧶 Tertiary Structure

The overall 3D shape of a single polypeptide, driven by interactions between R groups: hydrophobic interactions (nonpolar R groups cluster in the protein's interior, away from water), ionic bonds (oppositely charged R groups attract), hydrogen bonds (between polar R groups), and disulfide bonds (the only covalent bond in folding — forms between two cysteine side chains in the presence of oxygen). Most enzymes function at the tertiary level.

🧩 Quaternary Structure

Formed when multiple polypeptide subunits combine and interact to form one functional protein. Not every protein has quaternary structure — only those built from more than one polypeptide chain. Example: hemoglobin is made of 4 subunits (2 alpha chains + 2 beta chains) held together by weak interactions including hydrogen bonds and disulfide bonds.

🔍 Why this matters: Tertiary Structure

When a protein folds in a watery (aqueous) environment, nonpolar R groups cluster in the interior (hiding from water — a hydrophobic interaction), while polar and charged R groups face the exterior (interacting with water). This single principle explains a huge amount about how soluble proteins like enzymes achieve their functional 3D shape!
