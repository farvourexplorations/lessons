Phase 1 · Wonder

🚪 The World's Most Selective Doorman

Every second, your cells let some molecules in and keep others out — without a brain, a security guard, or a single conscious decision. The plasma membrane does it all, automatically, based purely on chemistry.

Oxygen diffuses freely into your cells. Glucose cannot get in without help. Sodium ions are actively pumped out against their concentration gradient. Large proteins can't cross at all without being engulfed. How can one thin, 5–10 nm membrane "decide" what crosses and what doesn't — without using any intelligence?

✏️ Your Prediction (before we dig in)

The answer is selective permeability — a direct result of the membrane's molecular structure. The hydrophobic core of the phospholipid bilayer acts as a barrier to charged and polar molecules, while letting nonpolar molecules pass freely. Transport proteins provide carefully controlled passageways for everything else. The plasma membrane is not a wall — it's a highly dynamic, fluid mosaic of molecules, each performing a specific role.

Phase 2 · 5.1 Components & Structure

📖 The Fluid Mosaic Model — Core Vocabulary

Tap each term to reveal its definition. Singer and Nicolson proposed the fluid mosaic model in 1972 — it remains the best explanation of membrane structure. Know every component.

Fluid Mosaic Model Phospholipid Bilayer Integral Proteins Peripheral Proteins Glycoproteins & Glycolipids Glycocalyx Cholesterol Membrane Fluidity Selective Permeability
Fluid Mosaic Model (Singer & Nicolson, 1972) — Describes the plasma membrane as a fluid (the phospholipid bilayer is not rigid — proteins and lipids drift laterally) mosaic (made of multiple different components: phospholipids, proteins, cholesterol, and carbohydrates). Membranes range from 5–10 nm thick. The earlier "sandwich model" (Davson & Danielli, 1935) was replaced because TEM revealed a double, not single, lipid layer.
Phospholipid Bilayer — The main structural fabric of the membrane. Each phospholipid has a hydrophilic head (glycerol + phosphate group — polar, faces water) and two hydrophobic tails (fatty acid chains — nonpolar, face inward). In water, phospholipids spontaneously arrange into a bilayer with tails hidden inside. They can also form micelles (spheres with tails inside) and liposomes (hollow spheres with a bilayer).
Integral Proteins — Proteins embedded within the phospholipid bilayer. Often span the entire membrane (transmembrane proteins). Have hydrophobic membrane-spanning regions (often α-helices of 20–25 amino acids) that interact with the lipid tails. Also called integrins. Functions: transport molecules across the membrane (channels and carriers), receive signals (receptors), catalyse reactions (enzymes), and mediate cell adhesion.
Peripheral Proteins — Found on the inner or outer surface of the membrane, attached to integral proteins or phospholipid heads — NOT embedded in the bilayer. Functions: serve as enzymes, provide structural attachments for the cytoskeleton (inner surface), or act as cell recognition sites (outer surface). Also called "cell-specific proteins" — they help the immune system identify self vs. non-self.
Glycoproteins & Glycolipids — Carbohydrate chains (2–60 monosaccharides, straight or branched) attached to proteins (glycoproteins) or lipids (glycolipids) on the exterior surface only. Function: cell identity and recognition (like a barcode unique to each cell type), allow hormone/receptor binding, and determine blood type. HIV recognises glycoproteins (CD4) on T-helper cells to infect them.
Glycocalyx ("Sugar Coating") — The collective name for all carbohydrate components (glycoproteins + glycolipids) on the exterior membrane surface. Highly hydrophilic — attracts water to the cell surface. Critical for: cell-cell recognition, immune self/non-self distinction, embryonic development, and cell-cell adhesion in tissue formation. Viruses like HIV and hepatitis exploit the glycocalyx to identify and infect specific target cells.
Cholesterol — A lipid embedded between phospholipids in the bilayer of animal cell membranes. Acts as a fluidity buffer: at low temperatures it prevents phospholipids from packing too tightly (prevents freezing); at high temperatures it prevents them from becoming too fluid. Also organises clusters of transmembrane proteins into lipid rafts. Absent from most prokaryotic membranes.
Membrane Fluidity — Three factors control fluidity: (1) Unsaturated fatty acids — double bonds create kinks that prevent tight packing → more fluid; (2) Temperature — higher = more fluid, lower = less fluid; (3) Cholesterol — buffers both extremes. Fish in cold environments increase their proportion of unsaturated fatty acids to maintain membrane fluidity. The membrane is not elastic (can burst if overstretched) but a fine needle can penetrate and self-seal.
Selective Permeability — The membrane allows some substances to pass freely, requires transport proteins for others, and blocks others entirely. Rules: (1) Small, nonpolar molecules (O₂, CO₂, N₂, lipid-soluble vitamins A/D/E/K, steroid hormones) diffuse freely; (2) Water crosses via aquaporins; (3) Polar molecules and ions need transport proteins; (4) Large molecules (proteins, carbohydrates) cannot cross without bulk transport (endocytosis/exocytosis).

🥓 Fatty Acid Saturation — Straight vs. Kinked Tails

Saturated fatty acids: all single C–C bonds → straight, unbranched tails → pack tightly → more rigid membrane. Unsaturated fatty acids: one or more C=C double bonds → kinks in the tail (~30° bend per double bond) → tails can't pack tightly → more fluid membrane. Cold-climate fish have membranes rich in unsaturated fatty acids — this prevents their membranes from freezing and keeps them functional at low temperatures.

📌 AP exam connection: "A cold-water organism would be expected to have a higher proportion of unsaturated fatty acids in its membrane phospholipids."

🌡️ Temperature — Faster or Slower Movement

Higher temperatures give phospholipid molecules more kinetic energy, making them move more rapidly → membrane becomes more fluid. Lower temperatures reduce kinetic energy → molecules slow and pack more closely → membrane becomes less fluid (more rigid). If a membrane's fatty acid composition doesn't adapt to cold, it can solidify like butter in a refrigerator — disrupting all membrane-dependent functions.

📌 Cholesterol moderates both extremes: at low temps it prevents solidifying; at high temps it prevents becoming too fluid.

🫧 Cholesterol — The Fluidity Buffer

Cholesterol molecules nestle between phospholipids in the bilayer. At low temperatures: cholesterol disrupts the tight packing of saturated fatty acid tails, preventing the membrane from solidifying → maintains fluidity. At high temperatures: cholesterol helps restrain phospholipid movement, preventing the membrane from becoming excessively fluid and losing integrity → limits fluidity increase. This dual role extends the functional temperature range of animal cell membranes. Cholesterol is also involved in forming lipid rafts — microdomains that cluster specific proteins.

📌 Only animal cells contain cholesterol in their membranes. Bacteria and plant cells use other mechanisms to regulate membrane fluidity.

💨 Simple Diffusion

Movement of a substance from an area of high concentration to low concentration across the membrane — no protein needed, no energy required. Works for: small, nonpolar molecules (O₂, CO₂, N₂) and lipid-soluble substances (fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E, K; steroid hormones; lipid-soluble drugs). Continues until dynamic equilibrium is reached (equal concentration on both sides — net movement = 0, but molecules continue to cross randomly). Concentration gradient = a form of potential energy that drives diffusion.

📌 O₂ diffuses into cells; CO₂ diffuses out — both by simple diffusion. Neither requires a transport protein. Ions (Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻) and polar molecules (glucose, amino acids) CANNOT cross by simple diffusion.

🚪 Facilitated Diffusion — Channel Proteins & Carrier Proteins

Polar molecules, charged ions, and large molecules that cannot cross the hydrophobic bilayer use transport proteins — still moving down their concentration gradient (no ATP). Two types: (1) Channel proteins — provide a hydrophilic pore through the membrane; specific for one substance; can be gated (open/close in response to signals) or always open. Aquaporins are channel proteins specific for water. Channels transport up to tens of millions of molecules/sec. (2) Carrier proteins — bind a specific substance, change shape, release it on the other side. More specific but slower (thousands–millions/sec). Can become saturated when all proteins are occupied.

📌 Glucose cannot cross by simple diffusion — it uses GLUT carrier proteins. Na⁺ and K⁺ use gated channel proteins in nerve and muscle cells. Water uses aquaporins in red blood cells and kidney tubules.

💧 Osmosis — Water's Special Case of Diffusion

Osmosis is the diffusion of water only through a selectively permeable membrane — from high water concentration (low solute) to low water concentration (high solute). Driven by the water concentration gradient, which is inversely proportional to solute concentration. Water moves through aquaporins. Osmosis proceeds until either concentration is equalised or hydrostatic pressure balances the osmotic pressure. Key insight: water moves TOWARD higher solute concentration (lower water concentration).

📌 Mnemonic: SALTOUT — Salty water pulls water OUT. If a cell is placed in a salty (hypertonic) solution, water leaves the cell. If placed in a dilute (hypotonic) solution, water enters the cell.

🫧 Tonicity — How Osmolarity Affects Cell Volume

Hypotonic solution: extracellular fluid has LOWER solute concentration than cytoplasm → water enters the cell → cell swells (animal cells may lyse/burst; plant cells become turgid — good!). Isotonic solution: equal solute concentrations on both sides → no net water movement → cell unchanged. Hypertonic solution: extracellular fluid has HIGHER solute concentration than cytoplasm → water leaves the cell → cell shrinks (animal cells crenate; plant cells plasmolyse — bad!). Organisms maintain osmotic balance via osmoregulation.

📌 IV fluids are formulated as isotonic to blood plasma to prevent red blood cell lysis. Saltwater fish secrete excess salt through gills (hypertonic ocean pulls water out); freshwater fish actively take in salt (hypotonic stream pushes water in).

⚡ Six Factors That Affect Diffusion Rate

(1) Concentration gradient: larger gradient → faster diffusion.
(2) Molecular mass: heavier molecules → slower diffusion.
(3) Temperature: higher temp → faster diffusion (more kinetic energy).
(4) Solvent density: denser cytoplasm (e.g., during dehydration) → slower diffusion.
(5) Lipid solubility: nonpolar/lipid-soluble → faster membrane diffusion.
(6) Surface area & membrane thickness: larger surface area → faster; thicker membrane → slower.

📌 Distance limits cell size — the further a molecule must diffuse, the slower it gets there. This is why cells either stay small or develop specialised transport systems.
