Getting started

Grabbed… or was grabbed?

Tap each sentence to reveal what's really going on underneath it, then talk with a partner: what's the difference between them?

Sentence A
The professor grabbed the controls.
Tap to reveal ▾
The professor is the subject, and he does the action — he grabs. This is the active voice: the doer comes first, and the focus is on the professor.
Sentence B
The controls were grabbed by the professor.
Tap to reveal ▾
The controls is now the subject, but they don't do anything — the action is done to them. This is the passive voice: the professor is pushed to the end (or could be left out completely), and the focus shifts to the controls.

Think ahead: which version feels more mysterious, or puts more focus on the object instead of the person? Why might a writer choose one over the other?

How passive verbs are built

Passive verbs have a 'helping' verb (to be) + past participle.

Examples: The bag was dropped. The clothes are damaged.

The helping verb agrees with the subject and shows the tense (past, present or future). The main verb appears as the past participle.

Author's craft

Why do writers choose the passive voice?

The passive voice can leave out unimportant details, or it can build suspense by hiding who or what does the action. Tap each example to find out which.

Suspense in a single sentence

"Suddenly, I was lifted off the ground."
Tap to reveal: how does this create suspense?
The passive voice hides who or what lifted the narrator. The reader doesn't know if it's a person, a creature, a machine, or something stranger — that gap in knowledge is exactly what makes it tense and unsettling.

Language focus · Mood

If clauses (conditionals)

If clauses say what will or might happen in the present or future, or what could have happened — but didn't — in the past.

The main clause is always the result of the if clause.

If I take a torch, I will need batteries.
If clause main clause

If the If clause comes first, separate it from the main clause with a comma.

Key word

Dependent clause: a clause that cannot form a separate sentence on its own but adds information to the main clause — also called a subordinate clause. The If clause is a dependent clause.

Practice

Possible, likely, or impossible?

Some conditionals could really happen; others are impossible. Tap each one to check your thinking.
