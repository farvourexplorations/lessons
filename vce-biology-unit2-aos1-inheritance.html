Phase 1 · Wonder

🧬 Why Don't You Look Exactly Like Your Parents?

You inherited half your DNA from each parent — yet you're not a perfect blend of the two. Siblings from the same parents can look completely different. A genetic disorder can skip generations and then reappear. Identical twins share 100% of their DNA but can still develop different diseases. How does any of this work?

A mutation in a single gene causes cystic fibrosis. Two parents who are both healthy carriers have a 1 in 4 chance of having a child with the condition — every pregnancy. But if you don't know the parents are carriers, the disease can seem to appear "from nowhere." What determines whether a disease allele is expressed or hidden? And how do disease alleles persist in populations for generations without disappearing?

✏️ Your Prediction — Before We Begin

This Area of Study (B1.1.1 of the VCE Study Design) covers chromosomes to genomes, the production of gametes through meiosis, how genotypes and phenotypes relate, and the analysis of pedigree charts and patterns of inheritance — including autosomal, sex-linked, codominant, and incomplete dominance. You'll be able to complete monohybrid and dihybrid genetic crosses by the end.

Phase 2 · From Chromosomes to Genomes

🔬 Chromosomes, Genes, Alleles & Genomes

The VCE Study Design requires you to distinguish between genes, alleles, and a genome, and to understand how homologous chromosomes carry the same gene loci. Tap each term to explore.

Gene Allele Locus / Loci Genome Homologous Chromosomes Autosomes vs Sex Chromosomes Diploid vs Haploid Chromosome Variation
Gene — A sequence of DNA nucleotides at a specific location (locus) on a chromosome that codes for a functional product, usually a polypeptide (protein). Genes control characteristics by producing proteins that shape the structure and function of cells. One gene may influence multiple traits (pleiotropy), and many genes often contribute to a single trait (polygenic). VCE definition: "the unit of hereditary information."
Allele — One specific form of a gene. Alleles arise by mutation and occupy the same locus on homologous chromosomes. A diploid organism carries two alleles for each autosomal gene — one on each homologue. Alleles may be dominant (expressed when one copy is present) or recessive (expressed only when two copies are present). A population can have many alleles for one gene (multiple alleles), but an individual carries at most two.
Locus (plural: Loci) — The fixed physical location of a gene on a chromosome. All members of a species have the same loci for the same genes. The locus is fixed; what varies is which allele occupies that locus. Analogy: the locus is the "address" on the chromosome; the allele is "who lives there." Identifying a locus requires specifying both the chromosome and position on it.
Genome — The complete set of genetic information of an organism, including all DNA sequences in all chromosomes (and in mitochondria/chloroplasts). The human genome contains ~3.2 billion base pairs and ~20,000–25,000 protein-coding genes, occupying only ~1.5% of the total DNA. Genome size doesn't correlate with organism complexity (the C-value paradox — wheat has a larger genome than humans).
Homologous Chromosomes — Pairs of chromosomes (one from each parent) that carry genes for the same characteristics at the same loci. They are the same size and shape and pair up during meiosis I. Homologues may carry different alleles at corresponding loci. Humans have 23 pairs of homologous chromosomes (22 pairs of autosomes + 1 pair of sex chromosomes). The key word is "corresponding loci" — homologues are not identical.
Autosomes vs Sex Chromosomes — Autosomes are the 22 pairs of non-sex chromosomes in humans (chromosomes 1–22). Alleles on autosomes follow standard autosomal inheritance patterns. Sex chromosomes (X and Y in humans) determine biological sex. Females have two X chromosomes (XX); males have one X and one Y (XY). Genes on sex chromosomes show sex-linked (X-linked or Y-linked) inheritance — crucial for patterns like haemophilia and colour blindness.
Diploid (2n) vs Haploid (n) — Diploid cells contain two complete sets of chromosomes (one from each parent). In humans, 2n = 46. Haploid cells contain one set. Human gametes (sperm and eggs) are haploid (n = 23), produced by meiosis. When two gametes fuse at fertilisation, the resulting zygote is diploid (2n = 46). This maintains the chromosome number across generations. Many plants are polyploid (more than 2 sets).
Chromosome Variation — Different species have very different chromosome numbers and sizes. Chromosome number does not reflect complexity: humans have 46, dogs have 78, goldfish have 94, a fern may have over 1000. Chromosomes also vary in length and shape. Within a species, karyotype abnormalities can arise (e.g., trisomy 21 = Down syndrome, where chromosome 21 is present in three copies instead of two). The VCE Study Design requires you to be able to identify chromosome abnormalities from a karyotype.

Phase 3 · Meiosis & Genetic Diversity

🔀 Meiosis — How Gametes Are Made

Meiosis is the cell division that produces haploid gametes from diploid parent cells. The VCE Study Design specifically requires knowledge of crossing over of chromatids and independent assortment as sources of genetic diversity. Tap each stage to expand it.

If every gamete from a human contained the exact same chromosomes, siblings would be genetically identical. Instead, two processes in meiosis — crossing over and independent assortment — mean the number of theoretically possible different gametes from one person is over 8 million. Add in crossing over and the actual number is effectively limitless.

✏️ Before the Lab — Predict

1
Before Meiosis — DNA Replication
Before meiosis begins, the cell replicates its DNA during the S phase of interphase. Each chromosome now consists of two genetically identical sister chromatids joined at the centromere. The cell is still diploid (2n). This is the same replication that occurs before mitosis — the key difference is what happens after the DNA is copied.

Key term: Sister chromatids are identical copies joined at the centromere. They are NOT the same as homologous chromosomes.
VCE link: You are not required to know the details of S phase, but you must understand that the starting cell for meiosis is diploid (2n) with replicated chromosomes.
2
Meiosis I — Homologues Separate (Reductive Division)
Prophase I — Homologous chromosomes pair up (synapsis) forming a bivalent. During this pairing, chromatids from homologues physically overlap at points called chiasmata. At these points, segments of non-sister chromatids break and rejoin to a different homologue — this is crossing over (recombination). The result: new combinations of alleles on each chromosome.

Metaphase I — Bivalents line up at the cell equator. The orientation of each homologous pair is random — each pair can face either pole independently. This is independent assortment.

Anaphase I — Homologous chromosomes are pulled to opposite poles. Sister chromatids remain joined.

Telophase I / Cytokinesis — Two haploid cells form (n = 23 in humans), but chromosomes still consist of two sister chromatids each.
VCE exam tip: Meiosis I is the REDUCTIVE division — it halves the chromosome number from 2n to n. This is different from meiosis II.
3
Crossing Over — New Allele Combinations
Crossing over occurs between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes during Prophase I. Physical segments of DNA are exchanged at chiasmata (singular: chiasma). This creates recombinant chromosomes — new combinations of alleles that did not exist in either parent. The more crossovers, the greater the genetic diversity. Genes that are far apart on the same chromosome are more likely to be separated by crossing over. This is the basis of gene mapping.
VCE Study Design: You need to explain the significance of crossing over for genetic diversity — not the detailed molecular mechanism, just the outcome (new allele combinations on chromosomes).
4
Independent Assortment — Random Chromosome Orientation
During Metaphase I, each pair of homologous chromosomes lines up at the cell equator independently of every other pair. The side each chromosome faces (which pole it goes to) is random. With 23 pairs in humans: 2²³ = 8,388,608 possible chromosome combinations in gametes from independent assortment alone — before even considering crossing over. This explains why siblings from the same parents have different genetic makeups.
VCE exam: If asked to "explain the significance of independent assortment," state: each homologous pair orients independently at Metaphase I, producing gametes with different combinations of maternal and paternal chromosomes, increasing genetic variation in offspring.
5
Meiosis II — Sister Chromatids Separate
Meiosis II is similar to mitosis. Sister chromatids are pulled apart at Anaphase II. The result: four haploid cells (n), each with a unique combination of alleles due to crossing over and independent assortment. In males, all four cells become sperm. In females, meiosis is unequal — one large egg (ovum) and three small polar bodies form. The polar bodies are discarded.
VCE tip: Meiosis II does NOT reduce chromosome number (it's already haploid after Meiosis I). It separates sister chromatids. Final product = 4 haploid cells with unique genetics.

Phase 4 · Karyotypes

🧫 Karyotypes — Seeing the Whole Genome at Once

A karyotype is a visual representation of an organism's complete set of chromosomes, arranged in homologous pairs by size. Karyotypes allow scientists to detect chromosome number abnormalities. The VCE Study Design requires you to interpret karyotypes to identify chromosome abnormalities.

Karyotypes are produced by taking a photograph of chromosomes during mitosis (when they're most condensed), staining them, and arranging them in order. A normal human karyotype shows 46 chromosomes in 23 pairs. Deviations from this — extra chromosomes, missing chromosomes, or structural rearrangements — can cause genetic disorders. Prenatal karyotyping from amniotic fluid can detect many of these before birth.

Phase 5 · Genotypes & Phenotypes

🎭 Genotypes, Phenotypes & Patterns of Dominance

A genotype is an organism's actual allele combination. A phenotype is the observable characteristic produced by that genotype combined with environmental influences. The VCE Study Design requires you to work with dominant, recessive, codominant, and incomplete dominance phenotypes.

🧠 Core Terminology — Tap to Reveal

Homozygous Heterozygous Dominant Recessive Carrier Genotype vs Phenotype
Homozygous — Having two identical alleles at the same locus (e.g. AA or aa). A homozygous individual is "true-breeding" for that trait. All gametes carry the same allele for this gene. Written as homozygous dominant (AA) or homozygous recessive (aa).
Heterozygous — Having two different alleles at the same locus (e.g. Aa). Also called a "hybrid" for that trait. The phenotype expressed depends on the dominance relationship between the alleles. A heterozygous individual produces two types of gametes in equal proportions (A and a).
Dominant Allele — Expressed in the phenotype even when only one copy is present (in a heterozygote). Written with an UPPERCASE letter (e.g. A). In a heterozygote (Aa), the dominant phenotype is the one you see. Dominance is about which allele masks the other — it's a relationship between two alleles, not an intrinsic property.
Recessive Allele — Only expressed in the phenotype when two copies are present (homozygous recessive, aa). Written in lowercase (e.g. a). In a heterozygote (Aa), the recessive allele is "hidden" but still there and can be passed to offspring. Many genetic diseases (cystic fibrosis, phenylketonuria) are caused by recessive alleles.
Carrier — A heterozygous individual (Aa) who does not express a recessive trait but carries the recessive allele and can pass it to offspring. Carriers are clinically significant for autosomal recessive diseases (e.g. CF carriers are healthy but have a 1 in 4 chance of having an affected child if both parents are carriers).
Genotype vs Phenotype — Genotype is the actual allele combination at a locus (e.g. Aa). Phenotype is the observable characteristic (e.g. brown eyes). Same phenotype can arise from different genotypes (AA and Aa both look the same if A is dominant). The same genotype can produce different phenotypes in different environments (see Section 8). This distinction is fundamental to all genetics.

Sex-linked Inheritance — Genes located on the X chromosome (X-linked) have different inheritance patterns in males and females because males are hemizygous for X-linked genes (only one X chromosome). Males cannot be carriers for X-linked recessive traits — they either have the trait or they don't. This explains why colour blindness and haemophilia are far more common in males.

Phase 7 · Pedigree Analysis

🌳 Pedigree Charts — Tracing Inheritance Patterns

A pedigree chart shows the inheritance of a trait across multiple generations of a family. The VCE Study Design requires you to read and interpret pedigree charts to determine patterns of inheritance (autosomal dominant, autosomal recessive, X-linked) and predict genotypes of individuals.

Pedigree symbols: □ = unaffected male · ■ = affected male · ○ = unaffected female · ● = affected female · ◑/◐ = carrier (for X-linked) · horizontal line = mating · vertical line descends to offspring · siblings connected by horizontal line above them.

📊 Pedigree 1 — Autosomal Recessive Trait

Study this pedigree, then answer the questions below.
Q1. Both parents in Generation I are unaffected, but some of their children are affected. What does this tell you about the inheritance pattern?
Correct! Unaffected parents with affected children is the hallmark of autosomal recessive inheritance. Both Gen I parents must be carriers (Aa). The trait skips Generation I entirely. X-linked recessive would also give unaffected parents, but affected females (like II-2) make X-linked less likely unless her father were affected — which he's not. Autosomal recessive fits best.
Q2. What is the most likely genotype of individual I-1?
Correct! I-1 is unaffected but has affected children (e.g. II-2 is affected). For an affected child (aa) to arise from two unaffected parents, both parents must be carriers. Therefore I-1 must be Aa (a carrier). If I-1 were AA, no affected children could be produced.
Q3. What is the probability that III-1 (unaffected son of II-1 and II-2) is a carrier?
Correct! II-1 is unaffected (genotype A?) and II-2 is affected (aa). This means II-1 must be Aa (carrier) since he has an affected child. Cross: Aa × aa → offspring: Aa (carrier), aa (affected) in 1:1 ratio. Among the unaffected offspring, all must be Aa. But wait — if II-1's genotype is unknown before analysis, and the cross is Aa × aa, unaffected offspring are 100% Aa. However, if we don't know II-1's genotype for certain (he could be AA × aa = all Aa, or Aa × aa = 50/50), we need context. Given at least one affected child, II-1 must be Aa. So among unaffected offspring of Aa × aa: all are Aa. But the answer 2/3 applies when both parents are carriers (Aa × Aa) — the classic question. This pedigree shows Aa × aa, making all unaffected offspring carriers.

📊 Pedigree 2 — X-linked Recessive Trait

In this pedigree, the trait appears only in males but is carried by females. Determine the inheritance pattern.
Q4. The trait only appears in males. All affected males have carrier mothers. This pattern best indicates:
Correct! Classic X-linked recessive pattern: affects males more than females; unaffected carrier females pass it to sons; affected males have carrier mothers but unaffected fathers. Y-linked would pass directly from father to ALL sons — this doesn't match (I-1 is affected but his sons in Gen II differ). X-linked recessive is the correct answer.
Q5. Write the genotype of individual II-3 (carrier female) using X-linked notation. Let XN = normal allele, Xn = affected allele.
Correct! A carrier female has one normal X and one X carrying the recessive allele: XNXn. She shows the normal phenotype because XN is dominant. Her sons have a 50% chance of receiving Xn and being affected; her daughters have a 50% chance of being carriers. XnXn would be an affected female (rare for X-linked recessive).

Phase 8 · Environment & Epigenetics

🌍 Genes, Environment & Epigenetics

The VCE Study Design requires you to understand the proportionate influences of genetic material, and environmental and epigenetic factors, on phenotypes. The same genotype can produce different phenotypes depending on environmental conditions — and epigenetic marks can be heritable.

🌱 The Key Principle: Genotype + Environment = Phenotype

Phenotype is not determined by genes alone. Temperature, diet, toxins, stress, and even social environment can all influence how genes are expressed. Epigenetic modifications (changes to how genes are expressed without changes to the DNA sequence itself) can switch genes on or off — and some can even be passed to the next generation.

🌺 Hydrangea Colour — Soil pH

Hydrangeas (Hydrangea macrophylla) produce blue flowers in acidic soil and pink flowers in alkaline soil — with the same genotype. Soil pH affects the availability of aluminium ions, which modify the flower pigment. This is a classic example of the environment (soil pH) directly altering the phenotype without any change to the DNA. The gene is the same; the product it creates depends on environmental conditions.

📌 VCE link: Use this as an example to show that phenotype is not always predictable from genotype alone — environmental conditions must be considered.

🐱 Siamese Cat Coat — Temperature-sensitive Enzyme

Siamese cats have a mutation in the enzyme that produces dark pigment (melanin) — this enzyme is heat-sensitive and is deactivated above a certain temperature. At normal body temperature (~38°C), the enzyme is inactive — hence pale body fur. At the cooler extremities (ears, paws, face, tail), the enzyme is active, producing dark colouring. Temperature is the environmental variable determining where the gene is expressed. This is called temperature-dependent expression.

📌 VCE exam: If asked how environment influences phenotype, Siamese cats are a perfect example — same genotype throughout the body, different phenotype due to temperature variation.

🧪 PKU — Genetic Disease Controlled by Diet

Phenylketonuria (PKU) is caused by a homozygous recessive allele that prevents the breakdown of phenylalanine (an amino acid). Without treatment, phenylalanine builds up and causes intellectual disability. However, if diagnosed at birth and placed on a low-phenylalanine diet, affected individuals can develop normally — the same genotype produces a very different phenotype depending on diet. This shows environmental management can overcome genetic predisposition.

📌 PKU testing at birth (newborn screening) is routine in Australia. The VCAA may ask you to use PKU to illustrate how environmental factors can modify phenotypic expression of a genotype.

👯 Identical Twins — Same DNA, Different Outcomes

Monozygotic (identical) twins share 100% of their DNA. Yet they can develop differently — in personality, disease risk, body weight, and even physical appearance — due to different environmental exposures. Studies of identical twins raised apart show both similarities (genetic) and differences (environmental). As twins age, their epigenomes diverge more and more, despite identical DNA sequences. This is the strongest evidence that environment shapes phenotype independently of genotype.

📌 Twin studies are a key methodology in studying gene-environment interactions. VCE may ask you to interpret twin study data.

🔬 Epigenetic Modifications

Epigenetics refers to changes in gene expression that do not involve changes to the DNA sequence. Two main mechanisms: (1) DNA methylation — addition of methyl groups (−CH₃) to cytosine bases, typically silencing gene expression; (2) Histone modification — chemical modifications to histone proteins affect how tightly DNA is wound, controlling access of transcription machinery. Epigenetic marks can be influenced by diet, stress, toxin exposure, and age. Some epigenetic marks are heritable — passed from parent to offspring cells during mitosis, and potentially between generations.

📌 VCE Study Design: You need to know that epigenetic factors influence phenotypic expression. You do not need detailed molecular mechanisms — focus on the concept that gene expression can be altered without DNA sequence change.

📏 Height — Polygenic + Environmental

Human height is controlled by hundreds of genes (polygenic inheritance) AND substantially influenced by environment — especially nutrition in childhood. In populations with improved nutrition over generations, average height has increased even though the gene pool hasn't changed significantly. Studies of identical twins show a heritability of ~80% for height, meaning ~20% of variation is environmental. This demonstrates that even strongly "genetic" traits are not immune to environmental influence.

📌 A useful VCE answer format: "Height is a polygenic trait influenced by both genetic (multiple alleles contributing to height potential) and environmental factors (particularly nutrition during development, which can either realise or limit genetic potential)."
