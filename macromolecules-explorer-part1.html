Phase 1 · Wonder

🧬 Built From Six Ingredients

Bread, fruit, cheese, your own muscle and bone — all of it is built from just six elements: sulfur, phosphorus, oxygen, nitrogen, carbon, and hydrogen (remember it as SPONCH). Yet from these six elements, life builds an almost infinite variety of structures.

A strand of your DNA, the keratin in your hair, the starch in a potato, and the fat stored in your cells are all built the exact same way — by linking small repeating units together. If all of life's macromolecules are built using the same basic chemical reaction, what determines whether the result is a carbohydrate, a protein, a fat, or DNA?

✏️ Your Prediction (before we dig in)

Here's the answer: it all comes down to the type of monomer used and the reaction that links them. Complex polymers are built from monomers through dehydration synthesis (removing a water molecule to form a bond), and broken back down into monomers through hydrolysis (adding a water molecule to break a bond). Think of a train: each monomer is a boxcar, and the polymer is the entire train.

🗝️ The Big Picture

There are four major classes of biological macromolecules: carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids. Each is built from its own type of monomer (simple sugars, fatty acids/glycerol, amino acids, and nucleotides, respectively) using the same basic chemistry — dehydration synthesis to build, hydrolysis to break down. In this interactive, we'll master Sections 3.1 (Synthesis) and 3.2 (Carbohydrates).

Phase 2 · 3.1 Synthesis of Biological Macromolecules

📖 Building & Breaking — Core Vocabulary

Tap each term to reveal its definition and significance. This is the chemistry that underlies every single macromolecule you'll study this chapter.

SPONCH Monomer & Polymer Dehydration Synthesis Hydrolysis Energy of Each Reaction Four Classes Miller-Urey Experiment Enzyme Catalysis
SPONCH — A mnemonic for the six elements that make up most biological macromolecules: Sulfur, Phosphorus, Oxygen, Nitrogen, Carbon, Hydrogen. All four classes of macromolecules (carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, nucleic acids) are built primarily from these six elements in different combinations and quantities.
Monomer & Polymer — A monomer is a single subunit or "building block" molecule (like a train boxcar). When monomers link together via covalent bonds, they form a polymer (like the entire train). Even a single type of monomer can combine in different ways to form very different polymers — for example, glucose monomers alone make up starch, glycogen, AND cellulose, depending on how they're linked.
Dehydration Synthesis (Condensation) — The reaction that builds polymers from monomers. The hydrogen of one monomer combines with the hydroxyl (OH) group of another monomer, releasing a water molecule as the two monomers form a covalent bond. "Dehydration" = "to put together while losing water." This reaction requires an investment of energy to form the new bond.
Hydrolysis — The reaction that breaks polymers back down into monomers, using water. "Hydrolysis" = "to split with water." A water molecule is split: one part of the polymer gains an H⁺ and the other gains an OH⁻. This is exactly the reverse of dehydration synthesis, and it releases energy that can power cellular processes (this is how digestion works — enzymes hydrolyze the food you eat).
Energy Dynamics — Dehydration synthesis reactions typically require an investment of energy for new bond formation (they are anabolic/endergonic). Hydrolysis reactions typically release energy that can be used to power cellular processes (they are catabolic/exergonic). Both reaction types are catalyzed — "sped up" — by specific enzymes.
The Four Classes of Macromolecules — Carbohydrates (monomer: monosaccharides), Lipids (not true polymers — built from glycerol + fatty acids), Proteins (monomer: amino acids), and Nucleic Acids (monomer: nucleotides). Each macromolecule class performs distinct, essential biological functions, and each is broken down by a specific class of enzyme during digestion.
The Miller-Urey Experiment (1953) — Stanley Miller and Harold Urey built an apparatus to simulate early Earth conditions: boiling water (mimicking the ocean), methane, ammonia, and hydrogen gases (mimicking the early atmosphere), and electrical sparks (mimicking lightning). The result: organic compounds, including amino acids and nucleotides, spontaneously formed. This supports the hypothesis that the organic precursors of life's macromolecules could form from simple inorganic molecules on primitive Earth.
Enzyme Catalysis of Dehydration & Hydrolysis — Both reaction types are catalyzed by specific enzymes in living cells. Examples from digestion: carbohydrates are broken down by amylase, sucrase, lactase, maltase; proteins are broken down by pepsin and peptidase (plus HCl); lipids are broken down by lipases. Each macromolecule class has its own dedicated set of hydrolyzing enzymes.

🍯 Monosaccharides

Simple sugars — the basic monomer unit of carbohydrates. The number of carbons usually ranges from 3 to 7. Sugars with an aldehyde group are aldoses; sugars with a ketone group are ketoses. By carbon count: trioses (3C), pentoses (5C), and hexoses (6C). Glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆) is the most common monosaccharide and the primary energy source for cellular respiration.

📌 Glucose, galactose, and fructose are all hexoses (C₆H₁₂O₆) — but glucose and galactose are aldoses while fructose is a ketose.

🥛 Disaccharides

Form when two monosaccharides undergo dehydration synthesis, forming a glycosidic bond (the carbohydrate-specific name for the covalent bond linking two sugar monomers). Common disaccharides: maltose (glucose + glucose, "malt sugar"), lactose (glucose + galactose, found in milk), and sucrose (glucose + fructose, table sugar — the most common disaccharide).

📌 Sucrose forms when glucose (carbon 1) bonds to fructose (carbon 2) via a glycosidic linkage, releasing one water molecule.

🌾 Polysaccharides

Long chains of monosaccharides (may be branched or unbranched). Four key examples: Starch (plant energy storage — amylose is unbranched α1-4 links, amylopectin is branched with α1-6 links); Glycogen (animal energy storage — highly branched, stored in liver/muscle); Cellulose (plant structural support — unbranched β1-4 links, indigestible to humans); Chitin (insect/fungal structural support — nitrogen-containing).

📌 Storing glucose as starch/glycogen (rather than free glucose) prevents it from leaking out of cells and avoids creating high osmotic pressure.

🔀 Isomers (α vs. β bonds)

Glucose, galactose, and fructose share the same chemical formula (C₆H₁₂O₆) but differ in atom arrangement — they're structural isomers. In ring form, glucose can form an alpha (α) bond (OH below the ring at carbon 1 — digestible by animals) or a beta (β) bond (OH above the ring — NOT digestible by most animal enzymes). This single structural difference is why starch (α bonds) is digestible but cellulose (β bonds) is not!

📌 Cows and termites can digest cellulose only because of symbiotic bacteria/protists in their gut that produce the enzyme cellulase, which breaks β1-4 bonds.

✅ Reflect — Carbohydrate Classification

Carbohydrates provide about 4.3 Kcal/gram compared to fat's 9 Kcal/gram. Dietary fiber (mostly cellulose) can't be digested by human enzymes, but it promotes regular digestion, helps regulate blood glucose uptake, and binds to cholesterol in the small intestine to help remove it from the body.
