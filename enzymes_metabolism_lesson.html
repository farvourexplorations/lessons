Getting started

What is metabolism, really?

Metabolism isn't one reaction — it's the entire web of thousands of enzyme-controlled reactions happening inside you, right now. Every one of those reactions falls into one of two families.

Catabolic reactions break large molecules into smaller ones. Energy stored in chemical bonds is released — this is how you get usable energy out of food.

Why do we need enzymes?

Getting over the energy hill

Most reactions in your cells could happen on their own — but painfully slowly, over years. Before reactant molecules can turn into products, they need a small energy "push" called the activation energy, shown as the hill on the graph.

An enzyme is a biological catalyst. It doesn't change the reactants or products — it simply lowers the size of that hill, so far more molecules have enough energy to react at body temperature.

Remember

An enzyme is not used up or changed by the reaction. Once it releases its products, it's free to catalyse the same reaction again — and again, thousands of times a second.

Without enzyme — high activation energy With enzyme — lowered activation energy
Enzyme structure

A perfect (and flexible) fit

Enzymes are globular proteins folded into a precise 3D shape. Part of that shape forms the active site — a pocket where one specific substrate binds.

The active site isn't a rigid, fixed shape. As the substrate binds, the active site moulds slightly around it, forming a snug enzyme–substrate complex. This flexibility is why it's called induced fit — the fit is induced by the substrate binding.

Early scientists imagined the active site as a rigid lock that only one key (substrate) could fit — a useful first picture, but too simple. Real active sites flex and adjust, which is why induced fit replaced this model.

Factors affecting rate — temperature

Too cold, too hot, just right

Drag the slider to change temperature and watch what happens to the rate of an enzyme-catalysed reaction.

0°C 70°C 20°C

As temperature rises, molecules move faster and collide more often — the rate of reaction increases. But past the optimum, rising heat starts to break the weak bonds holding the enzyme's 3D shape together.

The protein denatures — its active site loses its precise shape, the substrate no longer fits, and the rate collapses.

Exam tip

Most human enzymes have an optimum around 37–40°C. The rise before the optimum is gradual; the fall after it is sharp and steep — the two halves of the curve are not symmetrical.

Factors affecting rate — pH

Every enzyme has its own comfort zone

pH affects the charges on amino acids in the enzyme, which can distort the active site. Different enzymes in your body have very different optimum pH values, matched to where they work.

pH 0 pH 14 pH 7.0

Pepsin digests protein in the highly acidic stomach — its optimum is around pH 2. Trypsin digests protein in the small intestine, where pancreatic secretions make the environment mildly alkaline — its optimum is around pH 8.

Move far enough from an enzyme's optimum pH in either direction — too acidic or too alkaline — and the same thing happens as with heat: the enzyme's shape distorts and, if extreme enough, it denatures.

Why it matters

This is exactly why pepsin would be useless in the small intestine, and trypsin would be useless in the stomach — each enzyme is matched to the pH of the compartment it works in.

Factors affecting rate — concentration

More molecules, faster reaction — up to a point

Unlike temperature and pH, increasing substrate or enzyme concentration never slows the reaction down. Instead, the rate rises and then levels off at a plateau.

Increasing substrate concentration

With enzyme concentration fixed, adding more substrate speeds things up — until every active site is occupied at once. After that, adding more substrate makes no difference.

Increasing enzyme concentration

With substrate fixed, adding more enzyme speeds things up — until substrate becomes the limiting factor. Beyond that point, extra enzyme molecules simply have nothing to bind.

Both curves plateau for the same underlying reason: whichever reactant is scarcer becomes the limiting factor.
A closer look

Denaturation isn't the same as "dying"

A protein isn't alive, so it can't die — but heat, extreme pH, and heavy metal ions can permanently disrupt the bonds holding its 3D shape together.

Native (folded) enzyme

The active site is a precise shape, complementary to its substrate. Catalysis proceeds normally.

Denatured enzyme

The tertiary structure has unravelled. The active site no longer exists in a usable shape — the substrate can't bind at all.

Precise wording matters

In an exam answer, say the enzyme's tertiary structure is disrupted and its active site changes shape — not that the enzyme "melts" or "gets killed." Denaturation breaks weak bonds (hydrogen, ionic, some disulfide) holding the folded shape; it does not break the strong covalent bonds of the primary structure (the amino acid sequence itself stays intact).

HL extension

Enzyme inhibitors

An inhibitor is a molecule that reduces enzyme activity. There are two very different ways this can happen.

✕

A competitive inhibitor is shaped like the real substrate and binds directly to the active site — it competes with the substrate for the same spot.

Key fact: because it's a fair fight for the same site, adding a lot more substrate can out-compete the inhibitor and restore the rate. The effect is reversible by increasing substrate concentration.

A non-competitive inhibitor binds somewhere else entirely — an allosteric site — and distorts the enzyme's overall shape so the active site stops fitting the substrate.

Key fact: since it isn't competing for the active site, adding more substrate does not reverse the effect — the substrate simply has nowhere to bind.

The "does more substrate fix it?" question is the fastest way to tell these two apart in an exam.
