Phase 1 · Wonder

🌳 No Heart, No Pump — So How Does a 100m Tree Drink?

A giant redwood can grow taller than a 30-storey building. Its roots pull water from the soil, but the leaves at the very top are over 100 metres away — with no heart, no pump, and no muscles anywhere in the plant.

Meanwhile, sugar made by photosynthesis in the leaves has to travel all the way down to the roots, which have no chlorophyll and cannot make their own food. Two substances need to travel in almost opposite directions, through two completely separate tissues. How do you think water manages to rise so high against gravity with no pump — and how might sugar travel from the leaves down to the roots?

✏️ Your Prediction (before we dig in)

The answer: plants have two separate transport tissues. Xylem carries water and mineral ions upward in one direction only, pulled by the continuous evaporation of water from the leaves (transpiration). Phloem carries dissolved sugars and amino acids in either direction, from wherever they are made or stored to wherever they are needed — a process called translocation. Let's explore both systems.

Phase 2 · 2.53–2.54 Xylem & Phloem

🌿 Two Transport Tissues, Two Jobs

Flowering plants have a vascular bundle containing two transport tissues that run side by side but do completely different jobs. Tap each term to reveal its definition.

Xylem Phloem Translocation Transpiration Stream Vascular Bundle
Xylem — Tissue made of dead, hollow, lignified (strengthened) tubes that transport water and mineral ions from the roots to the stem, leaves and other parts of the plant. Movement is one-way only — upward. The lignin in xylem walls also gives the plant mechanical support.
Phloem — Tissue made of living sieve tube elements (with perforated end walls) and companion cells, which transport sucrose and amino acids made in the leaves (or released from storage organs) to other parts of the plant that need them, such as roots, flowers, fruits and growing shoots.
Translocation — The movement of sucrose and amino acids through phloem tissue, from a "source" (where they are made or stored, e.g. a photosynthesising leaf) to a "sink" (where they are used or stored, e.g. roots, buds, fruits). Unlike xylem, translocation can move substances up or down the plant depending on where the source and sink are.
Transpiration Stream — The continuous, one-way movement of water through a plant: from the soil → into root hair cells → across the root → up the xylem → into the leaves → out through the stomata as water vapour. It is driven by evaporation of water from the leaf surface, which "pulls" more water up behind it.
Vascular Bundle — A strand of tissue running through the root, stem and leaf of a plant, containing both xylem (usually toward the centre) and phloem (usually toward the outside) running alongside each other. This arrangement allows water/ion transport and sugar transport to happen simultaneously throughout the whole plant.

💧 Xylem

Made of dead cells joined end to end into long, hollow tubes with no cross-walls or cell contents — this creates an unobstructed pipe. Their walls are thickened with a strengthening material called lignin, which also helps support the plant and keeps it upright. Xylem transports water and dissolved mineral ions from the roots, and movement only ever goes one way: upward, driven by transpiration.

📌 Exam answer: "Xylem transports water and mineral ions from the roots to the rest of the plant. It is made of dead, hollow, lignified cells, which also provide structural support."

🍯 Phloem

Made of living sieve tube cells stacked end to end, with perforated "sieve plates" between them that allow dissolved substances to flow through. Each sieve tube is supported by a companion cell next to it, which provides the energy needed for transport. Phloem transports sucrose and amino acids and can move them in either direction — this process is called translocation.

📌 Exam answer: "Phloem transports sucrose and amino acids between the leaves, where they are made, and other parts of the plant that need them, such as the roots and growing regions."

Phase 3 · 2.55B Root Hair Cells

🌱 Root Hair Cells — Where the Journey Begins

Before water can travel up the xylem, it first has to get into the plant. This happens at specialised cells on the root surface called root hair cells.

Root hair cells are single-celled extensions of the root epidermis. Each one grows a long, thin hair-like projection into the soil, dramatically increasing the surface area available for water and mineral ion absorption.

📐 Large Surface Area

The long, thin hair-like extension increases the surface area of the cell that is in contact with the soil, so more water and mineral ions can be absorbed at the same time. This is a very common IGCSE "adaptation" answer style.

📌 Exam answer: "The root hair provides a large surface area for the absorption of water and mineral ions."

🧻 Thin Cell Wall / Membrane

A thin wall and membrane means substances have a shorter distance to diffuse or move across, increasing the rate of water and ion uptake.

📌 Exam answer: "A thin cell wall provides a short diffusion pathway for water and mineral ions entering the cell."

🧪 Concentrated Cell Sap

The vacuole of a root hair cell contains a fairly concentrated solution of sugars and salts. This gives the cell a lower water potential than the surrounding soil water, which is usually very dilute. This water potential difference is exactly what drives water into the cell by osmosis.

📌 Exam answer: "The cell sap has a lower water potential than the soil water, so water moves into the root hair cell by osmosis, down the water potential gradient."

⚡ Many Mitochondria

Mineral ions (such as nitrate and potassium ions) are often already at a higher concentration inside the root hair cell than in the soil. Diffusion alone cannot move them further in. Instead, mineral ions are absorbed by active transport, which needs energy (ATP) from respiration — hence the large number of mitochondria.

📌 Exam answer: "Root hair cells contain many mitochondria to provide energy (ATP) from respiration for the active transport of mineral ions against their concentration gradient."

Phase 4 · 2.56B Transpiration

💨 Transpiration — The Engine Behind the Pull

So what actually drives water all the way up the xylem to the top of a tree? The answer is transpiration — and it happens at the opposite end of the plant from where water enters.

Definition (learn this word for word): Transpiration is the evaporation of water from the surface of a plant, mainly through tiny pores called stomata on the underside of leaves. As water vapour is lost from the leaf's internal air spaces, more water is drawn up through the xylem to replace it — creating a continuous pull called the transpiration stream.

🌡️ Step 1 — Water evaporates from mesophyll cells into the leaf's air spaces▼
Water on the surface of spongy mesophyll cells inside the leaf evaporates into the air spaces between the cells. This lowers the water potential of the mesophyll cells, so water moves into them by osmosis from neighbouring cells.
📌 The leaf's internal air spaces provide a large surface area for this evaporation to occur efficiently.
🚪 Step 2 — Water vapour diffuses out through open stomata▼
Because the water vapour concentration is higher inside the leaf's air spaces than in the air outside, water vapour diffuses out of the leaf through the open stomata (tiny pores, mostly on the underside of the leaf, each controlled by a pair of guard cells).
📌 Exam link: Guard cells control the size of the stomatal pore, and therefore the rate of transpiration.
🧲 Step 3 — Loss of water "pulls" more water up the xylem▼
As mesophyll cells lose water, they draw in replacement water from the xylem in the leaf veins. This creates a continuous "pull" of water molecules all the way down the xylem, through the stem, and up from the root — this unbroken chain of moving water is the transpiration stream.
📌 This pulling effect is strong enough to draw water to the top of very tall trees with no pump needed — water molecules effectively "stick together" as they are pulled upward.
🌱 Step 4 — More water is drawn into root hair cells to replace the loss▼
As water leaves the xylem in the roots to replace water pulled up higher in the plant, the water potential in the root drops, so more water is drawn in from the soil into root hair cells by osmosis — restarting the whole process.
📌 Exam tip: transpiration is often described as a "necessary side-effect" of gas exchange — stomata must open for CO₂ to enter for photosynthesis, and this inevitably lets water vapour escape too.

📊 Why Does the Plant Need the Transpiration Stream?

💧 Humidity

Humidity is the amount of water vapour already in the air. In low humidity (dry air), there is a much steeper water vapour concentration gradient between the leaf's air spaces and the outside air, so water vapour diffuses out faster. In high humidity, the air outside is already nearly saturated with water vapour, reducing the gradient and slowing diffusion out of the stomata.

📌 Exam answer: "In low humidity, the concentration gradient of water vapour between the leaf and the air is steeper, so water diffuses out of the stomata faster, increasing the rate of transpiration."

💨 Wind Speed

Moving air constantly sweeps away water vapour molecules from around the stomata as soon as they diffuse out, preventing a "humid layer" building up near the leaf surface. This keeps the concentration gradient steep, so diffusion — and transpiration — continues at a fast rate.

📌 Exam answer: "Higher wind speed removes water vapour from around the leaf surface, maintaining a steep concentration gradient, so the rate of transpiration increases."

🌡️ Temperature

Higher temperatures give water molecules more kinetic energy, so they evaporate from cell surfaces inside the leaf faster and diffuse out of the stomata more quickly. Higher temperature also means the air can hold more water vapour before becoming saturated, keeping the gradient steep.

📌 Exam answer: "As temperature increases, water molecules gain more kinetic energy and evaporate faster, increasing the rate of diffusion of water vapour out of the stomata."

☀️ Light Intensity

Light intensity affects transpiration indirectly, through the stomata. In bright light, guard cells cause stomata to open wide (so the plant can take in CO₂ for photosynthesis), which also lets more water vapour escape. In darkness, most stomata close, dramatically reducing water loss.

📌 Exam answer: "In bright light, stomata open wider to allow more carbon dioxide in for photosynthesis, which also increases the rate at which water vapour is lost, so transpiration increases."

🧪 2.58B Practical — Investigating Factors Affecting Transpiration Rate

Using a potometer, a piece of apparatus that measures the rate of water uptake by a cut, leafy shoot (used as an estimate for transpiration rate).

🔬 Method — Setting Up a Potometer▼
Cut a leafy shoot underwater (to prevent air entering the xylem) and attach it to the potometer, again underwater, so no air bubbles enter the system. Ensure all joints are airtight and watertight (e.g. sealed with petroleum jelly). Introduce a single air bubble into the capillary tube. As the shoot takes up water (as a proxy for transpiration), the air bubble moves along the scale on the capillary tube — the distance moved in a set time gives the rate of water uptake.
📌 Exam trap: the potometer measures the rate of water uptake, not transpiration directly — but the two are assumed to be approximately equal, since most water taken up is lost by transpiration.
📊 Variables in the Investigation▼
Independent variable: the environmental factor being tested (e.g. use a fan for wind speed, a lamp at different distances for light intensity, a heater for temperature, or a plastic bag/humidifier for humidity).

Dependent variable: distance moved by the air bubble in a fixed time (rate of water uptake).

Controlled variables: the same shoot/species, same leaf area, and keep all other environmental factors constant while only changing the one being tested.
📌 Exam tip: always reset the air bubble to the same starting point before each repeat, and take repeat readings to calculate a mean.
📈 Typical Results & Explanation▼
The air bubble moves further per minute under conditions of: high temperature, low humidity, high wind speed, and high light intensity — because each of these increases the concentration gradient for water vapour diffusion, or (for light) causes the stomata to open wider.
📌 A student can plot rate of bubble movement (mm/min) against each factor to produce a graph showing a positive correlation for temperature, wind and light, and a negative correlation for humidity.

📝 Pearson Edexcel-Style Exam Questions

Written in the style and command words used by Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Biology (4BI1) past papers. Write your answers, then click Reveal All Answers to check against mark-scheme-style responses.

Command word: Describe · 3 marks
Describe the role of xylem in a flowering plant. [3]
Tip: "Describe" wants a developed account, not just one word.
Indicative content (3 marks):
  • Transports water (and mineral ions) [1]
  • From the roots to the stem / leaves / other parts of the plant [1]
  • Made of dead, hollow, lignified cells (which also provide support) [1]
Note: "Xylem carries water" alone only earns 1 mark — you need direction and a structural detail too.
Command word: Describe · 2 marks
Describe the role of phloem in a flowering plant. [2]
Tip: name both substances transported.
Indicative content (2 marks):
  • Transports sucrose and amino acids [1]
  • Between the leaves and other parts of the plant (e.g. from source to sink) [1]
Do not say "food" — the mark scheme wants the named substances: sucrose and amino acids.
Command word: Explain · 3 marks
Root hair cells absorb mineral ions by active transport. Explain why active transport, rather than diffusion, is needed for this process. [3]
Tip: "Explain" needs a reason, not just a description.
Indicative content (3 marks):
  • The concentration of mineral ions is often already higher inside the root hair cell than in the soil [1]
  • So diffusion cannot move ions further in, as diffusion only moves substances down a concentration gradient [1]
  • Active transport uses energy (ATP) from respiration to move ions against their concentration gradient [1]
Command word: Explain · 4 marks
A plant is moved from a humid greenhouse to a windy, dry field. Explain the effect this is likely to have on its rate of transpiration. [4]
Tip: address BOTH factors named in the question (humidity AND wind).
Indicative content (4 marks):
  • Transpiration rate will increase [1]
  • Lower humidity increases the water vapour concentration gradient between the leaf and the air, so water diffuses out faster [1]
  • Higher wind speed removes water vapour from around the leaf/stomata [1]
  • This maintains a steep concentration gradient, so diffusion of water vapour out of the stomata continues at a fast rate [1]
Command word: Determine · 2 marks
A potometer is used to measure water uptake in a leafy shoot. The air bubble moves 24mm in 6 minutes. Determine the rate of water uptake in mm per minute. [2]
Tip: "Determine" requires a numerical answer with working shown.
Indicative content (2 marks):
  • 24 ÷ 6 [1]
  • = 4 mm per minute [1]
Always show the division, not just the final answer — method marks are available even if the final number is wrong.
Command word: Suggest · 3 marks
Cacti live in hot, dry environments and have very few stomata, most of which are sunk into pits on the stem surface. Suggest how this reduces water loss by transpiration. [3]
Tip: "Suggest" allows you to apply your knowledge to an unfamiliar (novel) situation.
Indicative content (3 marks):
  • Fewer stomata means a smaller total area/number of pores through which water vapour can diffuse out [1]
  • Sunken stomata trap a pocket of still, humid air around the pore [1]
  • This reduces the water vapour concentration gradient between the leaf/stem and the air outside, slowing diffusion out [1]
Command word: Explain · 3 marks
A ring of bark, including the phloem, is removed from around a tree trunk (a process called ring-barking), while the xylem underneath is left intact. Explain why the roots eventually die, but the leaves remain healthy for some time. [3]
Tip: think about which tissue was removed and what it transports.
Indicative content (3 marks):
  • Phloem transports sucrose (and amino acids) from the leaves (source) down to the roots (sink) [1]
  • With the phloem removed, sucrose can no longer reach the roots, so the roots starve of food/energy [1]
  • The xylem is undamaged, so water and mineral ions can still travel up to the leaves, keeping them alive/healthy in the short term [1]
