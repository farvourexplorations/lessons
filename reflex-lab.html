The Big Question

How fast is "fast"?

Touch a hot pan and your hand pulls back before you even decide to move it. A ball flies at your face and you blink without thinking. Something in your body is faster than your thoughts. Today, we're going to measure it.

Think about the fastest you've ever reacted to something — dodging a dodgeball, catching a falling phone, slamming the brakes on your bike. What did that feel like? Did you have time to "think," or did your body just do it?

🗂️ Three types of neurons work together in every reflex:

👁️ Sensory Neuron 🧠 Interneuron 💪 Motor Neuron
Sensory Neuron — the lookout
Detects information from the outside world (light, sound, touch, the ruler falling past your fingers) and carries that signal toward the spinal cord and brain.
Interneuron — the relay
Lives inside the spinal cord and brain. It connects sensory neurons to motor neurons — sometimes skipping the brain entirely, which is exactly what makes reflexes so fast.
Motor Neuron — the action-taker
Carries the "do something!" signal away from the spinal cord/brain to your muscles — telling your fingers to snap shut around that ruler.

4
Investigation

📏 The Ruler Drop Experiment

Time to test your own reflex arc for real, with a partner. Read the instructions below, then go run the experiment — come back here to log your results.

🧪 How to run it

You'll need: 1 ruler (30 cm, marked in mm) + 1 partner

  1. Catcher: Rest your thumb and finger open around the bottom of the ruler (the 0 mm end) — but don't touch it.
  2. Dropper: Hold the ruler from the top, vertically, so the bottom of the ruler sits right between the catcher's open fingers.
  3. Dropper: Without counting down or giving any warning, let go of the ruler.
  4. Catcher: Grab the ruler as fast as you possibly can, using only your finger and thumb — don't move your whole hand down to meet it!
  5. Both: Read the number (in mm) lined up with the TOP of the catcher's thumb. That's how far the ruler fell before being caught — a smaller number means a faster reaction!
  6. Repeat 5 times in silence and record your "Quiet" trials.
  7. Then your teacher will play music — repeat 5 more times while the music plays and record your "Music" trials.
  8. Switch roles so both partners get a turn catching for both conditions.
💡 Why mm and not seconds? A falling ruler accelerates due to gravity, so the distance it falls tells us how long it fell — scientists can convert this into an actual reaction time using a simple drop-distance formula. We won't do that maths today, but just know: every measurement in mm is secretly also a measurement in milliseconds!

5
Why This Matters

🌍 Reflexes in the real world

The nervous system isn't just a science-class idea — it's working every second of your life, often before you even notice.

🏎️

Sports

A goalkeeper diving for a shot, or a sprinter exploding off the blocks — both rely on trained reflex speed. Athletes practice reactions just like muscles, to shave milliseconds off their response time.

🚗

Driving Safety

Reaction time is exactly why following distance matters. A driver going 60 km/h travels several metres in the time it takes their reflex arc to respond to a brake light.

🩺

Doctors & Reflex Tests

That little hammer-tap-on-the-knee test? Doctors check reflexes like the knee-jerk to make sure your spinal cord and nervous system are working correctly — no brain required for that one!

🤔 Compare: Reflex vs. Decision

⚡ Reflex Action

Automatic, doesn't need conscious thought, often skips the brain (just spinal cord).

🧠 Conscious Decision

Involves the brain weighing choices — slower, but more flexible.
