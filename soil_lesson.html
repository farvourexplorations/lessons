Where does soil come from?

From solid rock to soft soil

The Earth's crust is made almost entirely of rock. Weathering slowly breaks that rock down into tiny mineral particles. Mixed together with the remains of dead plants and animals, those particles become soil — a process that can take hundreds or even thousands of years.

Frost-shattered rock — the very beginning of the long journey from solid rock to soil.

Rock + time + life = soil

As weathered rock fragments build up, plants begin to take root among them. As those plants — and the animals that feed on them — die and decay, their remains mix in with the mineral particles. Over a very long time, this mixture deepens and matures into the soil that covers most of the land around us.

What's in a handful of soil?

Soil is not just crushed-up rock. Tap each slice of this diagram to discover the four ingredients that make up healthy soil.

1

1 — Mineral particles (about 45%)

Tiny weathered fragments of rock — sand, silt and clay — that make up almost half of most soils.

2 — Water (about 25%)

Water fills many of the gaps between particles, carrying dissolved nutrients that plant roots absorb.

3 — Air (about 25%)

Air fills the remaining gaps between particles — plant roots and soil organisms need this air to respire.

4 — Organic matter / humus (about 5%)

Decayed remains of plants and animals. Even in a small amount, humus is packed with the nutrients plants need to grow.

Also living in the soil…

Earthworms, insects, fungi and countless bacteria live throughout these four ingredients. Earthworms burrow through soil, mixing and aerating it, while bacteria and fungi break down dead matter into the humus that feeds new plants.

a   Colour

You can see straightaway whether soil is pale, yellowish, reddish, brown or almost black — colour often hints at how much organic matter or iron the soil contains.

b   Texture

How gritty, smooth or sticky the soil feels between your fingers tells you about the size of its particles — sandy soils feel gritty, clay soils feel smooth and sticky.

c   Living things & organic matter

Look closely for bits of dead leaves, roots, insects or worms — these show how much organic matter (humus) the soil contains.

d   Drainage

Pour water onto a soil sample and time how quickly it drains away — this tells you how well the soil lets water pass through it.
Not all soil is the same

Three main types of soil

The size and mix of the mineral particles in a soil completely changes how it behaves. Explore the three main types below.

Large particles, big gaps

Sandy soil is made of large, gritty mineral particles with big air gaps between them. Water drains through very quickly, so sandy soil dries out fast and does not hold onto nutrients well. It is light, easy to dig, and warms up quickly in the sun.

Large particlesFast drainageDries out quickly
Sandy soil — loose, gritty and free-draining.

Tiny particles, packed tight

Clay soil is made of extremely tiny particles packed closely together with almost no air gaps. Water drains through very slowly, so clay soil can become waterlogged — but when it dries out, it becomes hard and cracks. It is heavy and difficult to dig.

Tiny particlesVery slow drainageHard when dry
Clay soil — dense, sticky when wet, and rock-hard when dry.

The best of both worlds

Loam soil is a balanced mixture of sand, silt and clay particles, together with plenty of organic matter. It drains well enough to avoid waterlogging, yet still holds enough water and nutrients for plants. This makes loam the soil most gardeners and farmers want for growing healthy crops.

Balanced mixtureGood drainageBest for plants
Loam soil — dark, crumbly and rich in organic matter.

The soil sedimentation test

Scientists and gardeners find out what a soil is made of by shaking a sample with water in a jar and letting it settle. Press the button to run the test — watch which particles sink first.

Soil and water, freshly shaken

Why do the layers separate like this?

When you shake soil up with water, every particle is briefly mixed together. As the jar settles, gravity pulls the heaviest, largest particles down first — the rest settle in order of size, lightest last.

Sand — largest, heaviest particles, settles first at the bottom
Silt — medium-sized particles, settles next
Clay — tiny particles, settles last as a fine top layer
Organic matter — lightest of all, often floats on the water's surface

By measuring the thickness of each layer, you can work out roughly how much sand, silt and clay a soil sample contains — and whether it is closer to a sandy, clay or loam soil.

Workbook · 3.4 Practice, question 4

Sofia and Zara's vegetable plot

Sofia and Zara want to plant vegetables on a small plot of ground. The soil is hard and dry. The soil is yellowish in colour. There are no bits of dead leaves or animals in it.

a   Describe the texture and colour of the soil.

The soil is hard and dry in texture, and yellowish (pale) in colour.

b   Does the soil contain organic matter? How do you know this?

No. There are no bits of dead leaves or animals in it, and organic matter is exactly what those remains would be — so this soil has little or no organic matter (humus).

c   Is this a sandy soil, a clay soil or a loam soil?

This sounds like a clay soil. Clay is made of very fine, tightly packed particles that become hard and can crack as they dry out — unlike sandy soil, which stays loose and crumbly even when dry.

d   Do you think the soil is suitable for growing vegetables? Explain why or why not.

Not as it is. Clay soil like this drains poorly when wet and becomes rock-hard when dry, making it difficult for roots to grow through. With no organic matter present, it also lacks the nutrients vegetables need to grow well.

e   Write down three ways that Sofia and Zara can change the composition of the soil to make it more suitable for growing vegetables.

1) Dig in plenty of compost or well-rotted manure to add organic matter and nutrients. 2) Mix in some sand or grit to open up the structure and improve drainage. 3) Add a mulch on top and turn the soil regularly to improve air flow and stop it compacting again.

Organic farming & biological pest control

Organic farming uses environmentally friendly methods that improve the soil and keep people healthy, because no chemicals end up in the food produced. Organic farmers always rotate their crops — they never plant the same crop on the same soil year after year — and use compost instead of artificial fertilisers.

Biological pest control means using a pest's natural predators, rather than chemicals, to protect crops. Natural predators can be insects such as ladybirds, bees and spiders, or lizards, frogs and birds. Organic farmers plant flowers near their crops and hedges at the edges of fields to attract these predators. Aphids, for example, are a common pest that eat leaves, stems and fruit — so if a farmer makes sure there are always ladybirds nearby, the ladybirds will eat the aphids. It is even possible to buy ladybirds for this purpose!

A ladybird larva eating an aphid — biological pest control in action.

Answer the questions — tap each one to reveal the model answer

a   Describe two ways organic farmers maintain the composition of their soil.
1) They rotate their crops, so the same crop is never grown in the same soil year after year — this stops nutrients being used up and pests or diseases building up. 2) They use compost instead of artificial fertilisers, which adds organic matter and nutrients back into the soil naturally.
b   What is biological pest control?
Using a pest's natural predators — such as ladybirds, bees, spiders, lizards, frogs or birds — to control its numbers, instead of using chemical pesticides.
c   How do organic farmers use biological pest control?
They plant flowers near their crops and grow hedges at the edges of fields to attract natural predators such as ladybirds. Some farmers even buy ladybirds and release them onto the farm so they eat pests like aphids.
d   Find out the names of two birds and two animals that are natural predators of snails.
Birds: thrushes and ducks (owls and starlings are also known to eat snails). Animals: hedgehogs and frogs or toads (some beetles are natural predators too).
