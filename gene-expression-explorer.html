Phase 1 · Wonder

🧬 How Does DNA Become You?

You are studying for the AP Biology exam. Unit 10 covers the Central Dogma — the flow of information from DNA → RNA → Protein — including transcription, translation, and mutations. Let's start with a big question.

Every cell in your body contains the exact same DNA. Yet a muscle cell looks and acts completely differently than a neuron or a skin cell. If they all have the same genetic instructions, what determines which "instructions" actually get used in each cell?

✏️ Your Prediction (before we dig in)

Here's the kicker: the answer is gene expression — the process by which the information in a gene is used to make a functional product (usually a protein). Every trait you have, every protein in your body, and even the genetic diseases that exist all trace back to this one process.

🗝️ The Big Picture

The central dogma of molecular biology describes how genetic information flows in one general direction: DNA → RNA → Protein. DNA is transcribed into messenger RNA (mRNA) in the nucleus, and that mRNA is then translated into a chain of amino acids — a protein — at the ribosome. Proteins do almost everything: they're enzymes, structural materials, signals, transporters, antibodies, and receptors.

Central Dogma of Molecular Biology — Explains that genetic information flows from DNA → RNA → Protein. DNA is transcribed into RNA, and RNA is translated into protein. This one-way flow of information is the foundation of molecular biology. Q1.
Gene Expression — The entire process by which the information in a gene is used to direct the synthesis of a functional product (a protein, or sometimes a functional RNA). It includes both transcription and translation. Q4, Q5.
Five Functions of Proteins — Proteins are incredibly versatile. Five major functions: (1) Enzymes (catalyze reactions), (2) Structural (e.g. collagen, keratin), (3) Transport (e.g. hemoglobin), (4) Signaling/hormones (e.g. insulin), (5) Defense/antibodies (immune system) — also receptors and motor proteins. Q2.
Codon — A sequence of three nucleotides in mRNA that codes for one amino acid (or a start/stop signal). Since each codon = 1 amino acid, it takes 3 codons (9 nucleotides) to code for 3 amino acids. Q6.
Amino Acids — The building blocks (monomers) of proteins. There are 20 different amino acids used in living cells. Nucleotide sequences determine the order of amino acids, which determines protein structure and function. Q3, Q7.
mRNA (messenger RNA) — Carries the genetic "message" copied from DNA out of the nucleus to the ribosome, where it is translated into a protein. mRNA is crucial because it's the actual molecule that ribosomes "read" to build proteins — it's the go-between linking DNA's code to protein synthesis. Q8.
tRNA (transfer RNA) — Each tRNA carries a specific amino acid and has an anticodon that base-pairs with a codon on the mRNA, ensuring the correct amino acid is added to the growing protein chain during translation. Q9.
rRNA (ribosomal RNA) — A structural and catalytic component of the ribosome itself. rRNA helps form the ribosome's structure and catalyzes the formation of peptide bonds between amino acids during translation. Q9.
Cellular Location — In eukaryotic cells, transcription occurs in the nucleus (where DNA is housed), while translation occurs in the cytoplasm, at ribosomes (free-floating or on the rough ER). This spatial separation is unique to eukaryotes. Q10.

🧬 DNA Template & The mRNA Transcript

In transcription, one strand of DNA is used as a template for building a complementary, antiparallel RNA strand. The mRNA is synthesized using base-pairing rules: A pairs with U (not T, since this is RNA!), and C pairs with G. The mRNA is built in the 5' → 3' direction, antiparallel to the template strand it's reading.

📌 Q11: "In transcription, DNA is used as a template for building an RNA strand."
📌 Q13: Template 5' ATTACGTAAA 3' → mRNA transcript reads 5' UUUACGUAAU 3' (complementary AND antiparallel — flip the strand and swap T→U, A→A, etc.)

🚦 The Promoter

The promoter is a specific DNA sequence located before (upstream of) a gene's coding region. It acts like a "landing pad" — it's the sequence that RNA polymerase binds to in order to initiate (begin) transcription. Without a promoter, RNA polymerase wouldn't know where to start.

📌 Q15: The sequence RNA polymerase binds to in order to initiate transcription is the promoter.

⚙️ RNA Polymerase

RNA polymerase is the primary enzyme of transcription. It binds to the promoter, unwinds the DNA double helix itself (no helicase needed!), and synthesizes a complementary mRNA strand by adding RNA nucleotides one at a time in the 5'→3' direction. Primase is also NOT needed — RNA polymerase doesn't require a primer the way DNA polymerase does. RNA polymerase detaches from the DNA template when it reaches the terminator sequence.

📌 Q14: The primary enzyme of transcription is RNA polymerase.
📌 Q17: Helicase is NOT needed — RNA polymerase unwinds DNA itself.
📌 Q18: Primase is NOT needed for transcription.
📌 Q19: RNA polymerase detaches when it reaches the terminator sequence.

🛑 The Terminator

The terminator sequence marks the end of the gene's coding region. When RNA polymerase transcribes this sequence, it signals the enzyme to stop adding nucleotides and detach from the DNA template, releasing the newly made mRNA transcript.

📌 Q19: "When does RNA polymerase detach from the DNA template strand?" → When it transcribes the terminator sequence.
📌 Q20: In eukaryotes, after termination, the mRNA travels out of the nucleus, through nuclear pores, to the cytoplasm/ribosomes for translation.

🚦 1. Initiation

RNA polymerase binds to the promoter sequence on the DNA. The DNA double helix unwinds and separates near the start of the gene, exposing the template strand. No helicase or primase is required — RNA polymerase handles unwinding itself.

📌 Packet connection: Q15 (promoter), Q17 & Q18 (no helicase/primase needed).

➡️ 2. Elongation

RNA polymerase moves along the DNA template in the 3'→5' direction, synthesizing the new mRNA strand in the 5'→3' direction. It adds complementary RNA nucleotides (remember: U pairs with A) one at a time, following base-pairing rules.

📌 Packet connection: Q13 & Q16 (building the complementary mRNA transcript from a DNA template).

🛑 3. Termination

When RNA polymerase reaches the terminator sequence at the end of the gene, it stops adding nucleotides, detaches from the DNA, and releases the completed mRNA transcript.

📌 Packet connection: Q19 (RNA polymerase detaches at the terminator).

🚪 mRNA Export (Eukaryotes Only)

In eukaryotic cells, the newly made mRNA must exit the nucleus through nuclear pores to reach the cytoplasm, where ribosomes will translate it into a protein. This extra step is one reason eukaryotic gene expression takes longer than prokaryotic gene expression.

📌 Packet connection: Q20 — after transcription termination, eukaryotic mRNA travels to the cytoplasm (to be translated at ribosomes).
