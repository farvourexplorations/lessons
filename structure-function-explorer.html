Phase 1 · Wonder

🔬 Why Does Shape Matter?

Welcome to Anatomy & Physiology I. Every unit this semester — bones, muscles, nerves, blood — builds on one core idea you'll use again and again. Let's find it for ourselves first.

A red blood cell is shaped like a flattened, dimpled disc — not a sphere, not a tube. That exact shape lets it squeeze through capillaries one-tenth its own diameter while still carrying huge amounts of oxygen. What is it about a structure's shape that determines what it's able to do?

✏️ Your Prediction (before we dig in)

Here's the idea: this is the structure-function relationship — anatomy (structure) and physiology (function) are two sides of the same coin. The shape, size, and organization of a structure directly determines what it can do. Learn to ask "why is it shaped this way?" and physiology stops being memorization and starts making sense.

🗝️ The Big Picture

Anatomy is the study of the body's structure — its shape, size, and how its parts are arranged. Physiology is the study of how those structures function — how they work, individually and together, to keep you alive. Neither one makes full sense without the other, which is exactly why this course studies them side by side, starting from the smallest building blocks and working up to the whole organism.

Phase 2 · Foundational Vocabulary

📖 Core Vocabulary — Tap to Reveal

Tap each term to see its definition. These are the building blocks for every unit this semester — know all of these cold.

Anatomy Physiology Gross vs. Microscopic Structure-Function Relationship Levels of Organization Anatomical Position Directional Terms Body Planes
Anatomy — The study of the body's structure: the shape and arrangement of its parts, from the whole body down to individual cells. Anatomy asks "what does it look like, and where is it?"
Physiology — The study of how the body's structures function: the chemical and physical processes that let cells, tissues, organs, and systems do their jobs. Physiology asks "how does it work?"
Gross vs. Microscopic Anatomy — Gross anatomy studies structures large enough to see with the naked eye (organs, muscles, bones — approached regionally or system by system). Microscopic anatomy studies structures too small to see without a microscope, including histology (tissues) and cytology (cells).
Structure-Function Relationship — The principle that a structure's form directly enables (and limits) what it can do. A flat, thin surface is built for diffusion; a long, branching cell is built for signaling; a hollow muscular tube is built for pumping. You'll use this lens in every unit of A&P.
Levels of Organization — The body is organized in a hierarchy, from simplest to most complex: chemical → cellular → tissue → organ → organ system → organismal. The chemical level starts with subatomic particles (protons, electrons, neutrons) that combine into atoms and then molecules — the building blocks of every structure above it. Each level is built from the level below it.
Anatomical Position — The universal reference posture: standing upright, facing forward, arms at the sides, palms facing forward, feet flat and facing forward. Every directional term in anatomy assumes the body is in this position — regardless of how the person is actually oriented.
Directional Terms — Standardized pairs used to describe location relative to anatomical position: superior/inferior (above/below), anterior/posterior (front/back), medial/lateral (toward/away from the midline), proximal/distal (closer to/farther from the point of attachment — used on limbs), and superficial/deep (closer to/farther from the body surface).
Body Planes — Imaginary flat surfaces used to section the body for study: a sagittal plane divides left and right, a frontal (coronal) plane divides anterior and posterior, and a transverse (horizontal) plane divides superior and inferior.

🩸 Red Blood Cell — Biconcave Disc

A red blood cell is shaped like a flattened, dimpled disc rather than a sphere. That biconcave shape increases its surface area relative to its volume, which speeds gas diffusion, and it also makes the cell flexible enough to bend and squeeze through capillaries far narrower than its resting diameter. Mature red blood cells even lose their nucleus and most organelles — freeing up interior space to pack in more oxygen-carrying hemoglobin.

📌 Structure: flattened, dimpled, no nucleus → Function: maximum surface area, flexibility, and hemoglobin capacity for oxygen transport.

🧠 Neuron — Long, Branching Cell

A neuron's cell body sprouts many short, branching dendrites that increase surface area to receive signals from other cells, plus a single long axon — sometimes over a meter long — built to carry an electrical signal rapidly across distance. Many axons are wrapped in a fatty myelin sheath, which speeds conduction even further.

📌 Structure: branching dendrites + long myelinated axon → Function: receive input from many sources and transmit signals quickly over long distances.

🌿 Intestinal Villi & Microvilli

The lining of the small intestine is folded into finger-like projections called villi, and each cell on a villus is covered in even smaller projections called microvilli. These folds-within-folds dramatically increase the absorptive surface area, and each villus contains its own capillary network and lacteal to carry absorbed nutrients straight into the bloodstream and lymph.

📌 Structure: folded villi covered in microvilli → Function: massively increased surface area for absorbing digested nutrients.

🫁 Alveoli — Thin-Walled Air Sacs

Alveoli are tiny, thin-walled sacs at the end of the airway tree, each wrapped in a dense network of capillaries. Their walls are only a single layer of flattened (squamous) cells thick, minimizing the distance gases must diffuse, and there are hundreds of millions of them — creating an enormous total surface area packed into a small space.

📌 Structure: single-cell-thick walls, capillary-wrapped, enormous total surface area → Function: fast, efficient diffusion of oxygen and carbon dioxide.

⚖️ How the Body Keeps Its Balance

Homeostasis is the body's ability to maintain a relatively stable internal environment despite changes in the outside world. It's maintained through feedback loops — and almost all of them are negative feedback loops, which counteract a change to restore balance.

⚡ Stimulus

The stimulus is the change in the internal environment that sets the feedback loop in motion — for example, a rise in body temperature during exercise, or a drop in body temperature in cold weather.

📡 Receptor

The receptor detects the stimulus and monitors the current value of the variable being regulated. Thermoreceptors in the skin and in the hypothalamus itself detect changes in temperature.

🧭 Control Center

The control center receives information from the receptor and compares it to the set point — the ideal target value (about 98.6°F / 37°C for body temperature). For thermoregulation, the control center is the hypothalamus. If the value has drifted from the set point, the control center signals an effector to respond.

🦾 Effector

The effector is the muscle, organ, or gland that carries out the corrective action. For body temperature, effectors include sweat glands (to cool down) and skeletal muscles that shiver (to generate heat) along with blood vessels that dilate or constrict.

✅ Response

The response is the effector's action itself, which moves the variable back toward the set point. In negative feedback, the response counteracts (reverses) the original stimulus — once the set point is reached, the receptor stops signaling and the loop shuts off.

🔄 Negative Feedback

The response counteracts, or reverses, the original stimulus — pushing the variable back toward its set point. This is how the body maintains stability most of the time.

📌 Examples: body temperature regulation (sweating/shivering), blood glucose regulation (insulin/glucagon), blood pressure regulation.

📈 Positive Feedback

The response amplifies, or strengthens, the original stimulus rather than reversing it. Positive feedback loops are rarer and are usually shut off by an outside event rather than by reaching a set point.

📌 Examples: uterine contractions intensifying during childbirth (stopped by delivery), platelet aggregation during blood clotting (stopped once the clot seals the vessel).
