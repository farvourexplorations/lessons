Phase 1 — Wonder

🌌 Why Do Directions Matter?

A pilot flying at 300 km/h into a 100 km/h headwind moves at 200 km/h relative to the ground. The same pilot flying with the wind reaches 400 km/h. But crosswinds are more complex — you can't just add or subtract them. Direction changes everything in physics.

✨ A river flows east at 2 m s⁻¹. A swimmer strikes out north at 1.5 m s⁻¹. Where do they actually go? A force of 100 N north and a force of 100 N east do NOT add up to 200 N. Understanding why — and what the resultant really is — is the foundation of all Newtonian mechanics, projectile motion, and beyond.

This workshop covers A1.2.1–A1.2.7 of the IB DP Physics 2023 syllabus: scalar vs vector quantities, vector representation, addition by triangle and parallelogram rules, resolving into perpendicular components, and resultant vectors. SL and HL content is labelled throughout.

🏆 Physics in the Real World: Why Vectors Matter

Every engineering structure, every GPS system, every flight plan, and every robotics system relies on vector mathematics. Architects use vector force analysis to ensure bridges don't collapse. Physicists use vectors to model particle collisions at CERN. Mastering vector addition here unlocks all of mechanics, fields, and waves.

Phase 2 · A1.2.1–A1.2.2 — Scalars & Vectors

📐 Scalar and Vector Quantities SL

A scalar quantity has magnitude only. A vector quantity has both magnitude and direction. This distinction is not cosmetic — it changes the rules of arithmetic entirely. You cannot add vectors the way you add scalars unless they are perfectly aligned.

Key IB definitions:
• Scalar: described completely by a magnitude and a unit (e.g. 35 J, 12 kg, 20°C)
• Vector: described by a magnitude, a unit, AND a direction (e.g. 40 N east, 15 m s⁻¹ at 30° above horizontal)
• Vectors are represented by arrows — the length encodes magnitude, the arrowhead encodes direction
• On paper: bold type (F) or an overarrow (F⃗) denotes a vector. In IB exams, context usually makes it clear.

🗂️ Scalar or Vector? — Tap Each

Displacement Distance Velocity Speed Force Mass Acceleration Energy
Displacement — Vector. Displacement is the straight-line distance from start to finish point, with direction. "5 km north-east" is displacement; "5 km" is distance. When you walk 3 m north then 4 m east, your displacement is 5 m north-east (not 7 m). IB note: always specify a reference direction or angle when stating displacement.
Distance — Scalar. Distance is the total path length travelled, regardless of direction. It is always positive and never negative. "I walked 400 m" is a distance — no direction needed. Distance ≥ magnitude of displacement. They are only equal when motion is in a straight line with no backtracking.
Velocity — Vector. Velocity is the rate of change of displacement. It has both magnitude (speed) and direction. "10 m s⁻¹ east" is a velocity; "10 m s⁻¹" is speed. An object in circular motion has constant speed but continuously changing velocity (direction changes), so it is always accelerating.
Speed — Scalar. Speed is the magnitude of velocity. It tells you how fast something moves but not where it is going. Average speed = total distance / total time. Instantaneous speed = magnitude of instantaneous velocity. Because it has no direction, two objects with the same speed can be heading in completely different directions.
Force — Vector. Forces have both magnitude (in newtons) and direction. A 10 N force upward and a 10 N force downward do NOT cancel to 20 N — they produce a net force of 0 N. This is the key reason vectors matter in mechanics. Forces must always be added using vector addition, not simple arithmetic.
Mass — Scalar. Mass is the amount of matter in an object, measured in kilograms. It has no direction. Regardless of where you are in the universe or how you are oriented, the mass of a 2 kg ball is 2 kg. Mass is often confused with weight — weight is a force (a vector); mass is a scalar.
Acceleration — Vector. Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity. Since velocity is a vector, its rate of change is also a vector. Acceleration can be caused by a change in speed, a change in direction, or both. IB HL note: centripetal acceleration points toward the centre of a circular path even when speed is constant.
Energy — Scalar. Energy (kinetic, potential, thermal, etc.) has magnitude and a unit (joules) but no direction. You cannot say "3 J upward." Energy is stored, transferred, or dissipated — these are all scalar processes. This makes energy calculations simpler: you can add and subtract energy values algebraically without worrying about direction.

Phase 3 · A1.2.3–A1.2.4 — Vector Addition

➕ Adding Vectors: Triangle & Parallelogram Rules SL

The resultant vector is the single vector that has the same effect as two or more vectors combined. There are two equivalent graphical methods: the tip-to-tail (triangle) rule and the parallelogram rule. For calculation, we use Pythagoras and trigonometry.

Triangle Rule (Tip-to-Tail): Draw vector A, then draw vector B starting at the tip of A. The resultant R goes from the tail of A to the tip of B. Order doesn't matter — you get the same resultant.

Parallelogram Rule: Draw both vectors from the same point. Complete the parallelogram. The diagonal from the shared origin is the resultant. Useful when vectors act on a single point simultaneously (like two forces on a hook).

🔢 Worked Example: Two Perpendicular Forces

A hook is pulled by force A = 60 N east and force B = 80 N north. Find the resultant force.

Step 1 — Sketch: draw A east and B north from the same point.
Step 2 — Magnitude: R = √(60² + 80²) = √(3600 + 6400) = √10000 = 100 N
Step 3 — Direction: θ = arctan(80/60) = arctan(1.333) = 53.1° north of east (i.e. bearing 036.9°)
Answer: Resultant = 100 N at 53.1° north of east (N53.1°E)

Phase 4 · A1.2.5–A1.2.6 — Resolving Vectors

📊 Resolving into Components SL HL

Any vector can be split (resolved) into two perpendicular components, usually horizontal (x) and vertical (y). This transforms a 2D vector problem into two independent 1D problems. Resolving is the algebraic method of vector addition — and is essential for IB exam calculations.

For a vector of magnitude F at angle θ above the horizontal:

• Horizontal component: Fₓ = F cos θ
• Vertical component: F_y = F sin θ

To add multiple vectors algebraically: resolve each into components, sum all x-components (ΣFₓ), sum all y-components (ΣF_y), then reconstruct: R = √(ΣFₓ² + ΣF_y²) and θ = arctan(ΣF_y / ΣFₓ).

Weight (W = mg)

Weight acts vertically downward with magnitude mg. On a slope, it cannot be balanced directly by the normal force (which acts perpendicular to the slope surface). This is why we must resolve weight into two components — one along the slope, one perpendicular to it.

W = mg e.g. for m = 5 kg: W = 5 × 9.81 = 49.1 N downward

W∥ — Component Parallel to Slope

W∥ = mg sin θ, where θ is the angle of the slope to the horizontal. This is the component that would cause the object to accelerate down the slope (if friction is insufficient). On a steeper slope, sin θ is larger, so this component is larger — explaining why steeper slopes feel harder to climb.

θ = 30°: W∥ = mg sin 30° = 0.5 mg  |  θ = 45°: W∥ = mg sin 45° = 0.707 mg

W⊥ — Component Perpendicular to Slope

W⊥ = mg cos θ. This component pushes the object into the slope surface. The normal force exactly balances W⊥ when the object is stationary or moving at constant speed (no acceleration perpendicular to slope). IB HL: friction force ≤ μN = μ mg cos θ.

θ = 30°: W⊥ = mg cos 30° = 0.866 mg  |  θ = 0° (flat): W⊥ = mg (all weight goes into surface)

Normal Force N

The normal force acts perpendicular to the slope surface, away from the surface. In equilibrium: N = W⊥ = mg cos θ. Note: the normal force is NOT equal to mg unless the slope is horizontal (θ = 0). This is a very common IB exam mistake — always resolve the weight before stating the normal force on an incline.

Common IB error: writing N = mg on a slope. Correct: N = mg cos θ

Phase 5 · A1.2.7 — Equilibrium

⚖️ Equilibrium and the Zero Resultant SL

An object is in translational equilibrium when the vector sum of all forces acting on it equals zero. This means ΣF = 0, or equivalently, ΣFₓ = 0 and ΣF_y = 0. The object is either at rest or moving at constant velocity (Newton's 1st Law).

A traffic light hanging from two wires does not fall. The tension in the wires, resolved into vertical components, must exactly balance the downward weight. If the wires are at a shallow angle, the tensions must be enormous — this is why suspension bridge cables are never horizontal. The shallow angle problem is a classic IB exam scenario.

⚖️ Equilibrium Conditions — Tap Each

Translational Equilibrium Closed Triangle Rule Three Force Problem Lami's Theorem
Translational Equilibrium: The vector sum of all forces = 0. This requires BOTH ΣFₓ = 0 and ΣF_y = 0. A body in equilibrium may be at rest (static equilibrium) or moving at constant velocity (dynamic equilibrium). IB note: you must state the vector condition, not just "forces are balanced" — examiners want ΣF = 0 or a diagram showing the closed triangle.
Closed Triangle (Polygon) Rule: If three (or more) forces are in equilibrium, their vectors drawn tip-to-tail form a closed polygon. For three forces, they form a closed triangle. This graphical test is useful for checking equilibrium problems. If the vectors do NOT close (gap at the end), the body is NOT in equilibrium.
Three Force Problem: A very common IB scenario: weight W acts downward; two cables make angles θ₁ and θ₂ with the vertical. Set ΣFₓ = 0: T₁ sin θ₁ = T₂ sin θ₂. Set ΣF_y = 0: T₁ cos θ₁ + T₂ cos θ₂ = W. Solve simultaneously. Always draw a clear free-body diagram first.
Lami's Theorem (HL extension): For three concurrent forces in equilibrium, each force divided by the sine of the angle between the other two is equal: F₁/sin α = F₂/sin β = F₃/sin γ, where α, β, γ are the angles between the force pairs (not the angles each force makes with a reference). Useful shortcut for three-force equilibrium problems.

🔢 Worked Example: Traffic Light on Two Cables

A 20 kg traffic light hangs from two cables each making 40° with the vertical.

Step 1 — Weight: W = mg = 20 × 9.81 = 196 N downward
Step 2 — Vertical: T₁cos40° + T₂cos40° = W. By symmetry T₁ = T₂ = T.
2T cos40° = 196 → T = 196 / (2 × cos40°) = 196 / 1.532 = 128 N
Check horizontal: T₁sin40° − T₂sin40° = 0 ✓ (equal and opposite by symmetry)
Answer: Each cable has tension ≈ 128 N. If angle is 80° instead: T = 196/(2cos80°) = 565 N — huge!

✏️ Explain: Why does reducing the cable angle increase tension so dramatically?

🔬 Physics in Context: Structural Engineering

Every bridge, crane, and cable-stayed structure is a vector equilibrium problem. Engineers must ensure ΣF = 0 at every joint. The Sydney Harbour Bridge, the Burj Khalifa's foundation, and the cables of the Golden Gate Bridge are all designed so that vector sums of forces equal zero. This is not abstract — it is why buildings stand up.

Phase 6 — IB-Style Exam Questions

📝 IB Exam Practice SL HL

Work through these exam-style questions, write your own answers first, then reveal the mark-scheme response. Focus on precise language, correct significant figures, and meeting the command terms (state, define, calculate, show, evaluate).

A1.2.1 — SL — State (2 marks)
State the difference between a scalar quantity and a vector quantity. Give one example of each.
Award 1 mark each (max 2):
  • A scalar has magnitude only; a vector has both magnitude and direction (1)
  • Scalar example: speed / mass / temperature / energy / distance (1); Vector example: velocity / force / displacement / acceleration / momentum (1)

Examiner note: "Vectors have direction" alone is insufficient for the definition mark — you must contrast with scalars. "Vector has magnitude and direction; scalar has magnitude only" scores both marks.

A1.2.4 — SL — Calculate (3 marks)
A boat has a velocity of 4.0 m s⁻¹ due east relative to the water. The current flows at 3.0 m s⁻¹ due north. Calculate the magnitude and direction of the boat's resultant velocity relative to the ground.
Award 1 mark each (max 3):
  • Correct method: uses Pythagoras (1) — R = √(4.0² + 3.0²) = √(16 + 9) = √25
  • Magnitude: R = 5.0 m s⁻¹ (1) (accept 5 m s⁻¹ with correct working)
  • Direction: θ = arctan(3.0/4.0) = arctan(0.75) = 36.9° north of east (accept 37° N of E or bearing 053°) (1)

Examiner note: Full marks require both magnitude and direction. Magnitude alone scores 2/3. Direction must be given relative to a named reference — "north of east" or a bearing is acceptable; "at an angle" alone is not.

A1.2.5 — SL — Show (3 marks)
A force of 80 N acts at 60° above the horizontal. Show that its horizontal component is approximately 40 N.
Full marks require:
  • States formula: Fₓ = F cos θ (1)
  • Substitution: Fₓ = 80 × cos 60° = 80 × 0.5 (1)
  • Result: Fₓ = 40 N (1) — "show" questions require all working visible; do not just state the answer

IB note: In "show" questions, every step must be shown — even if the arithmetic seems trivial. An unsupported answer of 40 N scores 0/3.

A1.2.7 — SL — Deduce (4 marks)
A 30 kg box is in equilibrium on a frictionless ramp inclined at 25° to the horizontal, held in place by a rope parallel to the slope. Deduce the tension in the rope. (g = 9.81 m s⁻²)
Award 1 mark each (max 4):
  • States or implies ΣF = 0 along the slope (equilibrium condition) (1)
  • Identifies component of weight along slope: W∥ = mg sin 25° (1)
  • Substitution: T = 30 × 9.81 × sin 25° = 294.3 × 0.4226 (1)
  • Answer: T = 124 N (accept 120–125 N) with correct unit (1)

Common errors: using cos instead of sin (confusing parallel and perpendicular components), or omitting the equilibrium reasoning. A free-body diagram that correctly shows weight resolved into two components earns the method marks even if the arithmetic is slightly off.

A1.2.6 — HL — Calculate (4 marks)
Three forces act on a point: F₁ = 120 N at 0°, F₂ = 90 N at 120°, F₃ = 60 N at 240°. Calculate the magnitude and direction of the resultant force.
Award 1 mark each (max 4):
  • Correct x-components: F₁ₓ=120, F₂ₓ=90cos120°=−45, F₃ₓ=60cos240°=−30; ΣFₓ = 120−45−30 = +45 N (1)
  • Correct y-components: F₁_y=0, F₂_y=90sin120°=+77.9, F₃_y=60sin240°=−52.0; ΣF_y = 0+77.9−52.0 = +25.9 N (1)
  • Magnitude: R = √(45² + 25.9²) = √(2025+671) = √2696 ≈ 51.9 N (accept 52 N) (1)
  • Direction: θ = arctan(25.9/45) = arctan(0.576) = 30.0° above positive x-axis (1)
A1.2.7 — SL/HL — Evaluate (4 marks)
A sign of weight W hangs from two ropes making equal angles θ with the vertical. Evaluate what happens to the tension in the ropes as θ increases from 0° to 90°. What is the physical significance of θ → 90°?
For full marks (4):
  • Derives: 2T cos θ = W → T = W/(2cos θ) (1)
  • At θ = 0°: T = W/2 — ropes vertical, each carries half the weight (1)
  • As θ increases, cos θ decreases, so T increases (1)
  • As θ → 90°: cos θ → 0, T → ∞ — ropes horizontal is physically impossible for a finite rope material; explains why cables must always have some sag (1)

Evaluating includes the limiting cases and a physical interpretation. "T increases as θ increases" without the formula or limit scores only 1–2.
