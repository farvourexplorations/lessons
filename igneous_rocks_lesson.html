Rocks are different

Meet the geologist's eye

Rocks differ from one another because of how they were formed. Scientists who study rocks are called geologists — and for the rest of this topic, that's exactly the job you're doing.

Geologists sort rocks into families by looking closely for clues: colour, grain size, whether crystals are visible, and whether the rock has layers.

Safety first

When collecting rock samples in the field: never climb or approach steep rock faces, always work with an adult, and label each sample with your name and location.

What are igneous rocks?

Born from fire

"Igneous" comes from the Latin for fire. Every igneous rock began life as magma — hot, liquid rock — that eventually cooled into a solid.

Cools fast, at the surface

Some magma erupts onto the surface as lava. Because it meets cool air or water, it loses heat quickly and solidifies in minutes to days. There's no time for large crystals to grow, so extrusive rock has crystals that are far too small to see with the naked eye.

Example: basalt — a dark, fine-grained rock that forms huge sheets across ocean floors and volcanic islands.

Lava reaching the surface — the birthplace of extrusive rock.

Cools slowly, underground

Some magma never reaches the surface. It stays trapped deep in the crust, insulated by rock above it, and cools over thousands to millions of years. That slow cooling gives crystals plenty of time to grow large and visible.

Example: granite — a pale, speckled rock with crystals you can easily see. It only appears at the surface once the rock above it has worn away.

Granite — an intrusive igneous rock with large, visible crystals.
Cause and effect

Cooling speed decides crystal size

Deep underground · thousands of years

Fast cooling

At the surface · minutes to days

Tiny, invisible crystals → extrusive rock (e.g. basalt)

Why does granite look speckled?

Granite forms slowly underground, so each of its minerals has time to grow its own separate crystal. As they crystallise side by side, they squash together — giving granite its patchwork, speckled appearance.

The Giant's Causeway, Northern Ireland — thousands of hexagonal basalt columns formed as a huge lava flow cooled and cracked as it shrank.

Why hexagons?

As a thick lava flow cools evenly from the top down, it contracts. The most efficient way for the rock to crack under that even stress is into repeating hexagonal columns — the same pattern you see in cracked mud as it dries out, just on a much larger scale.

Challenge

Arun and Marcus's holiday puzzle

Arun and Marcus have gone to an island on holiday. There's a big mountain in the middle of the island, and they're walking along a beach covered in black stones, smoothed by the sea.

"I think these stones are granite," says Arun.
"No, Arun — they're basalt," says Marcus.

Who's correct?

Marcus is correct. The stones are black and smooth with crystals too small to see — classic signs of basalt, an extrusive igneous rock. A mountain in the middle of an island is often a volcano, so lava reaching the surface and cooling quickly explains why this rock is on the beach. Granite would look speckled and pale, with large visible crystals, because it cools slowly underground.
Volcanic islands are often ringed with dark, fine-grained basalt — cooled lava from past eruptions.
