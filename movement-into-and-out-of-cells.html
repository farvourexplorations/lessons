Phase 1 · Wonder

💧 How Do Things Get In and Out of Cells?

Your cells are constantly exchanging substances with their surroundings — taking in oxygen and glucose, getting rid of carbon dioxide and waste. But how? The cell membrane controls everything.

If you drop a tea bag into hot water, the colour spreads outward without stirring. If you put a raisin into water overnight, it swells up. If a plant wilts in dry soil but recovers after watering — all three of these happen by the same set of principles that govern how your cells work. Can you name what's happening in each?

✏️ Your Thinking — Before We Begin

There are three mechanisms cells use to move substances across their membranes: diffusion (random movement down a gradient), osmosis (water movement through a partially permeable membrane), and active transport (energy-powered movement against a gradient). Each one explains one of the scenarios above.

Phase 2 · Diffusion

🌬️ Diffusion

The IGCSE examiners expect you to give a precise, formal definition of diffusion. They also expect you to explain the factors that affect its rate and give biological examples.

Definition (learn this word for word): Diffusion is the net movement of particles from a region of their higher concentration to a region of their lower concentration (down a concentration gradient), as a result of their random movement.

🔑 Key Vocabulary — Tap Each Term

Concentration Gradient Net Movement Equilibrium Passive Process Partially Permeable Membrane
Concentration Gradient — The difference in concentration of a substance between two regions. The steeper the gradient (bigger difference), the faster diffusion occurs. Particles always move DOWN their gradient (from high to low) until equilibrium is reached.
Net Movement — The overall direction of movement. Particles move randomly in all directions, but there are MORE moving from high to low concentration than the reverse — so the NET movement is from high to low. At equilibrium, particles still move but net movement = zero.
Equilibrium — When concentrations are equal on both sides of a membrane. Diffusion does NOT stop at equilibrium — random movement continues — but there is no net movement in either direction.
Passive Process — Diffusion requires NO energy (ATP). It relies entirely on the random kinetic energy that particles already possess. This distinguishes it from active transport, which requires energy from respiration.
Partially Permeable Membrane — A membrane with pores that allow small molecules (like water, oxygen, CO₂, glucose) to pass through but block larger molecules (like proteins and starch). The cell membrane is partially permeable. Exam note: do NOT say "semi-permeable" — use "partially permeable".

📐 Surface Area

A larger surface area provides more space through which particles can diffuse simultaneously. This is why organisms that rely heavily on diffusion (like the lungs and small intestine) have highly folded membranes to maximise surface area.

📌 Exam answer: "As surface area increases, the rate of diffusion increases because there is more membrane available for particles to pass through at the same time."

🌡️ Temperature

Higher temperature gives particles more kinetic energy, so they move faster and collide more frequently with the membrane. This increases the rate of diffusion. Exam trap: say "particles gain kinetic energy and move faster" — don't just say "particles move more".

📌 Exam answer: "As temperature increases, particles gain kinetic energy and move faster, increasing the rate of diffusion."

📉 Concentration Gradient

The steeper the gradient (the bigger the difference in concentration between two regions), the faster diffusion occurs. More particles are moving from the high side to the low side per unit time. This is the most important factor in biological systems.

📌 Exam answer: "A steeper concentration gradient means more particles move from high to low concentration per unit time, increasing the rate of diffusion."

📏 Distance

The shorter the distance particles must travel, the faster they arrive at their destination. This is why cell membranes are extremely thin and why the walls of alveoli are only one cell thick — minimising diffusion distance maximises rate.

📌 Exam answer: "A shorter diffusion distance means particles travel less far to reach their destination, so the rate of diffusion increases."

Phase 3 · Osmosis

💧 Osmosis

Osmosis is a special case of diffusion that applies only to water. The IGCSE definition is very specific — every word matters in the exam.

Definition (learn this word for word): Osmosis is the net movement of water molecules from a region of higher water potential (dilute solution) to a region of lower water potential (concentrated solution), through a partially permeable membrane.

🧠 Key Terms — Tap Each

Water Potential Turgid Flaccid Plasmolysis Hypotonic / Hypertonic / Isotonic
Water Potential — The potential (tendency) of water molecules to move. Pure water has the HIGHEST water potential. Adding solutes LOWERS water potential (because solute particles take up space and reduce the proportion of water molecules free to move). Water always moves from HIGH to LOW water potential via osmosis.
Turgid — A plant cell that has absorbed water by osmosis and swollen. The cell membrane pushes against the cell wall, creating turgor pressure. A turgid cell is firm and rigid — this is how plants stay upright. Animal cells DO NOT become turgid — they have no cell wall, so they BURST (lysis) if placed in too much water.
Flaccid — A plant cell that has lost water by osmosis. The vacuole and cytoplasm shrink, and the cell becomes limp and soft. The cell membrane still touches the cell wall (unlike in plasmolysis). A wilting plant has flaccid cells.
Plasmolysis — Occurs when a plant cell is placed in a very concentrated solution. So much water leaves by osmosis that the cytoplasm and cell membrane pull AWAY from the cell wall. The plant cell is said to be plasmolysed. This is more severe than being flaccid and can kill the cell.
Hypotonic — Solution with LOWER solute concentration (higher water potential) than the cell. Water enters the cell.
Hypertonic — Solution with HIGHER solute concentration (lower water potential) than the cell. Water leaves the cell.
Isotonic — Solution with the SAME concentration as the cell. No net movement of water. Cell volume stays the same.

Phase 4 · Active Transport

⚡ Active Transport

Sometimes cells need to move substances against their concentration gradient — from low to high concentration. This requires energy and protein carriers. It is called active transport.

Definition: Active transport is the movement of particles through a cell membrane from a region of lower concentration to a region of higher concentration (against the concentration gradient), using energy from respiration and carrier proteins in the cell membrane.

⚙️ How Active Transport Works — Tap to Expand

Step 1 — The molecule binds to the carrier protein▼
A molecule from the side with LOWER concentration binds to a specific carrier protein embedded in the cell membrane. Carrier proteins are specific — like a lock and key, they only accept certain molecules. This specificity ensures only the right substances are transported.
📌 Example: glucose molecules in the gut lumen bind to glucose carrier proteins in the epithelial cells of the ileum.
Step 2 — Energy from respiration (ATP) is used▼
ATP (from cellular respiration in mitochondria) provides the energy for the carrier protein to change shape. This is the key difference from diffusion — active transport REQUIRES energy. This is why cells that carry out a lot of active transport have many mitochondria.
📌 Exam link: "Root hair cells have many mitochondria to provide energy for active transport of mineral ions from the soil into the root."
Step 3 — The protein changes shape, releasing the molecule on the other side▼
The carrier protein changes shape using the energy from ATP, forcing the molecule through the membrane from the LOW concentration side to the HIGH concentration side — against the gradient. The protein then resets to its original shape, ready to transport another molecule.
📌 This is analogous to a revolving door that only spins one way and requires a push (energy) to operate.

🌱 Root Hair Cells — Mineral Ion Uptake

The concentration of mineral ions (e.g. nitrates, potassium) is often HIGHER inside root hair cells than in the soil water. Diffusion cannot move ions into the cell against this gradient. Active transport uses energy from respiration to pump mineral ions from the soil (low concentration) into the root (high concentration).

📌 Exam answer: "Root hair cells absorb mineral ions by active transport because the concentration of ions in the cell is already higher than in the soil, so diffusion cannot occur. Energy from respiration is used to move ions against the concentration gradient."

🫀 Ileum Villi — Glucose Uptake

After carbohydrate digestion, glucose concentration in the small intestine can drop very low. Yet the body still needs to absorb glucose into the blood. Active transport allows glucose to be absorbed from the gut even when its concentration in the gut lumen is LOWER than in the blood. Villi epithelial cells are packed with mitochondria for this purpose.

📌 Exam answer: "Active transport allows glucose to be absorbed from the ileum even when its concentration in the gut is lower than in the blood. This requires energy from respiration and carrier proteins in the cell membrane."

🫘 Kidney Tubules — Glucose Reabsorption

All glucose is filtered out of the blood in the kidney's glomerulus. Active transport is used to reabsorb all the glucose back into the blood from the kidney tubules — even when its concentration in the blood is higher — ensuring no glucose is lost in urine. (In diabetes, this mechanism is overwhelmed, so glucose appears in urine.)

📌 Active transport in kidney tubules prevents glucose loss in urine. Diabetic patients who exceed the kidney's reabsorption capacity will have glucose in their urine — a diagnostic indicator.

Phase 5 · Practical Experiments

🧫 IGCSE Practical Experiments

The IGCSE exam regularly includes questions based on standard practical investigations. You must know the method, variables, results, and explanation for each.

🥔 Experiment 1 — Osmosis in Potato Tissue▼
Method: Cut potato cylinders of equal size. Place them in solutions of different sugar concentrations (0%, 5%, 10%, 20%, 40%) and pure water. Measure length/mass before and after (usually 24 hours).

Variables: Independent = concentration of sugar solution. Dependent = change in mass/length. Controlled = size of potato cylinders, temperature, time, species of potato.

Results: In dilute solutions → potato gains mass (water enters by osmosis). In concentrated solutions → potato loses mass (water leaves by osmosis). There is a concentration at which mass does not change (isotonic point — this equals the concentration of the potato's cell sap).

Explanation: The cell sap of potato cells has a certain water potential. In more dilute solutions, water potential outside is higher → water enters → mass increases. In more concentrated solutions, water potential outside is lower → water leaves → mass decreases.
📌 Exam trap: Always measure % change in mass, not absolute change, to account for different starting masses. Graph should show a curve crossing x-axis at the isotonic point.
🔴 Experiment 2 — Diffusion of Potassium Permanganate in Agar▼
Method: Place crystals of potassium permanganate (or another coloured substance) on agar jelly in a Petri dish. Measure how far the colour diffuses over time at different temperatures.

Variables: Independent = temperature (or time). Dependent = distance diffused. Controlled = amount of KMnO₄, concentration, agar thickness.

Results: At higher temperatures, the colour spreads further/faster. At lower temperatures, it spreads more slowly.

Explanation: At higher temperatures, particles have more kinetic energy and move faster, increasing the rate of diffusion. Distance diffused ∝ rate of diffusion.
📌 This experiment demonstrates that temperature increases the rate of diffusion because particles gain kinetic energy. Remember: in agar, there is no bulk flow — movement is purely by diffusion.
🌱 Experiment 3 — Osmosis in Plant Cells (Plasmolysis)▼
Method: Place strips of onion (or Elodea) cells in increasingly concentrated sucrose/salt solutions. Observe under a microscope. Look for plasmolysis (cell membrane pulling away from cell wall).

Variables: Independent = concentration of external solution. Dependent = whether plasmolysis occurs / degree of plasmolysis. Controlled = species of plant, thickness of slice, temperature.

Results: In dilute/distilled water → cells are turgid. In isotonic solution → no visible change. In concentrated solution → plasmolysis — cytoplasm visibly peels away from cell wall.

Explanation: In concentrated solutions, water potential outside is lower than inside the cell. Water leaves by osmosis, the vacuole shrinks, and the cytoplasm pulls away from the cell wall.
📌 Incipient plasmolysis = the point at which the cell membrane just begins to pull away from the cell wall. The concentration at this point equals the concentration of the cell's contents.

📝 IGCSE Past Paper Questions

CIE IGCSE Biology — Diffusion · 3 marks
Define the term 'diffusion'. [3]
Tip: The mark scheme awards one mark each for three key phrases.
Mark Scheme Answer (3 marks):
  • Net movement of particles / molecules / ions [1]
  • From a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration / down a concentration gradient [1]
  • As a result of random movement (of particles) [1]
Note: Do NOT just say "from high to low concentration" — you must say NET MOVEMENT and AS A RESULT OF RANDOM MOVEMENT to get full marks.
CIE IGCSE Biology — Diffusion Rate · 2 marks
A student measures how far a dye diffuses through agar jelly at 20°C and at 40°C. The dye diffuses further at 40°C. Explain why the rate of diffusion is greater at 40°C. [2]
Tip: You need to explain the mechanism — not just state the trend.
Mark Scheme Answer (2 marks):
  • At higher temperature, particles have more kinetic energy [1]
  • So particles move faster / collide more frequently, increasing the rate of diffusion [1]
Do NOT say "heat makes particles move more" — say "kinetic energy" and "move faster".
CIE IGCSE Biology — Osmosis Definition · 3 marks
Define the term 'osmosis'. [3]
Tip: Three separate marks — think about: what moves, which direction, and what structure is involved.
Mark Scheme Answer (3 marks):
  • Net movement of water molecules [1]
  • From a region of higher water potential (dilute solution) to a region of lower water potential (concentrated solution) [1]
  • Through a partially permeable membrane [1]
Critical: you must say WATER MOLECULES (not just particles), and PARTIALLY PERMEABLE MEMBRANE (not semi-permeable). Water potential language is expected at Extended level.
CIE IGCSE Biology — Osmosis in Potato · 3 marks
A student places potato discs in a concentrated sucrose solution. After 30 minutes, the potato discs have decreased in mass. Explain why the mass of the potato discs decreased. [3]
Mark Scheme Answer (3 marks):
  • The sucrose solution has a lower water potential than the potato cell sap [1]
  • Water moves out of the potato cells by osmosis [1]
  • Through the partially permeable cell membrane / from higher water potential (inside cell) to lower water potential (sucrose solution) [1]
Always say OSMOSIS — never just "diffusion" when water movement through a membrane is involved.
CIE IGCSE Biology — Plant Cell Osmosis · 2 marks
Fig. 1 shows a plant cell after being placed in distilled water. State the term used to describe this cell and explain how it reached this state. [2]
Mark Scheme Answer (2 marks):
  • Turgid [1]
  • Water entered the cell by osmosis because the water potential of distilled water (outside) is higher than the water potential of the cell sap (inside), so water moved into the vacuole, pushing the cell membrane against the cell wall [1]
CIE IGCSE Biology — Active Transport · 3 marks
The concentration of nitrate ions in the soil water surrounding plant roots is lower than the concentration of nitrate ions inside the root hair cells. Explain how root hair cells absorb nitrate ions from the soil, and why this process requires energy. [3]
Mark Scheme Answer (3 marks):
  • Nitrate ions are absorbed by active transport [1]
  • Against the concentration gradient (from lower concentration in soil to higher concentration in root) [1]
  • Energy from respiration (ATP) is required to change the shape of carrier proteins in the cell membrane [1]
Key phrases: "active transport", "against the concentration gradient", "energy from respiration". Also acceptable: root hair cells have many mitochondria to provide energy.
CIE IGCSE Biology — Comparing Processes · 2 marks
State two differences between active transport and diffusion. [2]
Mark Scheme Answer (2 marks — any 2 of):
  • Active transport requires energy / ATP; diffusion does not (is passive) [1]
  • Active transport moves substances against the concentration gradient; diffusion moves substances down the concentration gradient [1]
  • Active transport requires carrier proteins; diffusion does not (always) [1]
