The Big Question

How do scientists write numbers too big to say?

Fold a piece of paper in half and it's 2 layers thick. Fold again: 4 layers. Again: 8. If you could fold it 42 times, the stack would be 2⁴² layers thick — tall enough to reach the Moon.

Exponents make numbers grow ridiculously fast. 2¹⁰ is about a thousand. 2²⁰ is about a million. 2³⁰ is about a billion. How can we compare, multiply and divide numbers like these without writing all the zeros?

🧠 Warm-up — true or false?

An exponent tells you how many times to multiply the base by itself. Every rule today comes from that one idea.

⚡ Base & Exponent ➖ Negative Exponent 🟧 Square Root 🧊 Cube Root 🔭 Scientific Notation
Base & Exponent — in aⁿ, a is the base and n is the exponent: multiply a by itself n times.

5³ = 5 × 5 × 5 = 125
Negative Exponent — a⁻ⁿ means 1 ÷ aⁿ. A negative exponent makes a reciprocal, not a negative number.

2⁻³ = 1/2³ = 1/8
Square Root — √p is the side length of a square with area p. The equation x² = p has two solutions: ±√p.

x² = 49 → x = 7 or x = −7
Cube Root — ∛p is the edge of a cube with volume p. The equation x³ = p has exactly one solution.

x³ = 64 → x = 4
Scientific Notation — a number written as a × 10ⁿ, where 1 ≤ a < 10 and n is an integer.

93,000,000 = 9.3 × 10⁷ · 0.00042 = 4.2 × 10⁻⁴

6
Real-World Connections

Powers of ten in the real world 🌍

Scientists, engineers and programmers use exponents every day.

💾

Computer Memory

Storage doubles in powers of 2: 2¹⁰ bytes ≈ 1 kilobyte, 2³⁰ ≈ 1 gigabyte.

🦠

Biology

A bacterium is about 2 × 10⁻⁶ m long. Bacteria that double every 20 minutes grow by powers of 2.

🚀

Space

Light travels 3 × 10⁸ m every second. A light-year is about 9.46 × 10¹⁵ m.

🌎

Population

About 8.1 × 10⁹ people live on Earth. That's 8,100,000,000.
