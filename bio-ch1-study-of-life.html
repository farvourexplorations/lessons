Phase 1 · Wonder

🌍 What Makes Something Alive?

Before we define biology or learn the scientific method, let's start with the hardest question in the subject.

A crystal grows. A fire consumes energy and produces waste. A river flows and changes its surroundings. Are any of these things alive? How do you know? What's the actual difference between living and non-living?

✏️ Your Thinking — Before We Begin

Here's the kicker: biologists have argued about this for centuries. Viruses have DNA, evolve, and reproduce — but only inside a host cell. Most biologists don't call them alive. By the end of this lesson, you'll be able to say exactly why.

Chapter 1 of OpenStax Biology 2e covers three things: (1) what science is and how it works, (2) the eight properties shared by all living things, and (3) how life is organized — from atoms all the way up to the biosphere. These aren't just facts to memorize — they're the lens through which every other AP Biology topic is understood.

Phase 2 · The Science of Biology

📚 What Is Science — And What Isn't?

Science isn't a collection of facts. It's a process for answering questions about the natural world. Understanding that process is itself an AP exam topic.

"Science attempts to describe and understand the nature of the universe by rational means — using observation, evidence, and logical reasoning. It cannot address supernatural explanations."

📊 Inductive Reasoning

Uses specific observations to build a general conclusion. You observe many instances, then infer a rule. Also called "discovery science" — it's how scientists first notice patterns in nature.

📌 Example: You observe that every crow you've ever seen is black. You conclude: "All crows are black." (This is inductive — one white crow would disprove it.)
📌 AP Exam link: "A person notices houseplants exposed to music grow faster and concludes plants grow better with music." → Inductive reasoning

🔭 Deductive Reasoning

Starts with a general principle and predicts a specific result. "If… then…" statements are deductive. This is the logic behind forming and testing hypotheses.

📌 Example: "All living things are made of cells. Bacteria are living things. Therefore, bacteria are made of cells." → Deductive.
📌 Key distinction: Discovery science is inductive. Hypothesis-based science is deductive.

🎓 Basic Science

The goal is to expand knowledge — with no expectation of immediate practical use. Basic science asks "how does this work?" purely out of curiosity and intellectual interest.

📌 Example: Studying the genome of a deep-sea organism no one has ever seen before. No practical application expected — just discovery.

🏥 Applied Science (Technology)

The goal is to solve practical problems. Applied science uses findings from basic research to develop medicine, technology, or solutions to real-world challenges.

📌 Example: Using knowledge of bacterial genetics (basic science) to develop an antibiotic (applied science / technology).

📋 Key Vocabulary — Tap Each Term

Hypothesis Theory Variable Control Group Peer Review Scientific Law
Hypothesis — A testable, falsifiable proposed explanation for an observation. A good hypothesis must be possible to prove wrong. It is NOT a guess — it must be based on prior observation and be testable by experiment.
Theory — A well-tested, widely accepted explanation supported by extensive evidence. In science, "theory" does NOT mean "guess" — it means the most rigorously supported explanation available. Evolution is a theory. Gravity is a theory.
Variable — A factor that can change in an experiment. The independent variable is what the scientist manipulates. The dependent variable is what is measured. Controlled variables are kept constant.
Control Group — The group in an experiment that does NOT receive the experimental treatment. It provides a baseline for comparison. Without a control, you can't know if your results were caused by your manipulation or something else.
Peer Review — The process by which other scientists in the field evaluate a study before it is published. Peer review catches errors, ensures methods are sound, and is the cornerstone of scientific integrity. Scientists publish in peer-reviewed journals.
Scientific Law — Describes what happens under certain conditions but does NOT explain why. A law is a pattern. A theory is an explanation. Example: The Law of Thermodynamics describes energy behavior; theories explain the molecular mechanisms.

Phase 3 · The Scientific Method

🔬 The Scientific Method — Step by Step

The scientific method isn't a rigid checklist — it's a flexible, logical process for answering questions about the natural world. Click each step to expand it.

1
Observation
Notice something in the natural world that sparks a question. This can be a direct observation or data from prior research. Example: Your coffee maker doesn't work in the morning.
2
Question
Formulate a specific, answerable question based on the observation. Example: "Why won't the coffee maker turn on?"
3
Hypothesis (Prediction)
Form a testable, falsifiable "if… then…" prediction. Example: "If the coffee maker is unplugged, then plugging it in will make it work." This uses deductive reasoning.
4
Experiment / Test
Design a controlled experiment to test the hypothesis. Include an experimental group (receives the manipulation) and a control group (does not). Identify your independent and dependent variables.
5
Data Collection & Analysis
Collect quantitative or qualitative data. Use statistics and visualizations to analyze patterns. Good data collection is repeatable and objective.
6
Conclusion
Does the data support or refute the hypothesis? If not supported, revise the hypothesis and test again. A hypothesis can never be "proven" — only supported or falsified. Communicate results in a peer-reviewed paper.

🧪 Apply It: The Plant and Music Experiment

The OpenStax textbook uses the coffee maker example throughout Chapter 1 to illustrate the scientific method. The maker doesn't work → is it unplugged? → test it → it works when plugged in → original hypothesis was correct. Simple scenario, exact same logic as the most complex biology experiment.

🔩 Order

Living things are highly organized and structured. From the arrangement of atoms in a molecule to the organization of organs in a body — life maintains complex, non-random structure. Non-living things like crystals can be ordered, but lack the other seven properties.

📌 A cell membrane's precise phospholipid bilayer arrangement. A DNA double helix. An enzyme's specific 3D fold.

👁️ Sensitivity / Response to Stimuli

Living organisms detect and respond to changes in their internal or external environment. This includes everything from a bacterium swimming toward a sugar gradient to a human flinching from heat.

📌 A plant turning toward light (phototropism). A white blood cell moving toward a pathogen. Your pupils dilating in dim light.

👶 Reproduction

All living organisms reproduce — either sexually or asexually — passing genetic information to offspring. This is how the information of life is preserved across generations. A fire "reproduces" by spreading, but without genetic information.

📌 Bacteria dividing by binary fission. Humans reproducing sexually. A fern releasing spores.

📈 Growth and Development

Organisms grow and develop following genetically controlled instructions. A human embryo follows a precise developmental program encoded in DNA — not random expansion like a crystal growing.

📌 A caterpillar metamorphosing into a butterfly. A seed developing into a tree. An embryo undergoing cell division and differentiation.

⚙️ Regulation

Living organisms regulate their internal environment through complex feedback mechanisms. This includes controlling gene expression, enzyme activity, hormone levels, and cell cycle progression.

📌 Insulin and glucagon regulating blood sugar. The cell cycle checkpoints preventing uncontrolled division. Feedback inhibition in enzyme pathways.

🌡️ Homeostasis

A subset of regulation — the maintenance of a stable internal environment despite external changes. Temperature, pH, osmotic balance, and ion concentrations are all regulated to stay within narrow ranges compatible with life.

📌 Sweating to cool down. Kidneys regulating blood osmolarity. Buffers maintaining blood pH between 7.35–7.45.

⚡ Energy Processing

All living organisms take in energy, convert it, and use it to do work. Autotrophs capture light energy (photosynthesis); heterotrophs consume organic molecules (cellular respiration). Without energy processing, all other properties fail.

📌 Plants converting sunlight → glucose. Animals breaking down glucose → ATP. ATP powering muscle contraction, active transport, biosynthesis.

🦋 Evolutionary Adaptation

Over generations, populations of living organisms change in response to environmental pressures through natural selection. This is what drives the diversity of life on Earth. It is listed as a property of life because all life — by definition — has an evolutionary history.

📌 Antibiotic resistance in bacteria. Camouflage in moths. The human immune system's ability to learn from past infections.

So — why isn't a virus alive? Viruses have genetic material, evolve, and can reproduce — but only inside a host cell. They have no cellular structure, cannot process energy independently, and cannot maintain homeostasis on their own. They fail properties 2, 3 (independently), 5, 6, and 7.

🌳 The Diversity of Life & Phylogenetics

There are an estimated 8.7 million species on Earth. Biology organizes this incredible diversity using evolutionary relationships. The AP exam tests three domains and how to read a phylogenetic tree.

🦠 Bacteria

Prokaryotic cells — no membrane-bound nucleus. Single-celled. Incredibly diverse metabolically. Some perform photosynthesis, some are decomposers, some are pathogens, many are essential to ecosystems. Most ancient domain along with Archaea.

📌 E. coli, Streptococcus, Cyanobacteria (perform photosynthesis), Lactobacillus (in yogurt)

🌋 Archaea

Also prokaryotic, but genetically and biochemically distinct from Bacteria. Often found in extreme environments (extremophiles) — hot springs, deep-sea vents, highly salty lakes. Molecular evidence shows Archaea are more closely related to Eukarya than to Bacteria.

📌 Methanogens (produce methane in anaerobic environments), Halophiles (thrive in salt flats), Thermophiles (hot springs)

🧬 Eukarya

Eukaryotic cells — have a membrane-bound nucleus and membrane-bound organelles. Includes the four kingdoms: Protista, Fungi, Plantae, and Animalia. The endosymbiotic theory explains how eukaryotes evolved from ancient prokaryotic ancestors.

📌 Kingdoms: Protista (amoeba, algae), Fungi (mushrooms, yeast), Plantae (plants), Animalia (animals including us)

💭 Reflection Questions

Write your own answers — in your own words. This is the single most effective study technique for AP Biology.

1. Explain the difference between inductive and deductive reasoning. Give one original example of each (not from the textbook).
2. List all 8 properties of life. For each one, briefly explain why it's necessary — what would happen if a living thing lacked that property?
