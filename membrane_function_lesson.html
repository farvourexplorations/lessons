Getting substances across

Passive vs. active transport

The membrane is selectively permeable — it lets some things through and blocks others. Watch what happens with no energy spent, versus with energy spent.

No energy needed 被动运输

Diffusion is molecules spreading out from where there are many of them to where there are few — like a drop of dye spreading through water. It happens on its own, with no energy needed.

Energy required 主动运输

Active transport moves molecules the "wrong" way — from low to high concentration — like pushing water uphill. This always costs the cell ATP energy.

Real evidence

Osmosis you can actually see

This is a real photo of onion cells placed in a strong salt solution (hypertonic). Water left the cells by osmosis, so each cell's contents shrank away from its rigid cell wall — a process called plasmolysis.

Onion cells in a hypertonic (salty) solution — notice the shrunken contents pulling away from the cell wall.

A real-world use: the IV drip

This is exactly why hospitals give patients an isotonic saline solution through an IV drip, and never pure water. Pure water is hypotonic to blood cells — it would rush into the cells by osmosis and make them swell and burst. An isotonic solution keeps blood cells perfectly safe.

Same idea, real medicine 💉
A helping hand

Facilitated diffusion

Some molecules (like sugar) are too big or too charged to slip through the membrane's oily core alone. They still move passively — but need a transport protein to help them across.

Still passive — still free

Even with a protein's help, facilitated diffusion is still passive transport: molecules still move from high to low concentration, and the cell spends no ATP. The protein is just a doorway, not a pump.

Compare this to active transport (with its ATP-powered pump) from earlier — same membrane, two very different strategies!

Moving the big stuff

Swallowing and spitting out

Some cargo is too big to pass through the membrane at all — even with a protein's help. Instead, the membrane itself moves.

Bringing material in 胞吞作用

The cell membrane wraps around material outside the cell and pinches off inward, forming a vesicle. This is how some white blood cells "eat" bacteria.

Sending material out 胞吐作用

A vesicle from inside the cell (remember the endomembrane system?) moves to the membrane, fuses with it, and releases its contents outside. This is how cells release hormones and other signals.

Challenge

Mei and Tom's fish tank puzzle

Mei (美) and Tom have a freshwater fish. Mei suggests adding ocean salt water "to make it healthier."

"Adding salt water can't hurt — water is water," says Mei.
"No, Mei — that would badly harm the fish's cells," says Tom.

Who is correct?

Tom is correct. A freshwater fish's cells are adapted to a hypotonic environment. Suddenly adding salt water would make the outside hypertonic to the fish's cells — water would rush out of its cells by osmosis, causing them to shrink and stop working properly. Tonicity really can be a matter of life and death for a cell!
Remember

Water always follows the solute concentration. Sudden changes in tonicity can be deadly for a cell that isn't adapted to them.
