Functions & Requirements of Life

What actually makes something "alive," and what does the human body absolutely need to stay that way? These two questions set up everything you'll study about every organ system this semester.

Phase 1 · Wonder

⚡ What Actually Makes You "Alive"?

A cancer cell, a bacterium, and you are all technically "alive" — but a rock, a virus, and a corpse are not. What's the dividing line?

Cancer cells are sometimes described as "generic" cells that have lost their specialized identity and just keep dividing uncontrollably. If a cell can grow, use energy, and reproduce, but has lost its specialized identity and stopped responding normally to its surroundings — is it still doing everything "life" requires?

✏️ Your Prediction (before we dig in)

Physiologists boil it down to six defining functions: organization, metabolism, responsiveness, movement, development, and reproduction. Miss enough of these and you're not describing a living organism anymore. And keeping all six running requires the body to constantly meet a short list of non-negotiable requirements — oxygen, nutrients, a narrow temperature range, and the right atmospheric pressure.

Phase 2 · Functions of Human Life

🧬 The Six Functions That Define Life

Tap each function to see how it plays out in the human body.

Organization Metabolism Responsiveness Movement Development, Growth & Differentiation Reproduction
Organization — Trillions of cells are organized to maintain distinct internal compartments, separating body fluids from the outside world and from each other. Cell membranes separate a cell's interior from its exterior; blood vessels keep blood in a closed system; internal membranes separate organs like the lungs, heart, and kidneys from one another. The integumentary system (skin and its structures) is the body's largest organizational barrier against the outside world.
Metabolism — The sum of all chemical reactions that occur in the body. It has two directions: anabolism builds larger, complex molecules from smaller ones (uses energy), and catabolism breaks larger molecules down into simpler ones (releases energy). Together they keep the cell's ATP energy currency flowing.
Responsiveness — The ability to adjust to changes in the internal or external environment. Moving toward food and water, moving away from danger, and sweating in response to rising internal temperature are all responsiveness in action.
Movement — Not just walking or waving — movement also includes blood cells moving through vessels, muscle cells contracting to maintain posture, glands secreting chemicals, and your diaphragm moving air in and out of your lungs.
Development, Growth & Differentiation — Development is all the changes a body goes through across a lifetime. Differentiation is unspecialized cells becoming specialized in structure and function. Growth is an increase in body size, achieved by increasing the number of cells, the amount of material around cells (like bone mineral), and — within narrow limits — the size of existing cells.
Reproduction — The formation of a new organism from parent organisms, carried out in humans by the male and female reproductive systems. Since every complex organism eventually dies, reproduction is what keeps the line of organisms going.

🧱 Anabolism

The process by which smaller, simpler molecules are combined into larger, more complex substances. Anabolism uses energy — your body assembles the complex chemicals it needs by combining small molecules derived from the foods you eat, including the synthesis of ATP itself.

📌 Example: muscle cells assembling new proteins for growth and repair.

💥 Catabolism

The process by which larger, more complex substances are broken down into smaller, simpler molecules. Catabolism releases energy — the complex molecules in food are broken down so the body can use their parts, and stored ATP is broken down to release a controlled amount of energy for cellular work.

📌 Example: breaking down glucose to power a muscle contraction.

💨 Oxygen

Atmospheric air is only about 20% oxygen, but it's essential to the chemical reactions that keep the body alive, including ATP production. Brain cells are especially sensitive to oxygen loss because of their constant, high demand for ATP.

📌 Brain damage is likely within 5 minutes without oxygen; death is likely within 10 minutes.

🍎 Nutrients

A nutrient is any substance essential to survival. Water is the most critical — it's the medium in which all chemical reactions of life take place and makes up roughly 70% of adult body mass. Macronutrients (carbohydrates, lipids, and proteins) supply energy and the building blocks of the body itself. Micronutrients (vitamins and minerals) participate in essential reactions like nerve impulses; some, like calcium, also contribute to body structure.

📌 You can survive only days without water, but weeks without energy-yielding nutrients.

🌡️ Narrow Range of Temperature

The chemical reactions the body depends on can only occur within a narrow range around 37°C (98.6°F). When body temperature rises or drops too far, enzymes (proteins that facilitate reactions) lose their normal structure and stop functioning, halting metabolism. Short-term, the body compensates: sweating cools it down, shivering and increased fuel consumption warm it up.

📌 Severe cold reduces blood flow to the extremities to protect the core — but can cause frostbite in fingers and toes.

🎈 Narrow Range of Atmospheric Pressure

Atmospheric pressure constantly presses on the body and keeps gases like nitrogen dissolved in body fluids. It's also required for breathing — taking in oxygen and releasing carbon dioxide. At high altitude, lower atmospheric pressure reduces gas exchange, causing shortness of breath, confusion, and headache (altitude sickness).

📌 A sudden drop in pressure can cause dissolved nitrogen gas to form bubbles in the blood — this is decompression sickness ("the bends").

🤿 Decompression Sickness ("The Bends")

When a diver surfaces too quickly, the rapid drop in pressure causes dissolved nitrogen gas in the blood to come out of solution and form bubbles — the same effect you see when you open a carbonated drink. Symptoms include joint pain, headache, and vision problems; severe untreated cases can be fatal. Treatment involves pure oxygen and a hyperbaric chamber, which repressurizes the body so pressure can be released more gradually.

❄️ Controlled Hypothermia

Sometimes clinicians deliberately override the body's temperature-regulation system. During open-heart surgery, controlled hypothermia cools the body to 25–32°C, reducing the metabolic needs of the brain, heart, and other organs and lowering the risk of damage while blood flow is interrupted. Some emergency physicians also use induced hypothermia after cardiac arrest to slow metabolism and reduce the heart's workload during recovery.
