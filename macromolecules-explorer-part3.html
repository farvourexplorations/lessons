Phase 1 · Wonder

🧬 The Molecule That Remembers Everything

Every instruction for building you — eye color, height, how your cells repair a paper cut — is written in a molecule just 2 nanometers wide. If unwound, the DNA in just one of your cells would stretch about 2 meters long. You have trillions of cells.

DNA only uses a four-letter alphabet (A, T, C, G), yet it encodes the instructions to build an entire human being — and every other organism on Earth. How can a molecule built from just four repeating letters store such an enormous, precise amount of information?

✏️ Your Prediction (before we dig in)

The answer is sequence and length. Just like 26 letters can form millions of unique words and sentences, the order and arrangement of millions (or billions) of A, T, C, and G nucleotides can encode an almost unlimited amount of information. DNA also has a second superpower: its double-helix, complementary structure means it can be precisely copied — which is exactly how your cells pass identical genetic information to every new cell they create.

🗝️ The Big Picture

Nucleic acids are the fourth class of macromolecule, built from nucleotide monomers. There are two types: DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid — stores genetic information as a double helix) and RNA (ribonucleic acid — mostly single-stranded, involved in protein synthesis). Together, they carry out the Central Dogma of Life: DNA → RNA → Protein.

Phase 2 · 3.5 Nucleic Acids — Building Blocks

📖 Nucleotides — Core Vocabulary

Tap each term to reveal its definition. Every nucleic acid — DNA or RNA — is built from the exact same three-part monomer.

Nucleotide (3 Parts) Pentose Sugar Nitrogenous Base Purines vs. Pyrimidines Phosphodiester Bond Genome & Chromatin 5' & 3' Directionality
Nucleotide — The Monomer of Nucleic Acids — Every nucleotide has exactly three components: (1) a pentose (5-carbon) sugar, (2) a nitrogenous base, and (3) one or more phosphate groups. The base attaches to the 1′ carbon of the sugar; the phosphate attaches to the 5′ carbon. Nucleotides link together to form a polynucleotide (DNA or RNA).
Pentose Sugar — The 5-carbon sugar backbone of a nucleotide. In DNA, the sugar is deoxyribose; in RNA, it's ribose. The only structural difference: deoxyribose has an H at the 2′ carbon position, while ribose has an OH group there. This single oxygen atom difference is where "deoxy" (without oxygen) gets its name.
Nitrogenous Bases — Organic, ring-shaped molecules containing carbon and nitrogen. There are 5 total: Adenine (A), Guanine (G), Cytosine (C), Thymine (T), and Uracil (U). DNA contains A, T, G, C. RNA contains A, U, G, C (uracil replaces thymine).
Purines vs. Pyrimidines — Purines (Adenine, Guanine) have a double-ring structure (two fused carbon-nitrogen rings). Pyrimidines (Cytosine, Thymine, Uracil) have a single-ring structure. In base pairing, a purine always pairs with a pyrimidine — this keeps the double helix a consistent width all the way down its length.
Phosphodiester Bond — The covalent bond linking nucleotides together into a polynucleotide chain. It forms between the 5′ phosphate of one nucleotide and the 3′ hydroxyl group of the next nucleotide's sugar. Important AP distinction: this bond does NOT form via simple dehydration synthesis like other macromolecule linkages — its formation involves the removal of two phosphate groups, not just one water molecule.
Genome & Chromatin — The entire genetic content of a cell is its genome (the study of genomes = genomics). In eukaryotic cells, DNA wraps around histone proteins to form chromatin — the substance that makes up chromosomes. A single chromosome may contain tens of thousands of genes. Prokaryotic DNA is NOT enclosed in a membrane-bound nucleus and does not form chromatin the same way.
5′ and 3′ Directionality — The carbons of the pentose sugar are numbered 1′ through 5′. Each strand of a nucleic acid has direction: a free phosphate at the 5′ end and a free hydroxyl at the 3′ end. In double-stranded DNA, the two strands run antiparallel — one strand runs 5′→3′ while its partner runs 3′→5′ in the opposite direction. This directionality is essential for DNA replication, transcription, and translation.

🧪 Practice: The Base Complementary Rule

In DNA: A pairs with T (2 hydrogen bonds), and G pairs with C (3 hydrogen bonds). In RNA, U replaces T, so A pairs with U.

🧬 DNA's Double-Helix Structure

DNA's sugar-phosphate backbone forms the outside of the helix; nitrogenous bases stack in the interior like the steps of a staircase, held by hydrogen bonds. Each base pair is separated from the next by 0.34 nm. The two strands run in opposite directions — antiparallel — meaning the 5′ end of one strand faces the 3′ end of the other. Adenine pairs with Thymine; Guanine pairs with Cytosine (the base complementary rule).

📌 If one strand reads 5' AATTGGCC 3', its complementary strand reads 3' TTAACCGG 5' — written 5'→3' that's 5' GGCCAATT 3'.

➰ RNA's Single-Stranded Structure

RNA is usually single-stranded (unlike DNA's double helix), made of ribonucleotides linked by phosphodiester bonds. Even though it's single-stranded, RNA often folds back on itself, forming extensive intramolecular base pairing between complementary sequences within the same strand — creating predictable 3D shapes essential for its function (especially in tRNA and rRNA).

📌 Some viruses contain double-stranded RNA, but this does NOT occur in plants or animals — their RNA is single-stranded.

📨 Messenger RNA (mRNA)

Carries the genetic "message" copied from DNA. It's synthesized in the nucleus (in eukaryotes), then travels to the cytoplasm where it interacts with ribosomes. The mRNA sequence is complementary to its DNA template, with U replacing T. The mRNA is read in sets of three bases (codons) — each codon specifies one amino acid.

📌 If a DNA template reads AATTGCGC, the complementary mRNA reads UUAACGCG.

🏭 Ribosomal RNA (rRNA)

A major structural constituent of ribosomes — the molecular machines where mRNA binds for translation. rRNA ensures the proper alignment of mRNA within the ribosome. Critically, rRNA also has enzymatic activity (called peptidyl transferase activity) — it directly catalyzes the formation of peptide bonds between amino acids during protein synthesis. This makes rRNA a ribozyme — an RNA that acts as an enzyme.

📌 A ribosome has two parts: a large subunit and a small subunit, both built largely from rRNA + protein, with the mRNA sandwiched between them.

🚚 Transfer RNA (tRNA)

One of the smallest RNA types (usually 70–90 nucleotides long). Its job: carry the correct amino acid to the ribosome during protein synthesis. Base pairing between the tRNA's anticodon and the mRNA's codon is what ensures the correct amino acid is added to the growing polypeptide chain at each step.

📌 Despite being single-stranded, tRNA folds into a complex 3D "cloverleaf" shape due to extensive intramolecular base pairing.

🔇 microRNA (miRNA)

The smallest RNA molecules. Their role is gene regulation — they interfere with the expression of specific mRNA messages, effectively turning genes "down" or "off" after transcription has already occurred. This is a more recently understood layer of control over which genes actually get expressed as proteins.

📌 miRNA isn't involved in building proteins directly — it's a regulator that controls whether other mRNAs get used.

The flow of genetic information — DNA → RNA → Protein — is called the Central Dogma of Life. DNA dictates the structure of mRNA through transcription; RNA then dictates the structure of a protein through translation. This holds true for virtually all organisms (with some exceptions in viral infections)!
