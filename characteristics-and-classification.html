Phase 1 · Wonder

🌱 What Makes Something "Alive"?

A candle flame flickers, gives off heat, and grows taller as it burns. A crystal in a cave grows bigger every year. A robot vacuum moves around a room and changes direction when it hits a wall. None of these are alive — but why not? Biologists needed a strict checklist to decide.

Look at the three examples above. Each one does something that sounds like a "characteristic of life" — moving, growing, responding. Yet none of them are living organisms. What do you think is missing? What would you need to check before you could say something is truly alive?

✏️ Your Thinking — Before We Begin

Biologists use a checklist of seven characteristics — remembered by the acronym MRS GREN — and something is only classed as a living organism if it can, at some point, carry out ALL SEVEN. We'll also see how once something IS alive, we can name it and sort it into groups using classification systems.

Phase 2 · 1.1 Characteristics of Living Organisms

🧬 MRS GREN — The Seven Characteristics of Life

IGCSE examiners want the exact wording of each definition, not just a rough idea. Tap each letter below to reveal the precise syllabus definition, then check the exam traps underneath.

MRS GREN = Movement, Respiration, Sensitivity, Growth, Reproduction, Excretion, Nutrition. An organism must be capable of all seven to be classed as a living thing.

🔑 Tap Each Letter for the Exact Definition

M — Movement R — Respiration S — Sensitivity G — Growth R — Reproduction E — Excretion N — Nutrition
Movement — An action by an organism, or part of an organism, causing a change of position or place. Exam note: this does NOT have to be the whole organism relocating — a plant turning its leaves towards light still counts as movement.
Respiration — The chemical reactions in cells that break down nutrient molecules and release energy for metabolism. Exam trap: respiration is NOT the same as breathing (ventilation) or gas exchange — it is a set of chemical reactions happening inside cells.
Sensitivity — The ability to detect and respond to changes in the internal or external environment. Both parts matter: DETECTING a stimulus and RESPONDING to it.
Growth — A permanent increase in size and dry mass. Exam trap: it must be DRY mass, not wet mass — an organism's wet mass can rise and fall daily just from drinking water, but that isn't true growth.
Reproduction — The processes that make more of the same kind of organism. Can be sexual (two parents, offspring vary) or asexual (one parent, genetically identical offspring).
Excretion — The removal of the waste products of metabolism (chemical reactions inside cells) and substances in excess of requirements. Exam trap: this is DIFFERENT from egestion (removing undigested food/faeces) — undigested food was never part of the body's cells, so getting rid of it is not excretion.
Nutrition — The taking in of materials for energy, growth and development. Includes taking in nutrients (animals, by feeding) or making them internally (plants, by photosynthesis).

Phase 3 · 1.2 Concept and Uses of Classification Systems

🔑 Naming and Grouping Living Things

Once we know something is alive, scientists need a shared system to name it and to sort it into groups with similar organisms. This is classification.

Classification is the process of organising living organisms into groups based on the features they share. This makes it easier to study, identify, and communicate about them.

🧠 Key Terms — Tap Each

Species Binomial System Dichotomous Key Evolutionary Relationships DNA Base Sequences
Species — A group of organisms that can reproduce to produce fertile offspring. Exam trap: a horse and a donkey CAN mate and produce a mule, but the mule is infertile — so horses and donkeys are classed as different species.
Binomial System — An internationally agreed system in which the scientific name of an organism is made up of two parts, showing the genus and the species, e.g. Homo sapiens. Rules: the GENUS starts with a capital letter, the SPECIES is all lowercase, and both parts are italicised (or underlined separately if handwritten).
Dichotomous Key — A series of questions or statements, each with two possible answers (usually yes/no), used to identify an organism based on observable features. Each question splits the remaining organisms into two smaller groups until only one organism is left.
Supplement — Classification systems aim to reflect evolutionary relationships: organisms are grouped so that the classification shows how closely related they are through shared ancestry, not just superficial similarity.
Supplement — Sequences of bases in DNA are used as a means of classification. Groups of organisms that share a more recent common ancestor (more closely related) have DNA base sequences that are MORE similar than those that share only a distant common ancestor.

Phase 4 · 1.3 Features of Organisms

🐾 Kingdoms and Major Groups

All living organisms can be placed into one of five kingdoms, and within the animal and plant kingdoms there are further groups defined by shared features.

🐘 Animal Kingdom

Multicellular organisms with no cell wall and no chlorophyll. They gain nutrition by ingesting and digesting food (heterotrophic nutrition), and most can move the whole body and have nervous coordination.

📌 Examples: mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians, fish, insects, spiders.

🌿 Plant Kingdom

Multicellular organisms with a cell wall made of cellulose. They contain chlorophyll and make their own food by photosynthesis (autotrophic nutrition).

📌 Examples: ferns and flowering plants (dicotyledons and monocotyledons).

🍄 Fungus Kingdom

Organisms with a cell wall made of chitin (not cellulose). They cannot photosynthesise. Most feed saprotrophically — they release enzymes onto their food to digest it externally, then absorb the soluble products (extracellular digestion and absorption). The body is often a network of thread-like hyphae called a mycelium.

📌 Examples: mushrooms, moulds, yeast.

🦠 Prokaryote Kingdom

Unicellular organisms with no nucleus — their DNA is a single circular strand lying free in the cytoplasm. They have no membrane-bound organelles (no mitochondria, no chloroplasts) and very small ribosomes.

📌 Examples: bacteria.

🔬 Protoctist Kingdom

Simple organisms, mostly single-celled, but — unlike prokaryotes — they DO have a nucleus. A very varied group that doesn't fit neatly into the other kingdoms.

📌 Examples: Amoeba, Chlorella, Plasmodium (the malaria parasite).

Supplement — Viruses are not placed into any of the five kingdoms because they are non-cellular: they are not made of cells. A virus consists of genetic material (DNA or RNA) surrounded by a protein coat, and can only reproduce inside a living host cell using the host's own machinery. Examples: influenza, HIV, tobacco mosaic virus.

📝 IGCSE Past Paper Questions

These questions follow the style and content of CIE IGCSE Biology past papers (0610). Write your answers, then click Check All Answers to reveal the mark-scheme level answers.

CIE IGCSE Biology — Species · 2 marks
Define the term 'species'. [2]
Tip: Two separate marking points — what the organisms form, and what they can produce together.
Mark Scheme Answer (2 marks):
  • A group of organisms [1]
  • That can reproduce to produce fertile offspring [1]
Note: "fertile" is essential — organisms that can mate but only produce infertile offspring (e.g. horse × donkey = mule) are different species.
CIE IGCSE Biology — Binomial System · 2 marks
State two rules for writing a binomial name correctly, using the example Panthera leo (the lion). [2]
Tip: Think about capitalisation and formatting.
Mark Scheme Answer (2 marks):
  • The genus name (Panthera) starts with a capital letter, and the species name (leo) is all lowercase [1]
  • Both parts are italicised, or underlined separately if handwritten [1]
CIE IGCSE Biology — Kingdoms · 2 marks
A mushroom does not photosynthesise. It has a cell wall made of chitin and feeds by absorbing nutrients from dead organic matter. State which kingdom the mushroom belongs to and give ONE feature that supports your answer. [2]
Tip: Which kingdom has a chitin cell wall?
Mark Scheme Answer (2 marks):
  • Fungus (kingdom) [1]
  • Supporting feature: cell wall made of chitin, OR feeds by saprotrophic nutrition (extracellular digestion and absorption) [1]
CIE IGCSE Biology — Excretion vs Egestion · 2 marks
Explain the difference between excretion and egestion. [2]
Tip: One term relates to metabolism inside cells; the other relates to undigested food.
Mark Scheme Answer (2 marks):
  • Excretion is the removal of waste products of metabolism (or substances in excess of requirements) made inside the body's own cells [1]
  • Egestion is the removal of undigested food (faeces) that was never absorbed into the body's cells [1]
CIE IGCSE Biology — Dichotomous Keys · 2 marks
Four organisms, W, X, Y and Z, are being classified. W and X have a backbone; Y and Z do not. Write a suitable first question for a dichotomous key that would separate these two groups. [2]
Tip: The question must have exactly two possible answers and must split the four organisms into two groups.
Mark Scheme Answer (2 marks):
  • A question based on an observable feature with two answers, e.g. "Does the organism have a backbone?" [1]
  • Yes → W, X / No → Y, Z (the question must correctly divide the group into two) [1]
CIE IGCSE Biology — Arthropod Groups · 2 marks
State two features that distinguish insects from arachnids. [2]
Tip: Compare leg number and number of body parts.
Mark Scheme Answer (2 marks — any 2 of):
  • Insects have 6 legs (3 pairs); arachnids have 8 legs (4 pairs) [1]
  • Insect body is in 3 parts (head, thorax, abdomen); arachnid body is in 2 parts (cephalothorax, abdomen) [1]
  • Insects usually have antennae and may have wings; arachnids have neither [1]
CIE IGCSE Biology — DNA Classification · 2 marks
Explain why classification systems based on DNA base sequences are considered more reliable than classification based only on physical (observable) features. [2]
Tip: Think about what DNA similarity actually shows about ancestry.
Mark Scheme Answer (2 marks):
  • DNA base sequences directly reflect evolutionary relationships / how closely related organisms are [1]
  • Organisms with a more recent common ancestor have more similar DNA base sequences than those with only a distant common ancestor, whereas physical features can appear similar without close relation [1]
