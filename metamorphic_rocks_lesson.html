No melting required

Squeezed, not melted

A metamorphic rock starts life as an entirely different rock — igneous, sedimentary, or even another metamorphic rock. Buried deep underground, it is squeezed by huge pressure and baked by intense heat. This changes its minerals and structure completely, but — unlike in the formation of igneous rock — the rock never fully melts.

Folded, banded gneiss — the swirling pattern records the immense heat and pressure this rock survived deep in the crust.

Existing rock + heat + pressure = new rock

The word "metamorphic" comes from Greek words meaning "changed form". The minerals inside the original rock recrystallise into new patterns without the rock ever becoming liquid. This is what separates metamorphic rocks from igneous rocks, which form by cooling from a molten state.

What do metamorphic rocks look like?

Hard, and often banded

Because their minerals have recrystallised under such intense heat and pressure, metamorphic rocks are usually very hard and tough. Many also show banding — stripes of light and dark minerals that have been squeezed and lined up in the same direction, a little like a stack of books pushed sideways.

Gneiss — strong banding formed as pressure lined up its minerals into stripes.
Marble — hard and crystalline, but with little or no banding.

Not every metamorphic rock is banded — it depends on the minerals in the original rock and how much pressure it experienced. But almost all metamorphic rocks share one property: they are very hard, because their crystals have been packed tightly together.

Before the matching activity

Four famous transformations

The same story — burial, heat and pressure — plays out differently depending on the starting rock. Explore each pair below to see exactly what changes.

Shale Sedimentary
➜Low–moderate heat & pressure
Slate Metamorphic

Shale is a soft rock made of tightly packed clay and mud particles. When it is buried and squeezed, the tiny flat clay minerals inside it recrystallise and line up at right angles to the squeezing force. This gives slate slaty cleavage — the ability to split cleanly into thin, flat sheets — and makes it much harder than the shale it came from.

Sandstone Sedimentary
➜High heat & pressure
Quartzite Metamorphic

Sandstone is made of separate sand grains held together by cement. Intense heat and pressure make the quartz grains and the cement between them recrystallise into one continuous, interlocking mass of quartz crystals. The individual grain boundaries disappear — which is why quartzite is so much harder than sandstone that a break runs straight through the crystals instead of crumbling around them.

Limestone Sedimentary
➜Heat & pressure
Marble Metamorphic

Limestone is built from crushed shells and coral fragments made of calcium carbonate. Heat and pressure cause these calcite crystals to recrystallise into larger crystals that interlock tightly together, erasing the original shell shapes completely. This gives marble its smooth, sugary texture — and lets it take a brilliant polish, which is why it is prized for statues and buildings.

Granite Igneous
➜Extreme heat & pressure
Gneiss Metamorphic

Granite already has large, randomly arranged crystals of quartz, feldspar and mica. Under extreme heat and pressure, these minerals recrystallise and separate out into alternating light and dark stripes — gneissic banding — lined up at right angles to the pressure. Gneiss keeps granite's large crystals, but gains this new striped, banded pattern.

Build the identification key

You are handed a rock to identify. A key works by asking one yes/no question at a time until only one answer is left. Before you start, make sure these two words are clear:

Crystalline?

Made of crystals that grew together and interlock — either large and visible (like granite) or too small to see (like basalt). A rock that is not crystalline is instead made of separate grains or fragments cemented together, like sandstone.

Banded?

Shows stripes of alternating light and dark minerals, lined up in the same direction because pressure squeezed them that way. A rock with random, unaligned crystals is not banded.

Nothing is ever really "finished"

The rock cycle

Every rock on Earth is part of an endless cycle with no true beginning or end. Tap each stage on the diagram, just as it appears in your Learner's Book, to read what happens there.

Rocks on the surface

Any rock exposed at the surface — igneous, sedimentary or metamorphic — is attacked by weathering, starting the cycle over again.

Sediment

Weathered fragments are eroded, transported by rivers, wind or ice, and finally deposited — often on a sea or lake bed.

Sedimentary rock

Burial, compaction and cementation squeeze and glue the layers of sediment together into solid sedimentary rock.

Metamorphic rock

Deeper burial brings greater heat and pressure, changing the sedimentary (or any other) rock into metamorphic rock — without melting it.

Magma

If burial continues and the temperature rises high enough, the rock finally melts completely into liquid magma.

Igneous rock

As magma cools — slowly underground or quickly at the surface as lava — it solidifies into igneous rock, ready to be weathered all over again.
