Getting started

Breaking down, building up

A cell's metabolism is the sum of every chemical reaction happening inside it. Every reaction falls into one of two families — remember the polymer builder from the macromolecules lesson?

Breaking down, releasing energy 分解代谢

A catabolic pathway breaks large molecules into smaller ones — like hydrolysis breaking a polymer apart. This usually releases energy the cell can capture and use. Digesting your lunch and cellular respiration are both catabolic.

Think of a catabolic pathway like taking apart a wooden tower built from blocks: as each block comes loose, the tower's stored "potential" collapses into motion — in a cell, that released energy doesn't just vanish, it gets captured and stored in a useful form so the cell can spend it later.

Building up, requiring energy 合成代谢

An anabolic pathway builds larger molecules from smaller ones — like dehydration synthesis linking monomers into a polymer. This always requires energy input. Building muscle protein and photosynthesis are both anabolic.

An anabolic pathway is the opposite direction: like stacking those same blocks back into a tower. Lifting each block up against gravity costs energy — nothing gets built for free. This is why anabolic pathways in your body, like building new muscle after exercise, always draw on the cell's ATP supply.

Digesting a meal breaks large food molecules into small ones. Which type of pathway is this?

ADP + energy

Breaking the bond to the last phosphate is a hydrolysis reaction — the same reaction that breaks any polymer apart! This releases energy the cell can use immediately, and leaves behind ADP (adenosine diphosphate). Cells constantly rebuild ADP back into ATP using energy from food.

The negatively charged phosphate groups in ATP repel each other, like three magnets forced together with the same poles facing. That built-up tension is exactly why breaking off a phosphate group releases a useful burst of energy — it's a little like letting go of a tightly compressed spring. A typical cell recycles its entire ATP supply roughly once every minute during active work, so this reaction happens constantly, everywhere in your body.

Getting a reaction started

Every reaction needs a push

Even reactions that release energy overall need a small energy "push" to start, called activation energy. Think of it like needing to push a boulder over a small bump before it can roll downhill on its own — even though the boulder ends up lower (releasing energy overall), that first push still takes effort. An enzyme's job is to make that bump smaller.

Match the substrate to its enzyme

An enzyme's active site only fits one specific substrate shape — like a lock and key. Click a substrate, then click the enzyme that fits it.

The real "induced fit" model: the substrate and active site adjust their shape slightly as they bind, like a handshake rather than a rigid lock and key.

Scientists once pictured this as a completely rigid lock-and-key fit, but modern research shows the active site actually flexes a little as the substrate arrives, gripping it more snugly. Either way, the core idea holds: each enzyme is shaped for one job, which is why your body needs thousands of different enzymes.

Cause and effect

Enzymes have a favourite condition

Every enzyme works best at a certain temperature and pH. Move too far from that optimum, and activity drops — remember denaturation from the macromolecules lesson? The same weak bonds that hold an enzyme's 3D shape together can be disrupted by heat or extreme pH, and once that precise shape is lost, the active site no longer fits its substrate.

This is exactly why your body works hard to keep its internal temperature and pH steady — most human enzymes are finely tuned to work best around 37°C, and even a few degrees of fever can measurably slow some of them down.

Blocking the reaction

Two ways to jam an enzyme

Not every molecule that binds to an enzyme helps it work — some are inhibitors that jam it, on purpose or otherwise. Doctors and drug designers use this idea constantly: many medicines work by carefully inhibiting one specific enzyme.

Blocking the active site 竞争性抑制剂

A competitive inhibitor has a shape similar to the real substrate, so it competes for the same active site. Adding more substrate can outcompete it and restore activity, since the inhibitor and substrate are quite literally racing for the same parking spot.

Changing the enzyme's shape 非竞争性抑制剂

A noncompetitive inhibitor binds somewhere else on the enzyme entirely — a spot called an allosteric site — distorting the active site's shape from a distance. Adding more substrate doesn't help this time, because the problem isn't competition, it's that the enzyme itself has changed shape.

A real biochemistry diagram comparing the two kinds of inhibition you just read about, plus a third type called end-product (feedback) inhibition.
Real-life example

Many pesticides and nerve gases work as noncompetitive inhibitors — they permanently jam an enzyme your nerve cells need, which is exactly why they are so dangerous. On the safer side, some blood pressure medicines are carefully designed competitive inhibitors that gently dial down one specific enzyme's activity.

Mei and Tom's fever puzzle

Mei (美) has a high fever. Tom says a fever slightly above normal can actually help fight infection, but a very high fever is dangerous.

"A fever is bad, so hotter must always be better at fighting germs," says Mei.
"No, Mei — a very high fever can actually harm your own body," says Tom.

Who is correct?

Tom is correct. A mild fever can speed up immune enzymes and slow down some germs. But your own body's enzymes have an optimum temperature too — if a fever climbs too high, it starts to denature your own enzymes, just like heat denatures an egg white protein. That's why dangerously high fevers need medical treatment.
Remember

Enzymes have an optimum, not an "always better." Too cold slows them down; too hot destroys their shape.
