Modelled example

One change, many impacts: the big dam

Building a large dam changes a whole river system. It brings real benefits — but also real costs. Good geographers look for both sides before making a judgement.

A large hydroelectric dam — one change, with impacts on people, animals, and ecosystems

• Produces renewable energy for homes and businesses
• Gives flood control — protecting towns downstream during heavy rain
• Provides a reliable water supply for farming and drinking

• Destroys habitats when land is flooded
• Communities are displaced — families lose homes and farmland
• River ecosystems are disrupted — fish can't migrate, water flow changes

What's a 4-quadrant Impact Wheel?

It's a simple tool that sorts impacts two ways at once: Positive (+) or Negative (−), and affecting People or affecting Ecosystems/Animals & Plants. That makes four boxes — and a strong case study usually has evidence in all four.

People
Ecosystems / Animals & Plants
Positive (+)
People (+)

The dam provides a reliable water supply for nearby towns.

Ecosystem (+)

The dam helps control flooding, protecting habitats downstream.

Negative (−)
People (−)

Villages upstream are flooded and families lose their homes.

Ecosystem (−)

Fish can no longer swim upstream to reach their breeding grounds.

Notice how some impacts affect people and others affect animals & ecosystems — next, you'll sort more statements like these yourself.

Guided practice · Impact Wheel

Choose a case study for your Impact Wheel

In small groups, pick one of the changes below and build your own 4-quadrant Impact Wheel about it.

Trees are cleared for farming, building, or resources. Think: who benefits from the cleared land, and what happens to the animals and plants that lived there?

Harmful substances enter water, air, or land. Think: who is harmed by breathing or drinking pollution, and how does it affect wildlife?

Towns and cities grow as more people and buildings move in. Think: who gains new opportunities, and what natural habitat is lost?

Rising temperatures and extreme weather affect the whole planet. Think: who is most affected, and how are animals and ecosystems adapting or struggling?

What does "sustainability" really mean?

Modelled example

From problem to solution: deforestation

A strong solution doesn't just fix a problem — it explains why it works. Here's the model we'll use all lesson.

Problem

Deforestation is destroying rainforest habitats and increasing CO₂ in the atmosphere.

Solution

Reforestation and protected forest areas.

Why it works

New trees restore habitat for animals and absorb CO₂ from the air.

Notice: the "why it works" links directly back to the problem.
Guided research

Explore solutions to environmental problems

Tap through these five solution types, then research solutions for your own chosen issue.

Using solar, wind, or hydro power instead of fossil fuels. Why it works: it produces energy without releasing greenhouse gases into the air.

Planting new trees and protecting existing forests from being cut down. Why it works: it restores lost habitats and absorbs carbon dioxide.

Governments making laws that limit pollution, protect land, or regulate industries. Why it works: it stops harmful actions before they happen, for everyone at once.

Groups of volunteers removing rubbish from rivers, beaches, or parks. Why it works: it removes existing pollution and raises awareness in the community.

Using less, reusing items, and recycling materials instead of throwing them away. Why it works: it reduces waste and the demand for new raw materials.
