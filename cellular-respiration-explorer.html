Phase 1 · Wonder

⚡ Where Does Your Energy Actually Come From?

You eat food. You gain energy. But how does a slice of pizza become a muscle contraction? The answer involves one of the most elegant molecules in all of biology — ATP — and a carefully choreographed series of chemical reactions we call cellular respiration.

Your cells cannot directly use the energy stored in glucose or food. They need to convert it into a form they can actually spend — ATP (adenosine triphosphate). Every muscle contraction, every nerve signal, every protein your cells build costs ATP. You produce and consume your body weight in ATP every single day. So how does it get made — and why does oxygen matter so much?

✏️ Your Prediction (before we dig in)

Cellular respiration is the process that converts the chemical energy stored in glucose into the usable energy currency of ATP. It happens in three stages — glycolysis (in the cytoplasm), the citric acid cycle (in the mitochondria), and the electron transport chain (on the inner mitochondrial membrane). In this interactive, we'll master Sections 5.1 and 5.2 — ATP energy molecules and the overview of respiration plus glycolysis.

Cellular Respiration ATP Structure ATP Hydrolysis ADP Phosphorylation Gold vs. Cash Analogy 2nd Law of Thermodynamics Oxidation & Reduction NAD⁺ / NADH FAD / FADH₂
Cellular Respiration — The process by which cells break down organic molecules (primarily glucose) to produce ATP. The full equation: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + ATP energy. It is a catabolic, exergonic (energy-releasing) process. All kingdoms of life perform cellular respiration — bacteria, archaea, protists, fungi, plants, and animals. Q1, Q2.
ATP (Adenosine Triphosphate) — The universal energy currency of the cell. Structure: adenine base + ribose sugar + 3 phosphate groups. The bond between the 2nd and 3rd phosphate is a high-energy phosphoanhydride bond — breaking it releases energy the cell can use. Draw it: adenine–ribose–P–P–[P] where the last P–P bond is the "high energy bond." Q4.
ATP Hydrolysis — ATP + H₂O → ADP + Pᵢ + energy released. Adding water breaks the high-energy phosphate bond, releasing energy (~7.3 kcal/mol). This is an exergonic reaction — it releases energy, which powers cellular work (muscle contraction, active transport, biosynthesis). Q5.
ADP Phosphorylation — ADP + Pᵢ + energy → ATP + H₂O. The reverse of hydrolysis — requires a net input of energy to reform the high-energy bond. This is an endergonic reaction — it stores energy into ATP. In animal cells, this energy comes from the oxidation of glucose (cellular respiration). Q6, Q7.
Glucose = Bar of Gold / ATP = Cash — Q3 asks about this analogy. Glucose is like a bar of gold — high stored energy but not directly spendable. ATP is like cash — the immediately usable form of energy currency. Cells can't directly use glucose's energy; they must first "make change" by converting glucose → ATP during respiration. Q3.
2nd Law of Thermodynamics — Energy conversions are never 100% efficient; some energy is always lost as heat (disorder/entropy increases). This helps us understand cellular respiration: cells cannot convert all of glucose's energy into ATP — some is inevitably lost as heat. This is why you feel warm after exercise. Q9.
Oxidation & Reduction — Two always-paired processes (redox reactions). Oxidation = loss of electrons (OIL — Oxidation Is Loss). Reduction = gain of electrons (RIG — Reduction Is Gain). Remember: OIL RIG. In cellular respiration, glucose is oxidized (loses electrons) and oxygen is reduced (gains electrons to become water). Q10.
NAD⁺ / NADH — Electron carriers that shuttle electrons through cellular respiration. NAD⁺ + 2e⁻ + H⁺ → NADH is a reduction reaction (NAD⁺ gains electrons → becomes NADH). NADH carries electrons to the electron transport chain, where they are used to pump protons and generate ATP. Q11.
FAD / FADH₂ — Another electron carrier. FADH₂ → FAD + 2e⁻ + 2H⁺ is an oxidation reaction (FADH₂ loses electrons → becomes FAD). Like NADH, FADH₂ carries electrons to the electron transport chain. Both NADH and FADH₂ are the primary products of the citric acid cycle — they are the "loaded" form of these carriers. Q11.

NAD⁺ + 2e⁻ + H⁺ → NADH — This is REDUCTION

NAD⁺ gains two electrons (and a hydrogen) → becomes NADH. Gaining electrons = Reduction (RIG). NADH is the "loaded" or reduced form — it now carries electrons to the electron transport chain, where those electrons will be used to generate ATP.

📌 Q11: NAD⁺ + 2e⁻ + H⁺ → NADH is a reduction reaction.

FADH₂ → FAD + 2e⁻ + 2H⁺ — This is OXIDATION

FADH₂ loses two electrons → becomes FAD. Losing electrons = Oxidation (OIL). This happens at the electron transport chain, where FADH₂ donates its electrons. FAD is then recycled back to pick up more electrons in the citric acid cycle.

📌 Q11: FADH₂ → FAD + 2e⁻ + 2H⁺ is an oxidation reaction.

Glucose is OXIDIZED during cellular respiration

Throughout glycolysis and the citric acid cycle, glucose and its breakdown products lose electrons (are oxidized). Those electrons are picked up by NAD⁺ (becoming NADH) and FAD (becoming FADH₂). This is why cellular respiration is called oxidative phosphorylation — glucose is oxidized to release energy.

📌 Q17: In glycolysis, glucose is oxidized. NAD⁺ is reduced (to NADH).

O₂ is REDUCED during cellular respiration

At the very end of the electron transport chain, O₂ is the final electron acceptor. It accepts electrons (and hydrogen ions) and is reduced to water (H₂O). This is why oxygen is essential for aerobic respiration — without it, the ETC cannot run and ATP production collapses.

📌 This is why aerobic respiration requires oxygen — O₂ is the final electron acceptor and must be reduced to H₂O.

🔌 Oxidative Phosphorylation — Q12

Oxidative phosphorylation is the process of making ATP by using the energy from electron transfer (oxidation reactions) to drive the phosphorylation of ADP. "Oxidative" = electrons are transferred (oxidation occurs). "Phosphorylation" = a phosphate group is added to ADP to make ATP. The electron transport chain and ATP synthase are the key machinery — and it requires oxygen as the final electron acceptor.

📌 Q12: Oxidative phosphorylation = using electron transfer (oxidation) to power ATP synthesis (phosphorylation of ADP).

💨 Aerobic vs. Anaerobic — Q13

Aerobic processes require oxygen (O₂). Anaerobic processes do NOT require oxygen. Glycolysis is anaerobic — it happens in the cytoplasm with or without oxygen. The citric acid cycle and electron transport chain are aerobic — they require O₂. This is why you can briefly sprint without breathing heavily, but sustained exercise demands oxygen.

📌 Q13: Aerobic = requires O₂ (citric acid cycle, ETC). Anaerobic = no O₂ required (glycolysis, fermentation).

📋 The 3 Stages of Cellular Respiration — Q14

In order: (1) Glycolysis — in the cytoplasm, anaerobic, splits glucose into 2 pyruvate; (2) Citric Acid (Krebs) Cycle — in the mitochondrial matrix, aerobic, generates NADH/FADH₂/CO₂; (3) Electron Transport Chain (ETC) — on the inner mitochondrial membrane, aerobic, generates ~28 ATP via oxidative phosphorylation.

📌 Q14: Glycolysis → Citric Acid Cycle → Electron Transport Chain

📍 Glycolysis Location — Q15

Glycolysis occurs in the cytoplasm (cytosol). It is anaerobic — it does not require oxygen, and it can occur whether or not oxygen is present. This is why even cells that are temporarily starved of oxygen can still produce a small amount of ATP via glycolysis (followed by fermentation).

📌 Q15: Location = cytoplasm. Aerobic or anaerobic? Anaerobic (does not require O₂).

📦 Glycolysis Products — Q16 & Q17

Starting from 1 glucose molecule, glycolysis produces three types of products: 2 pyruvate (3-carbon molecules), 2 ATP net (actually uses 2 and produces 4, so net = 2), and 2 NADH (electron carriers). In the process, glucose is oxidized (loses electrons) and NAD⁺ is reduced (gains those electrons to become NADH).

📌 Q16: Products per glucose: 2 pyruvate, 2 ATP (net), 2 NADH.
📌 Q17: Glucose is oxidized. NAD⁺ is reduced (becomes NADH).

💰 Net ATP in Glycolysis — Q20

Glycolysis invests 2 ATP in the early "energy investment phase" (to phosphorylate glucose), then generates 4 ATP in the "energy payoff phase." Net gain = 4 − 2 = 2 ATP per glucose. This is why we say "net gain" — the cell must spend ATP to make more ATP (like investing money to earn a return).

📌 Q20: Invest 2 ATP → generate 4 ATP → net gain = 2 ATP per glucose.
