Phase 1 · Wonder

🔬 How Does One Cell Become a Whole Body?

Every human being begins as a single fertilised egg cell. That one cell divides, specialises, and organises itself into trillions of cells across hundreds of different types — liver cells, neurons, muscle fibres, immune cells — all with identical DNA but radically different functions. How?

Your liver right now is simultaneously detoxifying alcohol, storing glucose as glycogen, making bile to digest fats, and regulating your blood protein levels — all at the same time, in the same organ. Meanwhile, your kidneys are adjusting exactly how much water to keep based on signals from your brain. How does your body coordinate all of this without you thinking about it?

✏️ Lakshanya's Starting Point

This review covers VCE Biology Unit 1: cell structure and function (AoS 1), stem cells, plant vascular systems, animal digestive/endocrine/excretory systems (AoS 2), and homeostatic regulation of temperature, blood glucose and water balance — including the liver's central role.

Phase 2 · Cell Structure & Function

🧫 Prokaryotic vs Eukaryotic Cells

All cells are either prokaryotic (simple, no membrane-bound nucleus) or eukaryotic (complex, with a nucleus and organelles). The VCE Study Design requires you to explain cell structure, the surface area-to-volume ratio, and the specialised functions of key organelles.

A cell must be small enough to exchange materials across its membrane efficiently. As a cell grows, its volume increases faster than its surface area. A large cell has too little surface area relative to its volume — it can't get enough nutrients in or waste out quickly enough. This is why cells are small, and why large organisms need internal compartments (organelles) with specialised membranes.

🔵 Nucleus

The control centre of the eukaryotic cell. Contains the cell's DNA in the form of chromatin (chromosomes during division). Enclosed by the nuclear envelope (double membrane with pores). The nucleolus inside the nucleus assembles ribosomal RNA. Instructions for all protein synthesis originate here — DNA is transcribed to mRNA, which exits through nuclear pores to ribosomes.

📌 VCE: The nucleus is the defining feature of a eukaryotic cell. Prokaryotes have a nucleoid region — DNA in the cytoplasm — not a membrane-bound nucleus.

⚡ Mitochondria

The "powerhouse" — sites of aerobic cellular respiration, converting glucose and oxygen into ATP (adenosine triphosphate), the cell's energy currency. Have a double membrane: smooth outer membrane and highly folded inner membrane (cristae), which increases surface area for ATP production. Contain their own DNA and ribosomes — evidence for endosymbiotic origin. Cells with high energy needs (muscle, liver) have many mitochondria.

📌 Glucose + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O + ATP (in the presence of mitochondria). The liver cell's high metabolic activity requires abundant mitochondria.

🌿 Chloroplast

Found only in plant cells and algae. Site of photosynthesis — converting light energy, CO₂ and water into glucose and oxygen. Have a double outer membrane plus an internal system of thylakoid membranes (stacked into grana) where light reactions occur, and the stroma where the Calvin cycle occurs. Like mitochondria, contain their own DNA — evidence for endosymbiotic origin from cyanobacteria.

📌 VCE link: Plant cells have chloroplasts AND mitochondria — they photosynthesise AND respire. Animals only respire (no chloroplasts).

🌀 Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER)

Rough ER — studded with ribosomes; site of protein synthesis for export. Proteins are synthesised, folded, and moved to the Golgi. Smooth ER — no ribosomes; involved in lipid synthesis, detoxification (especially in liver cells), and calcium storage. The ER is an extensive network of membranes connected to the nuclear envelope, providing an internal transport system.

📌 The liver's smooth ER is especially well-developed — liver cells use it to detoxify alcohol, drugs, and metabolic waste products.

📦 Golgi Apparatus

The "post office" of the cell — receives proteins from the rough ER, modifies them (e.g. adds sugar chains), sorts them, and packages them into vesicles for export (secretion) or delivery to other organelles. The protein secretory pathway: ribosome → rough ER → Golgi → secretory vesicle → plasma membrane. Critical for secreted proteins like hormones (e.g. insulin) and digestive enzymes.

📌 Insulin is made on ribosomes of rough ER in pancreatic beta cells, processed through the Golgi, packaged into vesicles, and released by exocytosis.

🛡️ Plasma Membrane

The fluid mosaic model: a phospholipid bilayer with embedded proteins. Controls what enters and leaves the cell. Transport across it: osmosis (water — from high to low water potential), facilitated diffusion (polar/large molecules via protein channels — no energy required), active transport (against concentration gradient — requires ATP and carrier proteins). The hydrophobic core blocks polar molecules; proteins provide selective passage.

📌 Glucose enters cells via facilitated diffusion (GLUT proteins). Sodium is pumped out against its gradient by the Na⁺/K⁺ pump using active transport (ATP).

🔴 Ribosomes

Sites of protein synthesis (translation). Made of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and proteins. Found free in cytoplasm (make proteins for use inside the cell) or on rough ER (make proteins for secretion or membranes). Prokaryotic ribosomes (70S) are smaller than eukaryotic (80S) — this difference is exploited by antibiotics like streptomycin, which bind 70S ribosomes and disrupt bacterial protein synthesis without affecting human cells.

📌 All cells have ribosomes — they are the only organelle found in both prokaryotes and eukaryotes.

💧 Vacuole

Plant cells have a large central vacuole filled with cell sap, maintaining turgor pressure — pushing the plasma membrane against the cell wall, keeping the cell rigid (turgid). When a plant wilts, the vacuoles lose water and turgor is lost. Animal cells have small, temporary vacuoles for transporting materials. Contractile vacuoles in some protists pump out excess water by active transport.

📌 Turgor pressure in plant cells is what keeps non-woody plants upright. This is why plants wilt when dehydrated — loss of turgor in cells causes the plant to droop.

Phase 3 · Stem Cells & Differentiation

🌱 Stem Cells — From One to Many

A stem cell is an undifferentiated cell capable of self-renewal and differentiation into specialised cell types. The VCE Study Design requires you to understand pluripotency, totipotency, stem cell types, and differentiation — including the role of embryonic stem cells.

All ~37 trillion cells in your body descended from a single fertilised egg — a totipotent cell. As development progresses, cells become progressively more restricted in what they can become. Embryonic stem cells are pluripotent — they can become almost any cell type. This makes them powerful for medicine, but raises profound ethical questions about the status of early embryos.

🥚 Totipotent Stem Cells
Can differentiate into any cell type — including extra-embryonic tissues like the placenta. Found only in the very early embryo (zygote and cells up to the 8-cell morula stage, ~days 1–4). If you split a totipotent cell, each half can develop into a complete embryo — this is how identical twins form naturally. Totipotency is lost as the embryo develops past the morula stage.
🌟 Pluripotent Stem Cells — Embryonic Stem Cells (ESCs)
Can differentiate into almost any cell type — all three germ layers (ectoderm, mesoderm, endoderm) — but NOT extra-embryonic tissues like the placenta. Found in the inner cell mass (ICM) of the blastocyst (~day 5–6 after fertilisation). Embryonic stem cells (ESCs) are derived from the ICM of donated embryos (usually surplus IVF embryos). They are the most therapeutically valuable stem cells because of their broad differentiation potential. Ethical debate: harvesting ESCs destroys the embryo — is this acceptable?
📌 VCE: Know the difference between totipotent and pluripotent. Know where embryonic stem cells come from (blastocyst ICM) and why they are ethically controversial.
🔀 Multipotent Stem Cells — Adult Stem Cells
Can differentiate into a limited range of cell types within a particular tissue. Examples: haematopoietic stem cells (bone marrow → all blood cell types), neural stem cells (brain), mesenchymal stem cells (connective tissue, bone, cartilage, fat). Less flexible than ESCs but ethically uncontroversial — no embryo is destroyed. Used clinically in bone marrow transplants.
🎯 Unipotent / Progenitor Cells
Can only differentiate into one specific cell type. Examples: muscle satellite cells (→ muscle fibres only), skin progenitor cells (→ keratinocytes only). These are tissue-specific cells that replace worn-out cells. They have limited but important renewal capacity.
🫀 Differentiated Cells
Fully specialised — have switched on specific genes and switched off others. Examples: red blood cells (no nucleus, full of haemoglobin), neurons (long axons for signal transmission), hepatocytes (liver cells — packed with smooth ER for detox). Differentiation is the process of gene expression changes that transform an unspecialised cell into a specialised one — the DNA sequence doesn't change, but which genes are active does. This is epigenetics in action.

Why are embryonic stem cells so important for medicine? Diseases like Parkinson's disease, type 1 diabetes, and spinal cord injuries involve the death or malfunction of specific cell types. ESCs could potentially be grown into replacement neurons, beta cells, or nerve fibres. However, there are significant challenges: immune rejection, risk of tumour formation (teratomas), and the ethical issue of embryo destruction. Induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) — adult cells reprogrammed back to a pluripotent state — may offer an ethical alternative.

Phase 4 · Plant Systems & Water Regulation

🌿 Vascular Plants — Transport & Water Regulation

Plants must move water, minerals, and sugars between roots, stems and leaves across large distances without a pump. They do this through two specialised vascular tissues: xylem (water and minerals up) and phloem (sugars in both directions). The VCE Study Design requires you to explain water regulation in vascular plants.

1
Xylem — Water & Mineral Transport (Upward)
Xylem consists of dead, hollow cells (vessels and tracheids) arranged end-to-end to form continuous tubes. Water moves from roots → stem → leaves by transpiration pull: as water evaporates from leaf surfaces (stomata), it creates tension that pulls water up the xylem. This works because of water's cohesion (hydrogen bonds between water molecules) and adhesion (water sticking to xylem walls). The continuous water column extends from soil to leaf. Dissolved minerals (nitrates, phosphates) are carried along.
VCE: The TACT mechanism — Transpiration, Adhesion, Cohesion, Tension — drives water movement in xylem. You need to be able to explain why water moves upward without a pump.
2
Phloem — Sugar Transport (Both Directions)
Phloem consists of living sieve tube cells connected by sieve plates, with companion cells that provide energy. It transports sugars (mainly sucrose) produced by photosynthesis from source (leaves) to sink (roots, growing tips, fruit) by pressure flow. High sugar concentration at source → water moves in by osmosis → high pressure pushes fluid toward sink. Phloem can carry sugars both upward and downward (unlike xylem which is always upward).
VCE: Unlike xylem (dead cells, passive), phloem (living cells, active) requires energy. The distinction between xylem (water + minerals) and phloem (sugars) is frequently tested.
3
Stomata & Water Loss — Guard Cells
Stomata are pores on leaf surfaces (mainly underside) surrounded by two guard cells. When guard cells take up water (by osmosis) → they swell and bow outward → stoma opens → gas exchange (CO₂ in, O₂ out) and transpiration occur. When water is scarce → guard cells lose water → become flaccid → stoma closes → transpiration stops. The hormone abscisic acid (ABA) signals guard cells to close during drought. Factors affecting transpiration rate: temperature, humidity, wind speed, light intensity.
VCE: Be able to explain how guard cells control stomatal opening and closing using osmosis and turgor pressure. This links plant water regulation to the plasma membrane section.
4
Plant Cells — Specialisation for Transport
Root hair cells — long extensions that dramatically increase surface area for water absorption. No chloroplasts (no light underground). Thin cell walls for easy water movement. Epidermal cells — thin, no chloroplasts, transparent to allow light through. Mesophyll cells — packed with chloroplasts for maximum photosynthesis. Palisade mesophyll (upper layer) — columnar, tightly packed. Spongy mesophyll (lower layer) — loosely arranged with large air spaces for gas exchange.
VCE: Each cell type has a structure specifically suited to its function. "Structure determines function" is a recurring exam theme.

Phase 5 · Animal Systems

🫀 Animal Systems — Digestive, Endocrine & Excretory

Multicellular animals have specialised organ systems. The VCE Study Design covers the digestive, endocrine, and excretory systems and how cells within them are specialised for specific functions.

Digestive System Endocrine System Excretory System Cell Specialisation Tissues & Organs
Digestive System — Breaks down food into small molecules (nutrients) that can be absorbed into the bloodstream. Key organs: mouth (amylase in saliva breaks down starch), oesophagus (peristalsis), stomach (pepsin and HCl break down proteins; pH ~2), small intestine (lipase, amylase, peptidase from pancreas; bile from liver emulsifies fats; villi increase surface area for absorption), large intestine (water absorption; bacterial fermentation of fibre). Enzymes are specific to substrate: amylase → starch, lipase → lipids, pepsin/peptidase → proteins.
Endocrine System — A chemical communication system using hormones — chemical messengers secreted by glands directly into the bloodstream and carried to target organs. Slower than nervous system but longer-lasting effects. Key glands: pituitary (master gland — controls others), thyroid (metabolic rate), adrenal (stress response — adrenalin), pancreas (blood glucose — insulin and glucagon), ovaries/testes (sex hormones). Hormones bind to specific receptors — only target cells respond.
Excretory System — Removes metabolic waste products from the body. The kidneys filter blood (producing urine containing urea, excess water, and salts), regulated by ADH (antidiuretic hormone). The liver converts toxic ammonia (from protein breakdown) to less toxic urea (urea cycle). Lungs excrete CO₂ (waste from cellular respiration). Skin excretes small amounts of water and salts in sweat. Urea = main nitrogen-containing waste in humans.
Cell Specialisation — Different cell types have different structures because they express different subsets of their genes, producing different proteins. Examples: intestinal epithelial cells have microvilli (brush border) to maximise absorption surface area; goblet cells secrete mucus to protect the gut lining; smooth muscle cells in gut walls contract rhythmically (peristalsis) to move food; pancreatic beta cells have abundant rough ER and Golgi for insulin production and secretion.
Tissues, Organs and Systems — Organisation hierarchy: cell → tissue → organ → organ system → organism. A tissue is a group of similar cells performing a common function (e.g. muscle tissue, epithelial tissue, nervous tissue, connective tissue). An organ is made of multiple tissue types working together (e.g. the stomach has smooth muscle, epithelium, and connective tissue). An organ system is multiple organs coordinated for a larger function (e.g. digestive system).

Phase 6 · The Liver

🫁 The Liver — The Body's Chemical Processing Plant

The liver (hepatic organ) is the most metabolically active organ in the body. A single hepatocyte (liver cell) performs over 500 known functions. The VCE Study Design requires you to understand the liver's role in maintaining homeostasis, particularly in blood glucose regulation and detoxification.

Hepatocytes are packed with smooth endoplasmic reticulum (for detoxification), mitochondria (for high energy demands), Golgi apparatus (for protein secretion), and glycogen granules (for glucose storage). They are among the most structurally complex cells in the body — because they need to be.

🍯 Glycogen Storage — Blood Glucose Buffering

When blood glucose rises (e.g. after a meal), the liver removes excess glucose from the portal vein and converts it to glycogen (glucose polymer) by glycogenesis, under the influence of insulin. This lowers blood glucose back to normal. When blood glucose falls (e.g. between meals), the liver converts glycogen back to glucose by glycogenolysis, under the influence of glucagon. The liver can also make new glucose from non-carbohydrate sources (amino acids, glycerol) by gluconeogenesis.

📌 VCE: Glycogenesis (building glycogen) is stimulated by insulin. Glycogenolysis (breaking glycogen down) is stimulated by glucagon. The liver acts as a glucose buffer — smoothing out blood glucose spikes and troughs.

🟡 Bile Production

Hepatocytes produce bile — a yellow-green alkaline liquid stored in the gall bladder and released into the small intestine when fats arrive. Bile contains bile salts (emulsify fats into tiny droplets, increasing surface area for lipase) and bile pigments (bilirubin — from breakdown of haemoglobin in old red blood cells). Bile is not an enzyme — it doesn't break chemical bonds — but it is essential for efficient fat digestion. Bile also neutralises the acid from the stomach, raising small intestine pH to ~7–8.

📌 Bile emulsification is like dishwashing liquid on a greasy pan — it breaks large fat globules into tiny droplets so lipase has more surface to work on.

🧹 Detoxification

The liver is the primary site of detoxification. Its smooth ER contains enzymes that chemically modify toxic substances into less harmful, more water-soluble forms that can be excreted in bile or urine. Substances detoxified include: alcohol (ethanol → acetaldehyde → acetate; the acetaldehyde is what causes hangover and liver damage), drugs and medications (aspirin, paracetamol, antibiotics), hormones (used insulin and estrogen are broken down), and ammonia (converted to urea). Paracetamol overdose destroys liver cells because the toxic intermediate overwhelms the detox enzymes.

📌 This is why drug dosing matters — the liver's detox capacity is finite. And why alcohol + paracetamol combination is dangerous: both tax the same enzyme system.

♻️ Urea Production (Urea Cycle)

When amino acids are broken down (deamination), the amino group (−NH₂) is removed and converted to ammonia (NH₃) — which is highly toxic. The liver converts ammonia to urea (less toxic, water-soluble) via the urea cycle. Urea is released into the bloodstream, transported to the kidneys, and excreted in urine. Without a functional liver, ammonia builds up in the blood → hepatic encephalopathy (confusion, coma). The kidneys excrete urea but cannot make it — that's the liver's job.

📌 VCE link: Urea is the main nitrogenous waste in humans. Liver makes it; kidneys excrete it. Both organs are essential for nitrogen waste management.

🩸 Plasma Protein Synthesis

Hepatocytes synthesise most of the proteins found in blood plasma, including: albumin (maintains blood osmotic pressure — prevents water leaking out of blood vessels), fibrinogen and prothrombin (clotting factors — essential for blood clotting), globulins (immune function, transport proteins). Liver failure leads to oedema (fluid accumulation in tissues) because albumin levels drop and water leaks out of blood vessels. It also leads to uncontrolled bleeding because clotting factors are not produced.

📌 Vitamin K (fat-soluble) is needed to make some clotting factors. Since fat absorption requires bile, liver disease → reduced bile → poor fat absorption → vitamin K deficiency → bleeding problems. Multiple system effects!

🌡️ Heat Production

The liver is the body's largest internal heat-generating organ. Its intense metabolic activity — performing hundreds of chemical reactions simultaneously — releases significant heat as a by-product. This heat is distributed via blood flow throughout the body. The liver contributes substantially to maintaining core body temperature (37°C). This is why when you shiver (trying to generate heat), the liver also ramps up its metabolism. Liver temperature itself can be ~0.5°C higher than core blood temperature.

📌 This is why hypothermia can be treated in part by warming the blood — the liver continues to generate heat if metabolically active.

Phase 7 · Homeostasis & Feedback Loops

⚖️ Homeostasis — Keeping the Internal Environment Stable

Homeostasis is the maintenance of a stable internal environment despite changes in external conditions. The VCE Study Design requires you to understand stimulus-response models, negative feedback loops, and the homeostatic regulation of body temperature, blood glucose, and water balance.

Your body temperature is kept at ~37°C even when it's 0°C outside or 40°C. Your blood glucose stays between ~4–8 mmol/L regardless of whether you just ate a full meal or haven't eaten for 8 hours. Your blood pressure, pH, and osmolarity are all tightly regulated. This continuous, automatic self-correction is homeostasis — and it requires constant sensing, communicating, and responding.

🌡️ Temperature Regulation — Thermoregulation

↑
Too Hot — Cooling Responses
Stimulus: Body temperature rises above 37°C → Receptor: Thermoreceptors in skin and hypothalamus detect increase → Control Centre: Hypothalamus (thermostat of the body) → Effectors:
• Sweat glands increase sweat production → evaporation cools skin
• Skin blood vessels dilate (vasodilation) → more blood near skin surface → more heat lost by radiation
• Reduced metabolic rate → less internal heat generated
• Behavioural: seek shade, remove clothing
Result: Temperature drops back to 37°C → negative feedback reduces further response
VCE: Always include all 5 components: stimulus, receptor, control centre, effector, response. Vasodilation = arterioles widen near skin. Do NOT say "blood goes to skin" — say "blood vessels near the skin dilate, increasing blood flow to the skin surface."
↓
Too Cold — Warming Responses
Stimulus: Body temperature falls below 37°C → Receptor: Thermoreceptors detect decrease → Control Centre: Hypothalamus → Effectors:
• Skeletal muscles shiver — rapid, involuntary contractions generate heat
• Skin blood vessels constrict (vasoconstriction) → less blood near skin → less heat lost
• Increased metabolic rate → more internal heat
• Arrector pili muscles contract → goosebumps (raise hair for insulation — vestigial in humans)
• Behavioural: put on clothes, move to warm area
Result: Temperature rises back to 37°C → feedback reduces shivering etc.
VCE: Vasoconstriction = arterioles near skin narrow, diverting blood deeper. This reduces heat loss by radiation. Shivering generates heat through ATP hydrolysis in muscle.

Phase 8 · Blood Glucose Regulation

🍬 Blood Glucose Regulation

Blood glucose must stay between approximately 4–8 mmol/L. Too low (hypoglycaemia) → brain and organs starve. Too high (hyperglycaemia) → damages blood vessels, nerves, and organs. The pancreas regulates blood glucose using two antagonistic hormones: insulin and glucagon.

Phase 9 · Water Balance Regulation

💧 Water Balance — Osmoregulation

The body must maintain the water content and solute concentration of blood within tight limits. This is osmoregulation. Too much water dilutes blood (low osmolarity); too little concentrates it (high osmolarity). The kidneys are the primary osmoregulatory organ, controlled by the hormone ADH (antidiuretic hormone) from the pituitary gland.
