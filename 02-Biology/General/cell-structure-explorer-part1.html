Phase 1 · Wonder

🔬 The Universe Inside a Single Cell

About 250 human red blood cells could fit on the head of a pin. A typical bacterium is 10× smaller than that. Yet inside each of those bacteria is a complete, self-sustaining chemical factory — with its own DNA, protein-building machinery, and energy system.

A bacterium and a human cell are both alive — they both have DNA, make proteins, and maintain homeostasis. Yet one fits inside the other with room to spare. If cells are so tiny, how do scientists actually study them? And what structural features determine whether a cell can even survive at a given size?

✏️ Your Prediction (before we dig in)

The answer lies in the surface area-to-volume ratio. As a cell grows larger, its volume increases much faster than its surface area — meaning the plasma membrane can't keep up with the demands of the larger interior. This is why cells either divide when they get too large, or develop specialized internal compartments (organelles). Understanding how cells are studied and how they're structured is the foundation of all of biology.

📖 Microscopy & Cell Theory — Core Vocabulary

Tap each term to reveal its definition. These are the foundational tools and ideas that make all of cell biology possible.

Cell Theory Light Microscope Electron Microscope SEM vs. TEM Magnification vs. Resolution Two Types of Cells Biological Hierarchy
Cell Theory (Schleiden, Schwann & Virchow, late 1830s) — Three tenets: (1) All living things are composed of one or more cells. (2) The cell is the basic unit of life. (3) New cells arise from existing cells (cells don't appear spontaneously). First proposed by Matthias Schleiden (botanist) and Theodor Schwann (zoologist); Rudolf Virchow later added the third tenet. Antony van Leeuwenhoek (1670s) first observed bacteria and protozoa; Robert Hooke coined the term "cell" in 1665.
Light Microscope — Uses visible light bent through lens systems to magnify specimens. Advantages: can view living organisms; disadvantages: cells are transparent so require staining (which often kills cells). College lab light microscopes magnify up to ~400× (oil immersion lenses up to 1,000×). Resolution ~200 nanometers. Images appear inverted — moving the slide left makes the image appear to move right.
Electron Microscope — Uses a beam of electrons instead of light, providing much higher magnification (up to 100,000×) and resolution (~50 picometers). Disadvantage: the preparation method kills specimens — living cells cannot be viewed. Significantly more expensive and bulky than light microscopes. Electrons move best in a vacuum, so living specimens cannot be observed.
SEM vs. TEM — Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM): the electron beam moves back and forth across the cell's surface, creating detailed 3D images of surface features. Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM): the electron beam penetrates the cell and provides high-detail images of internal structures. SEM = surface; TEM = internal.
Magnification vs. Resolving Power — Magnification is simply enlarging an image — but a highly magnified image can still be blurry. Resolving power (resolution) is the ability to distinguish two adjacent structures as separate. Higher resolution = better clarity and detail. Resolution in light microscopy is limited by the wavelength of visible light; electron microscopes achieve far higher resolution because electrons have shorter wavelengths.
Two Broad Cell Types — Prokaryotic cells (domains Bacteria and Archaea) — no true nucleus, no membrane-bound organelles, generally smaller (0.1–5.0 μm). Eukaryotic cells (animals, plants, fungi, protists) — have a true membrane-bound nucleus, membrane-bound organelles, generally larger (10–100 μm). Both share: plasma membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes, and DNA.
Biological Hierarchy — Cells are the fundamental unit of life; cells of one kind working together form a tissue; multiple tissues combine to form an organ; multiple organs form an organ system; multiple organ systems form an organism. Life cannot be divided into pieces smaller than the cell and still be independently alive — which is why viruses are considered particles, not cells (they can't replicate independently).

🤝 The 4 Features All Cells Share

All cells — prokaryotic or eukaryotic — share these four features: (1) a plasma membrane separating the interior from the environment; (2) cytoplasm (the gel-like cytosol inside the cell, containing other components); (3) ribosomes for protein synthesis; (4) DNA to store and transmit hereditary information. These shared features reflect common ancestry — all life descended from a single common ancestor.

📌 AP tip: "Do prokaryotes have ribosomes?" YES — ribosomes are found in ALL cells. Prokaryotic ribosomes are smaller (70S) than eukaryotic ribosomes (80S), but both types exist.

🦠 Prokaryote-Specific Features

Prokaryotes ("before nucleus") lack a membrane-bound nucleus. Their DNA exists as a single circular chromosome in the nucleoid — a central region of the cell (not a membrane-bound organelle). Many bacteria also carry small circular DNA molecules called plasmids, which often contain genes for antibiotic resistance. Plasmids are transferred between bacteria via horizontal gene transfer, spreading antibiotic resistance rapidly.

📌 Prokaryotes: 0.1–5.0 μm in diameter. Eukaryotes: 10–100 μm. Prokaryotes are about 10× smaller, allowing faster diffusion of nutrients and wastes.

🧱 Cell Wall & Capsule

Most bacteria have a peptidoglycan cell wall surrounding the plasma membrane — a rigid network of complex sugars linked by peptide bridges. The cell wall provides shape, protection against osmotic pressure, and prevents dehydration. Antibiotics like penicillin disrupt peptidoglycan synthesis, causing bacterial cells to lyse (burst). Many bacteria also have a polysaccharide capsule outside the cell wall that helps cells adhere to surfaces and evade immune systems.

📌 The peptidoglycan layer is unique to bacteria — eukaryotic cells lack it entirely. This is why antibiotics that target peptidoglycan synthesis can kill bacteria without harming human cells.

🌀 Flagella, Pili & Fimbriae

Flagella (singular: flagellum) are whip-like appendages used for locomotion — they rotate like a propeller to move the cell through liquid environments. Pili (singular: pilus) are protein appendages used to exchange genetic material during a type of reproduction called conjugation (a form of horizontal gene transfer). Fimbriae are shorter, bristle-like appendages that help bacteria attach to host cells during infection.

📌 Key distinction: flagella = locomotion; pili = genetic exchange (conjugation); fimbriae = attachment to surfaces/host cells.

🌋 Archaea — Ancient Extremophiles

Archaea are the second domain of prokaryotes (alongside Bacteria). They likely evolved in the harsh conditions of early Earth — high temperatures, extreme acidity, or high salinity — and many archaea today are still found in extreme environments like hot springs, polar regions, hydrothermal vents, and highly salty lakes. Archaea are distinct from bacteria in cell membrane composition, ribosomal RNA sequences, and other molecular features, but share the general prokaryotic body plan.

📌 The earliest fossil evidence for life — microbial mats ~3.5 billion years old — suggests prokaryotes (likely archaea or their ancestors) were among the first life forms on Earth.

📐 Why Cell Size Matters — SA:V Ratio

As a cell's radius increases, surface area increases as the square of the radius (4πr²), but volume increases as the cube (4/3πr³) — much faster. This means a larger cell has less surface area relative to its volume, limiting how efficiently nutrients can enter and wastes can exit. If a cell grows too large, the plasma membrane can't supply the interior fast enough. Solution: divide, flatten, develop organelles, or fold membranes.

📌 Example (from textbook): A 1mm cube has SA:V = 6:1. A 2mm cube has SA:V = 3:1 — half as efficient per unit volume. Smaller = more efficient exchange.

✅ Reflect — What the SA:V ratio means for cells

Cells solve the SA:V problem in different ways: prokaryotes stay small; some cells become flat (like squamous epithelial cells); intestinal cells develop microvilli — fingerlike projections that massively increase surface area; and eukaryotic cells evolved membrane-bound organelles to compartmentalize functions internally.
