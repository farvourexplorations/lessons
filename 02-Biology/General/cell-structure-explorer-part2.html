Phase 1 · Wonder

🏭 The Cell as a City

A human cell contains about 2 meters of DNA, manufactures thousands of different proteins daily, detoxifies poisons, generates ATP, ships molecules to precise destinations, and communicates with neighbouring cells — all simultaneously, in a space smaller than a grain of sand.

A cell is like an entire city compressed into one microscopic unit. It has a city hall (nucleus), power plants (mitochondria), a postal system (Golgi apparatus and vesicles), recycling centres (lysosomes), roads (cytoskeleton), and walls (cell wall in plants). How does a cell manage to keep all of these systems organised and working together without getting confused — especially when some organelles need to "talk" to each other to complete a task?

✏️ Your Prediction (before we dig in)

The answer lies in the endomembrane system — a team of organelles (rough ER, Golgi apparatus, vesicles, lysosomes) that work together as an integrated protein processing and shipping network. Combined with the cytoskeleton for structural support and the cell junctions that let cells communicate with neighbours, eukaryotic cells are extraordinarily coordinated molecular machines.

Phase 2 · 4.3 Eukaryotic Cells — Organelles

📖 Organelles — Tap to Reveal

Tap each organelle to reveal its structure, function, and AP exam significance. The textbook specifically notes: "This is a rare case where you should create a list of organelles and their functions." Know every one of these cold.

Nucleus Ribosomes Mitochondria Chloroplasts Peroxisomes Vacuoles & Vesicles Lysosomes Centrosome & Centrioles Plant Cell Wall Endosymbiotic Theory
Nucleus — The cell's control centre, housing the DNA. Bounded by the nuclear envelope (a double phospholipid bilayer with nuclear pores). Inside: nucleoplasm (semi-solid fluid), chromatin (DNA + histone proteins), and the nucleolus (where ribosome subunits are assembled from rRNA and proteins). Nuclear pores control import/export of molecules. The nuclear envelope is continuous with the rough ER.
Ribosomes (80S in eukaryotes) — Found either free in the cytoplasm (make cytosolic proteins) or bound to the rough ER (make membrane/secreted proteins). Made of rRNA + protein in two subunits (large and small). Ribosomes in mitochondria and chloroplasts are 70S — like prokaryotic ribosomes, supporting endosymbiotic theory. Abundant in cells that synthesize lots of protein (e.g. pancreatic cells making digestive enzymes).
Mitochondria — Oval-shaped, double-membrane organelles — the "powerhouses" of the cell, producing most of the cell's ATP via cellular respiration. Inner membrane folded into cristae (increases surface area for ATP synthesis); interior = mitochondrial matrix. Have their own DNA and 70S ribosomes (evidence for endosymbiotic theory). Most abundant in high-energy cells like muscle cells.
Chloroplasts — Plant-only organelles that carry out photosynthesis. Double membrane; inner space contains stacked membrane sacs called thylakoids (stacks = grana), surrounded by stroma fluid. Contain chlorophyll (green pigment that captures light). Have their own DNA and 70S ribosomes — evidence for endosymbiotic theory (evolved from ingested cyanobacteria).
Peroxisomes — Small, round, single-membrane organelles. Oxidise fatty acids and amino acids; detoxify poisons (including alcohol in liver cells). Many oxidation reactions produce toxic H₂O₂ — peroxisomes contain the enzyme catalase, which safely converts H₂O₂ → H₂O + O₂, preventing cell damage. In plants: glyoxysomes are specialised peroxisomes that convert stored fats into sugars.
Vacuoles & Vesicles — Membrane-bound sacs for storage and transport. Vesicles (small) can fuse with other membranes and shuttle cargo around the cell. Vacuoles (larger) do not fuse with other membranes. Plant cells have a huge central vacuole (up to 90% of cell volume) that stores pigments, salts, nutrients, waste products, and maintains turgor pressure (keeps the plant rigid). Loss of water from the central vacuole = wilting.
Lysosomes (animal cells only) — The cell's "garbage disposal." Membrane-bound sacs containing ~50 hydrolytic (digestive) enzymes that function best at low pH (~4.5, kept acidic by proton pumps). Break down macromolecules (proteins, lipids, carbs, nucleic acids), recycle worn-out organelles (autophagy), and destroy pathogens (in immune cells like macrophages via phagocytosis). Plant cells use their central vacuole for these digestive functions instead.
Centrosome & Centrioles (animal cells) — The centrosome is the microtubule-organising centre (MTOC) located near the nucleus. Contains two centrioles oriented at right angles to each other — each centriole is a cylinder of 9 triplets of microtubules. Replicates before cell division and helps organise the mitotic spindle. Plant cells lack centrioles but can still divide (the centrioles are NOT the spindle itself).
Plant Cell Wall (cellulose) — A rigid layer external to the plasma membrane made primarily of cellulose (β-glucose polymer). Provides structural support, shape, and protection. Plant cells also have plasmodesmata — channels through the cell wall that connect adjacent plant cells, allowing communication and transport. Distinct from the peptidoglycan walls of bacteria.
Endosymbiotic Theory — Explains the origin of mitochondria and chloroplasts. Ancient host cells engulfed aerobic bacteria (which became mitochondria) and cyanobacteria (which became chloroplasts) without digesting them. Evidence: both organelles have their own circular DNA, their own 70S ribosomes, their own double membranes, and they reproduce by binary fission independently of cell division.

🏭 Rough Endoplasmic Reticulum (RER)

A network of interconnected membrane sacs studded with ribosomes on their cytoplasmic surface (giving the "rough" appearance under EM). The ribosomes synthesise proteins directly into the lumen (interior) of the RER, where they undergo structural modifications such as folding and addition of carbohydrate side chains. The RER also synthesises phospholipids for cellular membranes. Cells that secrete large amounts of protein (like pancreatic cells making digestive enzymes, or liver cells making blood proteins) have abundant RER.

📌 Route: Ribosome synthesises protein → protein folds inside RER lumen → transport vesicle buds off RER → vesicle travels to cis face of Golgi.

⚗️ Smooth Endoplasmic Reticulum (SER)

Continuous with the RER but lacks ribosomes. Functions: (1) synthesise carbohydrates, phospholipids, cholesterol, and steroid hormones; (2) detoxify medications and poisons (liver cells); (3) store calcium ions — especially important in muscle cells, where the specialised sarcoplasmic reticulum stores Ca²⁺ needed to trigger muscle contraction. Liver cells that detoxify drugs have extensive SER.

📌 A person who regularly takes medication will have liver cells with MORE SER — the SER proliferates in response to increased detoxification demand.

📮 The Golgi Apparatus

A stack of flattened membrane sacs (cisternae) that functions as the cell's postal sorting centre. Receives transport vesicles from the RER at its cis face; processes, tags (adds phosphate groups or other small molecules), and packages lipids and proteins; then ships them from its trans face in secretory vesicles. Destinations: plasma membrane (secretion), lysosomes, or other organelles. In plant cells, also synthesises polysaccharides for the cell wall.

📌 Cells with high secretory activity (salivary gland cells secreting digestive enzymes, immune cells secreting antibodies) have an abundance of Golgi apparatus.

🗑️ Lysosomes — Part of the Endomembrane System

Lysosomes bud off from the trans face of the Golgi, pre-loaded with hydrolytic enzymes. They maintain a low pH (~4.5) by pumping in H⁺ ions. Functions: (1) digest ingested macromolecules; (2) recycle worn-out organelles (autophagy); (3) destroy pathogens engulfed by immune cells via phagocytosis. In macrophages, a pathogen-containing vesicle fuses with a lysosome → enzymes destroy the pathogen.

📌 Tay-Sachs disease is caused by a missing lysosomal enzyme — lipids accumulate in neurons and cannot be broken down, causing progressive neurodegeneration.

🚢 Transport Vesicles

Small membrane-bound sacs that bud off from one organelle and fuse with another, carrying cargo (proteins, lipids) between compartments. The membrane of the vesicle is oriented so that the side that was inside the originating organelle faces the outside environment after fusing with the plasma membrane. Vesicles can fuse with: the plasma membrane (exocytosis/secretion), other organelles, or the cis face of the Golgi.

📌 Secretory pathway: RER → transport vesicle → Golgi (cis face) → modification → Golgi (trans face) → secretory vesicle → plasma membrane → released outside cell.

〰️ Microfilaments (Actin Filaments) — narrowest, 7 nm

Made of two intertwined strands of the globular protein actin. Actin is powered by ATP to assemble filamentous form. Functions: cellular movement (via myosin motor proteins), cell division (form contractile ring during cytokinesis), cytoplasmic streaming in plant cells, muscle contraction (actin + myosin). Concentrated just below the plasma membrane, helping define cell shape.

📌 AP tip: microfilaments + myosin = muscle contraction and cell division. Narrowest of the three types.

🔗 Intermediate Filaments — 8–10 nm

Made of various fibrous proteins depending on cell type — examples include keratin (epithelial cells, hair, nails), vimentin (connective tissue), and neurofilaments (neurons). Most stable and permanent of the three types — do NOT rapidly assemble/disassemble. Functions: resist mechanical stress, anchor the nucleus in place, maintain cell shape under tension. Desmosomes (cell junctions) connect to intermediate filaments.

📌 AP tip: intermediate filaments = structural stability and anchoring. No motor activity.

🧵 Microtubules — widest, 25 nm

Hollow tubes made of α- and β-tubulin protein dimers, with a characteristic 9+2 arrangement in cilia and flagella (9 pairs of fused microtubule doublets surrounding 2 central microtubules). Functions: maintain cell shape, form the mitotic spindle (separating chromosomes during division), serve as tracks for motor proteins (kinesin, dynein) to move vesicles and organelles, form cilia and flagella. Radiate outward from the centrosome (MTOC).

📌 AP tip: microtubules = cell division (spindle), vesicle transport, cilia/flagella structure. Widest, most dynamic.

🔒 Tight Junctions

Formed when membrane proteins of adjacent animal cells interlock in a zipper-like fashion, completely sealing the space between cells. Function: create a leak-proof barrier — nothing can pass between cells, only through them (transcellularly). Critical in intestinal lining (prevents undigested food from leaking into bloodstream), bladder, and blood-brain barrier. Cells cannot change neighbours in tissues with tight junctions.

📌 Think of tight junctions as the weatherstripping around a door — nothing leaks through the seal.

🧲 Desmosomes (Anchor Junctions)

Rivet-like protein structures that mechanically hold adjacent animal cells together without sealing the space between them. Formed by cadherin proteins in the plasma membrane that link to intermediate filaments (specifically keratin) in the cytoplasm. Found in tissues that must withstand mechanical stress — skin, cardiac muscle, uterine tissue. Allow cells to stay anchored to each other while permitting some flexibility.

📌 Think of desmosomes as the rivets on a pair of jeans — they hold things together tightly but leave gaps between the rivets.

📡 Gap Junctions (Communicating Junctions)

Channels made of connexin proteins that span both plasma membranes of adjacent animal cells, creating a direct cytoplasmic connection. Allow ions, small molecules, and signals to pass directly between cells without crossing the plasma membrane. Essential for coordinating cardiac muscle contractions (the electrical signal that makes your heart beat passes cell-to-cell through gap junctions), and for embryonic development.

📌 Think of gap junctions as phone lines between cells — direct, rapid communication. Your heartbeat depends on them.

🌿 Plasmodesmata (Plant Cell Equivalent)

Channels that pass through the cellulose cell walls of adjacent plant cells, lined with plasma membrane and containing a thin strand of ER called the desmotubule. Allow water, small solutes, and signalling molecules to move between plant cells. Function similarly to gap junctions in animals but are unique in structure to plants. Essential for coordinating plant development and responses to environmental signals.

📌 Plasmodesmata = plant equivalent of gap junctions. Through cell walls, not just membranes.
