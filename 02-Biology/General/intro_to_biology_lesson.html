A — Ecosystem 生态系统

All the living things in an area (plants, animals, bacteria…) plus the air, water, and soil around them, all interacting together. Example: a coral reef, a rainforest.

B — Population & community 种群 / 群落

A population is all the members of one species living in one place. Many populations living together form a community.

C — Organism 生物体

One complete living thing, built from many cells working together — for example, one tree, one dog, or one person.

D — Cell 细胞

The smallest basic unit of life. Some organisms are just one single cell, like bacteria. You are made of trillions of cells working together.

Living or non-living?

Meet the biologist's eye

Biologists are scientists who study living things — and for the rest of this course, that's exactly the job you're doing. To decide if something is alive, biologists look for these signs, called the properties of life (生命的特征):

Order 秩序 — a living thing has organised parts, like cells and organs.
Energy processing 能量处理 — it takes in and uses energy to live.
Growth & development 生长发育 — it grows and changes over time.
Response 反应 — it reacts to things around it.
Reproduction 繁殖 — it can make more of its own kind.
Regulation 调节 (稳态) — it keeps stable conditions inside itself.
A cheetah shows several properties of life at once: it moves and responds, it uses energy to run, and one day it will reproduce.

What is a cell?

Every living thing is built of cells

A cell is the smallest unit that is still alive. Biologists sort all cells into two big types — click each tab to compare them.

Simple cells, no nucleus 原核细胞

Prokaryotic cells are small and simple. They do not have a nucleus — their DNA just floats freely inside the cell. All bacteria and archaea are prokaryotes. These were the very first form of life on Earth, more than 3.5 billion years ago.

Example: E. coli — a common bacterium that lives inside your intestine.

E. coli bacteria seen with an electron microscope — a classic example of a prokaryotic cell.

Complex cells, with a nucleus 真核细胞

Eukaryotic cells are bigger and more complex. They have a real nucleus that keeps the DNA safe inside, plus many tiny structures called organelles (细胞器) — like tiny organs — that each do a special job. Plants, animals, fungi, and you are all made of eukaryotic cells.

Example: a plant cell — with a nucleus, a cell wall, and green chloroplasts for photosynthesis.

A plant cell — notice the nucleus and other organelles that prokaryotic cells do not have.

Why do biologists study ecosystems?

A coral reef looks like just colourful rock, but it is really thousands of living organisms working together — corals, fish, algae, and countless microbes — plus the water, sunlight, and nutrients around them. Studying an ecosystem means studying all of these parts and how they affect each other.

Charles Darwin 查尔斯·达尔文 — the scientist who explained how species change through natural selection.

Why evolution connects it all

Darwin showed that all life on Earth shares a common ancestor, and that species slowly change over many generations through evolution. This is why evolution 进化 is called biology's "core theme" — it explains both the unity we see (all cells use DNA) and the huge diversity of life (millions of different species).

Challenge

Mei and Tom's virus puzzle

Mei (美) and Tom are looking at pictures of a virus under a powerful microscope in class. A virus can copy itself, but only by using the machinery inside another cell.

"I think a virus is alive — it can make more of itself," says Mei.
"No, Mei — a virus isn't really alive," says Tom.

Who is correct?

Tom is closest to what most biologists say. A virus is not made of cells, has no way to process energy on its own, and cannot reproduce by itself — it must invade a host cell and use that cell's machinery. Because it is missing several properties of life, most biologists classify a virus as non-living — although scientists still debate this question today!
Compare: virus vs. cell

A living cell can: grow, use energy, respond, and reproduce on its own.

A virus can only copy itself — and only inside another living cell. Alone, it cannot do anything the properties of life describe.
