Harmful substances in the environment

The environment is everything around us. It includes air, water, soil and other living things. Some substances in the environment are harmful, or toxic, to living things. Tap each card to learn more.

1 How does the pesticide get into the food chain in the picture?

The farmer sprays the pesticide on the corn (the producer). This is how it enters the food chain.

2 How does the pesticide move through the food chain?

It moves when one living thing eats another. The snails eat corn leaves covered with pesticide. The bird eats the snails, so the pesticide goes into the bird. The wildcat eats the bird and takes in any pesticide still in the bird's body.

3 Redraw the food chain to show how the pesticide could be passed to people.

corn → snails → bird → person (for example, a farm chicken or duck eats the snails, and people eat the bird). People could also eat the sprayed corn: corn → person.

Toxic substances build up

Some toxic substances break down into harmless substances in the soil or water. Others do not break down. They stay in the bodies of living things and accumulate (build up) along the food chain. The predator at the end of the chain gets the most.

Play the food chain game

You will pretend to be producers and consumers in a rice field food chain. Pesticide “particles” show how toxic substances move through a food chain. No equipment is needed. Use your fingers and your notebook.

🙋 Act it out: no equipment needed
  1. Your teacher puts you in a group: 12 rice plants, 6 caterpillars, 3 frogs and 1 kingfisher. Everyone else is a scientist who records the results on the board.
  2. Rice plants take in pesticide from the field: each rice plant holds up some fingers (1 to 10). That is your number of particles. Say it out loud.
  3. Each caterpillar taps 2 rice plants (tapping = eating). Add their numbers together. The rice plants sit down.
  4. Each frog taps 2 caterpillars and adds their totals. Then the kingfisher taps all 3 frogs and adds their totals.
  5. Before each round, everyone predicts. After each round, the scientists write every player's total on the board.

Fact file: mercury in food chains

Almost all substances that contain mercury are toxic and can be dangerous, even at very low levels. Read the fact file together. Then answer the four questions in your notebook, and draw a food chain to help explain your answers.

📄 Mercury fact file

Where does it come from? Mercury is a metal. It gets into the environment when people burn coal in power stations, from mining (especially gold mining) and from factory waste. Volcanoes also release some mercury. Mercury in the air falls into rivers, lakes and the sea with the rain.

Into the food chain: In water, tiny living things change mercury into a form that is easily taken in by living things. Plant plankton and algae take it in from the water. This is how it enters the food chain, through the producers.

Moving along the chain: Animal plankton eat the plant plankton. Small fish eat the animal plankton. Big fish, such as tuna and sharks, eat many small fish. The body cannot get rid of mercury easily, so it accumulates. Big predator fish have the most mercury.

Why is it harmful? Mercury damages the brain and nerves. It is especially dangerous for babies and young children. People who eat a lot of big fish can take in a lot of mercury.

1 How does mercury get into the environment?

From burning coal in power stations, from mining, from factory waste and from volcanoes. It falls into water with the rain.

2 How does mercury get into food chains?

Plant plankton and algae (the producers) take it in from the water.

3 How does mercury move through food chains?

By eating. Each animal eats many of the living things before it. The body cannot get rid of mercury, so it accumulates, and big predator fish have the most.
Food chain: plant plankton → animal plankton → small fish → tuna → person

4 Why is mercury harmful to living things?

It damages the brain and nerves. It is especially dangerous for babies and young children.

The story of DDT

📄 DDT

DDT was one of the first and most powerful pesticides developed to kill insects. It was widely used to control the spread of malaria. Mosquitoes spread the malaria parasite when they bite people. DDT was also used a lot in the 1960s to spray crops, mostly in Europe and North America.

Much later, scientists discovered that DDT can move through food chains because animals' bodies cannot get rid of it. DDT is stored mainly in body fat. It also remains in the environment for a long time before it breaks down. Scientists also found that birds of prey, such as eagles and hawks, which are affected by DDT, lay eggs with very thin shells. Humans who eat plants or animals that contain DDT are more likely to develop cancer and other serious diseases.

DDT was banned in the 1980s and is no longer used as a pesticide.

a Why was DDT used a lot in the past?

To kill mosquitoes and stop the spread of malaria, and to kill insect pests on crops.

b i Explain how DDT can enter a food chain.   ii Why can DDT move through food chains?

i DDT was sprayed on crops and water. Plants and insects took it in, and then animals ate them.
ii Animals' bodies cannot get rid of DDT. It is stored in body fat, so it passes to the next animal when it is eaten.

c i Suggest two reasons why arctic animals such as seals and polar bears often have high levels of DDT in their bodies, even though DDT is no longer used.   ii Some people living in arctic regions eat seals. Why can this be dangerous for them?

i (1) DDT stays in the environment for a long time before it breaks down, and it can be carried to the Arctic by water and wind. (2) Seals and polar bears are near the top of long ocean food chains and eat many fish, so DDT stored in fat accumulates in their bodies.
ii The DDT in the seals passes into the people who eat them. People who take in DDT are more likely to develop cancer and other serious diseases.

d In a food chain affected by DDT, explain why a frog that eats three locusts does not die, but an owl that eats three frogs dies. What is the word that describes this?

The frog takes in the DDT from only 3 locusts, which is not enough to kill it. The owl eats 3 frogs, so it takes in the DDT from 9 locusts. The DDT builds up to a level that kills the owl. The word is accumulation (bioaccumulation).

e Why do you think the number of birds of prey in Europe and North America decreased a lot in the 1960s?

DDT was sprayed a lot on crops in the 1960s. It accumulated in birds of prey at the top of food chains. They laid eggs with very thin shells that broke easily, so fewer chicks hatched.

f Do some research to find out the full name of DDT.

Dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane
