Before we start

⚡ Why This Feels Hard (and Why That's Normal)

Cell respiration, photosynthesis, and cell signalling are three of the most process-heavy topics in the whole course — lots of steps, lots of similar-looking names (NAD⁺ vs NADP⁺, glycolysis vs the Calvin cycle), and diagrams that look intimidating at first glance. That's exactly why most students find them hard to self-study. You are not behind because you're slow — this material is genuinely dense.

🐢 Go at your own pace. Nothing here is timed. Re-read a box twice if you need to. Click things more than once. The goal by Friday isn't to memorise every arrow — it's to understand the logic of each process well enough that the details start making sense on their own.

🤔 Here's the big connecting idea for both C1 and C2: life runs on ATP, and almost everything in these three topics is really just answering one of two questions — "how do we make ATP?" (respiration, photosynthesis) or "how does a cell know what to do once it has that energy?" (cell signalling). Keep that thread in mind as you go.

Phase 1 · Cell Respiration — Core Vocabulary

🔥 Cell Respiration — Releasing Energy from Glucose

In plain English: cell respiration is the process of slowly, safely burning glucose inside a cell to make ATP — instead of releasing all the energy at once (like an explosion), the cell releases it in small, controlled steps. Click each term below to unlock a clear explanation.

Glycolysis — "Glucose splitting." Happens in the cytoplasm (not the mitochondrion!) of every cell, with or without oxygen. One 6-carbon glucose is broken down into two 3-carbon pyruvate molecules. Net result per glucose: 2 ATP (made directly) and 2 NADH (electron carriers, loaded up for later). Think of it as the universal first step — every cell does this.
Link Reaction (Pyruvate Oxidation) — Each pyruvate moves into the mitochondrial matrix and loses one carbon as CO₂ (that's decarboxylation), becoming a 2-carbon acetyl group, which attaches to coenzyme A to form acetyl CoA. Also produces one NADH per pyruvate. This is the "bridge" between glycolysis and the Krebs cycle — hence the name.
Krebs Cycle (Citric Acid Cycle) — Happens in the matrix. Acetyl CoA combines with a 4-carbon molecule to form citrate (6-carbon), which is then gradually broken back down, releasing 2 CO₂ per turn and regenerating the starting 4-carbon molecule so the cycle can repeat. Per acetyl CoA it yields 3 NADH, 1 FADH₂, and 1 ATP (made directly by substrate-level phosphorylation). Because 1 glucose gives 2 acetyl CoA, the cycle turns twice per glucose.
Electron Transport Chain (ETC) — Located in the inner mitochondrial membrane (cristae). NADH and FADH₂ drop off their electrons here. As electrons pass along a chain of protein carriers, energy is released and used to pump H⁺ ions from the matrix into the intermembrane space. At the very end, oxygen accepts the electrons (plus H⁺) to form water — this is why we need to breathe! No oxygen = the chain backs up and stops.
Chemiosmosis — The H⁺ ions pumped into the intermembrane space build up a steep concentration gradient. They can only get back into the matrix by flowing through a channel enzyme called ATP synthase, and that flow of H⁺ physically spins part of the enzyme, driving the synthesis of ATP from ADP + Pᵢ. This is oxidative phosphorylation — "oxidative" because it depends on the oxygen-driven ETC. Same core mechanism you'll see again in photosynthesis.
NAD⁺/NADH and FAD/FADH₂ — These are electron carriers ("energy taxis"). NAD⁺ picks up 2 electrons + 1 H⁺ to become NADH; FAD picks up electrons to become FADH₂. They shuttle their cargo to the ETC and drop it off, going back to their empty (oxidised) form to be reused. FADH₂ enters the ETC at a slightly later point than NADH, so it contributes slightly less ATP per molecule.
Anaerobic Respiration (Fermentation) — When oxygen is unavailable, the ETC can't run (no final electron acceptor), so NADH piles up and glycolysis would grind to a halt. Fermentation regenerates NAD⁺ so glycolysis can keep going, producing only the 2 ATP from glycolysis itself. In animals: pyruvate → lactate. In yeast/plants: pyruvate → ethanol + CO₂. Much less efficient than aerobic respiration.
Total ATP Yield — Roughly 30–32 ATP per glucose via aerobic respiration (textbooks vary slightly depending on the shuttle system used to move electrons into the mitochondrion), compared to just 2 ATP from anaerobic fermentation. The vast majority of ATP comes from the ETC + chemiosmosis, not from glycolysis or the Krebs cycle directly — that's the whole point of the electron transport chain.

Phase 3 · Photosynthesis — Core Vocabulary

☀️ Photosynthesis — Capturing Light as Chemical Energy

In plain English: photosynthesis is respiration's mirror image — instead of breaking glucose down to release energy, plants build up glucose using energy captured from sunlight. It happens in two connected stages inside the chloroplast.

Light-Dependent Reactions — Occur in the thylakoid membrane. Chlorophyll absorbs light energy, which excites electrons. Those excited electrons pass along an electron transport chain (very similar idea to respiration!), pumping H⁺ into the thylakoid lumen. H⁺ then flows out through ATP synthase, making ATP (this step is called photophosphorylation). Meanwhile, NADP⁺ is reduced to NADPH. Both ATP and NADPH are handed off to the Calvin cycle.
Photolysis — "Light splitting." Water (H₂O) is split at Photosystem II to replace the electrons that chlorophyll lost to the ETC. This produces O₂ (released as waste — the oxygen we breathe!) and H⁺ ions. This is the source of literally all the oxygen gas plants release.
Photosystem II then Photosystem I — Despite the confusing numbering (II acts first!), light hits Photosystem II first, exciting electrons that travel down the ETC to Photosystem I, where they get re-excited by a second burst of light and are used to reduce NADP⁺ to NADPH. Each photosystem is a cluster of chlorophyll + proteins tuned to absorb specific wavelengths.
Calvin Cycle (Light-Independent Reactions) — Occurs in the stroma. Doesn't need light directly, but does need the ATP and NADPH made by the light-dependent reactions, so in practice it still stops in the dark. CO₂ is "fixed" onto RuBP, and using ATP + NADPH the resulting molecules are reduced into G3P (triose phosphate) — some of which is used to make glucose and other organic molecules, and some of which regenerates RuBP so the cycle can continue.
Rubisco & RuBP — RuBP (ribulose bisphosphate, 5-carbon) is the molecule that CO₂ attaches to. Rubisco is the enzyme that catalyses this fixation step — it's thought to be the most abundant enzyme on Earth, because every plant needs huge amounts of it (it's a slow, inefficient enzyme, so lots of copies are needed). The unstable 6-carbon product immediately splits into two 3-carbon molecules of glycerate 3-phosphate (GP).
Limiting Factors — The rate of photosynthesis is controlled by whichever factor is in shortest supply: light intensity, CO₂ concentration, or temperature. Increasing a non-limiting factor won't speed anything up — only increasing the actual limiting factor will. This is Blackman's Law of Limiting Factors. Try the slider lab in the next section to see this in action.

Phase 5 · Cell Signalling

📡 Cell Signalling — How Cells Talk to Each Other

In plain English: a signalling cell releases a "message" (a chemical), and a target cell has a "mailbox" (a receptor) shaped to receive exactly that message. What happens next depends entirely on what kind of mailbox the cell has.

Diagram: a ligand released by one cell binds a specific receptor, triggering a signal transduction pathway and a response.
ligand receptor response
Ligand & Receptor
Cell-Surface Receptors
Intracellular Receptors
Signal Transduction
Second Messengers

Ligand & Receptor — A ligand is any signalling molecule (hormone, neurotransmitter, growth factor, local chemical). A receptor is a protein shaped to bind that specific ligand, like a lock and key. Whether the receptor is on the cell surface or inside the cell depends on whether the ligand can cross the (lipid) plasma membrane on its own.
Cell-Surface Receptors — Used for ligands that can't cross the membrane (usually water-soluble, e.g. peptide hormones, neurotransmitters). Three key types: ion channel-linked (ligand binding opens a channel, ions flood in/out — fast, used at synapses), G-protein coupled receptors (GPCRs) (ligand binding activates a G-protein, which activates an enzyme that produces a second messenger like cAMP), and enzyme-linked receptors (e.g. receptor tyrosine kinases — ligand binding directly switches on enzyme activity, starting a phosphorylation cascade).
Intracellular Receptors — Used for ligands that can cross the membrane — typically small, lipid-soluble molecules like steroid hormones (e.g. oestrogen, testosterone, cortisol). These diffuse straight through the membrane and bind a receptor inside the cytoplasm or nucleus. The hormone-receptor complex often acts directly as a transcription factor, switching genes on or off — slower than surface receptors, but longer-lasting effects.
Signal Transduction Pathway — The chain of molecular events that converts "ligand bound to receptor" into an actual cell response (e.g. an enzyme switching on, a gene being transcribed, a muscle contracting). Often involves a cascade of proteins activating other proteins one after another — each step can amplify the signal.
Second Messengers — Small molecules that relay the signal inside the cell once the receptor (the "first messenger" receiver) has been activated. Classic example: a GPCR activates an enzyme called adenylyl cyclase, which converts ATP into cAMP (cyclic AMP) — cAMP then activates protein kinase A, which phosphorylates target proteins. Other second messengers include Ca²⁺ and IP₃.
Signal Amplification — One ligand binding one receptor can end up activating thousands of downstream molecules, because each activated enzyme in a cascade can activate many copies of the next enzyme. This is why hormones like adrenaline can be effective at extremely low concentrations — a tiny signal produces a huge, fast, body-wide response (e.g. the "fight-or-flight" glycogen breakdown response in liver cells).

⚡ Ion Channel-Linked Receptor

Ligand binds → channel physically opens → ions (e.g. Na⁺, Cl⁻) flow across the membrane immediately. This is the fastest type of signalling — milliseconds — because there's no enzyme cascade in between.

📌 Neurotransmitter receptors at a synapse: the neurotransmitter binds and ion channels open instantly, changing the voltage across the postsynaptic membrane.

🔄 G-Protein Coupled Receptor (GPCR)

Ligand binds outside → receptor changes shape → activates a G-protein on the inside of the membrane → G-protein activates an enzyme (e.g. adenylyl cyclase) → second messenger (cAMP) is produced → cascade continues inside the cell.

📌 Adrenaline binding a GPCR on a liver cell triggers a cAMP cascade that rapidly breaks down glycogen into glucose — part of the "fight-or-flight" response.

🧩 Enzyme-Linked Receptor

The receptor itself has (or activates) enzyme activity — usually a kinase, which adds phosphate groups to proteins. Ligand binding triggers this enzyme activity directly, starting a phosphorylation cascade inside the cell.

📌 Insulin binds its receptor (a receptor tyrosine kinase) on muscle/fat cells, triggering a cascade that moves glucose transporters to the cell surface.

🧬 Intracellular Receptor

The ligand itself crosses the membrane (it's lipid-soluble), so no cell-surface receptor is needed at all. The receptor is inside the cytoplasm or nucleus, and the ligand-receptor complex typically binds DNA directly to switch gene expression on or off.

📌 Cortisol and other steroid hormones diffuse into target cells and alter which genes are transcribed — effects take longer to appear but can last much longer.
