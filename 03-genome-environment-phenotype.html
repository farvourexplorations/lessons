📦 How They All Fit Together

Think of it like a library: the genome is the entire library of DNA. Each chromosome is one bookshelf. Each gene is one book on that shelf, containing the instructions to build one specific protein. A human body cell contains 46 chromosomes (23 pairs) — that's 46 "shelves" holding roughly 20,000 genes in total.

Core Genetics Vocabulary

Genotype, Phenotype & Alleles

These seven words appear in almost every genetics question on the exam. Tap each chip to reveal its definition, then study the worked example.

Genotype Phenotype Allele Dominant Recessive Homozygous Heterozygous
Genotype — The genetic make-up of an organism for a particular characteristic: the actual combination of alleles it carries, e.g. Bb. Written as letters, not visible to the eye.
Phenotype — The observable physical or biochemical characteristic that results from an organism's genotype and its environment, e.g. purple flowers. This is what you can actually see or measure.
Allele — One of the alternative versions of a gene. E.g. the gene for pea flower colour has a "purple" allele and a "white" allele — both code for the same characteristic but produce different versions of it.
Dominant — An allele whose characteristic appears in the phenotype even when only one copy is present (i.e. in a heterozygote). Shown with a capital letter, e.g. B.
Recessive — An allele whose characteristic only appears in the phenotype when two copies are present (homozygous recessive). Shown with a lowercase letter, e.g. b. Masked by a dominant allele if present.
Homozygous — Having two identical alleles for a gene, e.g. BB (homozygous dominant) or bb (homozygous recessive).
Heterozygous — Having two different alleles for a gene, e.g. Bb. The dominant allele's characteristic is the one seen in the phenotype.

🌸 Worked Example — Pea Flower Colour

Purple flower colour (B) is dominant to white flower colour (b). A heterozygous plant (Bb) is crossed with a homozygous recessive plant (bb):

×Bb
bBbbb
bBbbb

Genotype ratio: 1 Bb : 1 bb. Phenotype ratio: 1 purple : 1 white (50% purple, 50% white) — because Bb is heterozygous and B is dominant, so it looks purple even though it carries a hidden b allele.

When DNA Changes

Mutations — What Happens When DNA Changes?

A mutation is a change in the base sequence of DNA. Because a gene's base sequence determines the order of amino acids in a protein, a mutation can change the protein that gene produces.

🔗 The Chain of Cause and Effect

DNA base sequence changes
→ order of amino acids in the protein may change
→ the protein's shape may change
→ the phenotype may change.

Each arrow is a "may" — a change at any step is not guaranteed to reach the next one.

📊 How Much Does a Mutation Matter?

Most mutations have no effect on phenotype at all — often because they don't change the amino acid produced, or they occur in a part of the DNA that isn't used.

Some mutations have a small effect on phenotype.

Very rarely, a mutation has a significant effect on phenotype — for example if it changes the shape of an enzyme's active site so it can no longer bind its substrate.

☢️ What Increases Mutation Rate?

Exposure to ionising radiation increases the rate at which mutations occur. Examples include gamma rays, X-rays, and ultraviolet (UV) radiation from the sun. Certain chemicals (mutagens) can also increase mutation rate.
