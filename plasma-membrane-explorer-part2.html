Phase 1 · Wonder

⚡ Swimming Upstream — When Cells Work Against the Current

Passive transport is easy — molecules just drift downhill. But sometimes a cell absolutely must move something uphill — against its concentration gradient. That takes energy, precision machinery, and some clever chemistry.

Your nerve cells fire because sodium ions rush IN and potassium ions rush OUT — then pumps immediately restore the original balance. This happens hundreds of times per second in every neuron you have. If a cell is constantly pumping ions against their gradients, spending massive amounts of ATP, why not just let them distribute evenly? What would happen if the sodium-potassium pump stopped working?

✏️ Your Prediction (before we dig in)

The answer: the carefully maintained ion gradient would collapse — Na⁺ would flood in, K⁺ would leak out, and the electrical potential would equalise. With no gradient, nerve cells cannot fire, heart muscle cannot contract, and kidney cells cannot reabsorb glucose. When cells need to move things in bulk — whole proteins, pathogens, or waste — they use a completely different approach: bulk transport, wrapping cargo in membrane vesicles.

Phase 2 · 5.3 Active Transport

📖 Active Transport — Core Vocabulary

Tap each term to reveal its definition. Active transport is one of the most tested AP Biology concepts — know the distinction between primary and secondary, and the Na⁺-K⁺ pump in detail.

Active Transport Electrochemical Gradient Primary Active Transport Na⁺-K⁺ ATPase Pump Electrogenic Pump Secondary Active Transport Co-transport & Symporters Uniporter / Symporter / Antiporter
Active Transport — Movement of substances against their concentration or electrochemical gradient — from low to high concentration. Requires energy (ATP) and specific carrier proteins acting as pumps. Examples: Na⁺-K⁺ ATPase, H⁺-K⁺ ATPase, Ca²⁺ ATPase. Unlike facilitated diffusion, active transport can move things uphill. Sensitive to metabolic poisons that block ATP production.
Electrochemical Gradient — The combined effect of a substance's concentration gradient AND its electrical gradient (difference in charge). For ions, both gradients must be considered. Na⁺ is driven into cells by BOTH gradients: higher concentration outside AND the negative interior attracts positive Na⁺. K⁺ is more complex — its concentration drives it OUT, but the negative interior pulls it IN.
Primary Active Transport — Directly uses ATP hydrolysis to move ions across the membrane, creating a charge difference. The Na⁺-K⁺ ATPase pump is the classic example — it uses ATP directly to move 3 Na⁺ OUT and 2 K⁺ IN per cycle. Creates the electrochemical gradient that powers secondary active transport.
Na⁺-K⁺ ATPase (Sodium-Potassium Pump) — An antiporter that moves 3 Na⁺ OUT and 2 K⁺ IN per ATP molecule hydrolysed. 6-step cycle: Na⁺ binds inside → ATP hydrolysed → phosphate attached → pump changes shape → Na⁺ released outside → K⁺ binds → phosphate released → pump returns → K⁺ released inside. Net result: interior becomes slightly more negative (electrogenic effect). Critical for nerve cell membrane potential.
Electrogenic Pump — A pump that creates a net charge difference across the membrane by moving unequal numbers of ions. The Na⁺-K⁺ pump moves 3 positive charges out for every 2 positive charges in — making the cell interior more negative. This contributes to the resting membrane potential (~-70 mV in neurons). In plants, H⁺ ATPase pumps are the major electrogenic pumps.
Secondary Active Transport — Uses the electrochemical gradient created by primary active transport (not ATP directly) to move other substances against their gradients. Indirectly depends on ATP. Example: glucose and amino acids ride the Na⁺ gradient into intestinal cells via Na⁺-glucose symporters. The Na⁺-K⁺ pump maintains the Na⁺ gradient that drives this.
Co-transport (Symporter) — A type of secondary active transport where two substances move in the same direction. Na⁺ moves DOWN its gradient into the cell, dragging glucose or amino acids with it AGAINST their gradients. Used in the small intestine to absorb glucose, and in kidney tubules to reabsorb glucose from urine. ATP synthase in mitochondria also uses H⁺ co-transport.
Uniporter / Symporter / Antiporter — Three types of carrier proteins: Uniporter: carries one specific ion or molecule in one direction. Symporter: carries two different molecules in the same direction (e.g., Na⁺ + glucose into cell). Antiporter: carries two different molecules in opposite directions (e.g., Na⁺-K⁺ ATPase moves Na⁺ out while K⁺ comes in).

😋 Phagocytosis — "Cell Eating"

The cell membrane invaginates and completely surrounds a large particle (bacteria, dead cells, debris) forming a large vesicle called a phagosome. The phagosome then fuses with a lysosome, whose hydrolytic enzymes digest the contents. Primary role: immune defence — neutrophils and macrophages engulf and destroy pathogens. Also used by protists like Amoeba for feeding.

📌 When bacteria invade the body, white blood cells (neutrophils) destroy them via phagocytosis. The phagosome fuses with a lysosome to form a phagolysosome where digestion occurs.

💧 Pinocytosis — "Cell Drinking"

The cell takes in small droplets of extracellular fluid by forming small vesicles (much smaller than phagosomes). The cell non-specifically ingests water and small solutes dissolved in it. A variation called potocytosis uses a protein called caveolin on the cytoplasmic side to form even smaller vesicles. Unlike receptor-mediated endocytosis, pinocytosis is non-selective.

📌 Pinocytosis vesicles do NOT need to fuse with lysosomes because the contents are small solutes, not large particles requiring digestion.

🎯 Receptor-Mediated Endocytosis — Targeted Uptake

Specific receptor proteins on the cell surface bind to a particular molecule (the ligand). Once bound, the membrane invaginates and forms a vesicle coated with the protein clathrin, which concentrates the receptors and cargo. This allows the cell to take in specific molecules at much higher concentrations than pinocytosis. Examples: LDL (cholesterol) uptake, insulin receptor binding, iron uptake via transferrin.

📌 Familial hypercholesterolaemia: defective LDL receptor proteins prevent LDL clearance from the blood → dangerous build-up of cholesterol → very early heart disease.

🚀 Exocytosis — Secretion Out of the Cell

A membrane vesicle inside the cell fuses with the plasma membrane and releases its contents to the extracellular space. The vesicle membrane becomes part of the plasma membrane. Used for: secreting proteins (insulin, antibodies, digestive enzymes), releasing neurotransmitters at synapses, secreting extracellular matrix components, and expelling waste. The entire secretory pathway (RER → Golgi → vesicle) ends in exocytosis.

📌 Neurotransmitter release at a synapse = exocytosis. The synaptic vesicle fuses with the presynaptic membrane, releasing neurotransmitters into the synaptic cleft.
