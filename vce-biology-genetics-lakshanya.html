Warm-Up · Wonder

🧬 Why Don't You Look Exactly Like Either Parent?

You share DNA with both of your parents, yet you're not identical to either of them — and if you have siblings, you're not identical to them either (unless you're an identical twin). Every cell in your body carries the same genome, but somewhere between your parents' cells and yours, new combinations were shuffled into existence.

Your parents each carry two copies of every gene, but they only pass on one copy to you — and which copy gets passed on is partly down to chance. Add in the shuffling that happens during meiosis, and every egg or sperm cell your parents ever produced was genetically unique. That's why siblings — other than identical twins — are never genetically identical, even with the same two parents.

7A · Genes & Chromosomes

🧬 Genes, Alleles & Chromosomes

Every instruction for building and running your body is stored as DNA, organised into genes, which are packaged into chromosomes. Getting these levels of organisation straight — and knowing the difference between a gene and an allele — is the foundation for everything else in this chapter.

Humans have 46 chromosomes arranged as 23 homologous pairs — one member of each pair from your mother, one from your father. 22 pairs are autosomes (non-sex chromosomes) and one pair is the sex chromosomes (XX in females, XY in males). A gene is a section of DNA coding for a particular characteristic; an allele is one version of that gene.

🧬 Gene

A section of DNA that codes for a particular characteristic, usually by providing the instructions to build a specific protein. Humans have roughly 20,000 genes. Each gene occupies a specific locus (position) on a chromosome.

📌 VCE: Don't confuse a gene with an allele — the gene is the "category" (e.g. eye colour), the allele is the specific "version" (e.g. brown or blue).

🔀 Allele

One of two or more alternative forms of the same gene, occupying the same locus on homologous chromosomes. Because you have two copies of every autosomal gene (one from each parent), you have two alleles for every autosomal gene — which may be the same or different.

📌 Example: the gene for pea plant height has a "tall" allele (T) and a "short" allele (t).

🧵 Chromosome

A single, very long molecule of DNA tightly coiled around proteins (histones), carrying hundreds to thousands of genes. Chromosomes are only visible as distinct rod-like structures under a microscope during cell division — the rest of the time DNA exists as loosely packed chromatin.

📌 Humans: 46 chromosomes = 23 pairs. Different species have different chromosome numbers — this is not linked to complexity.

👯 Homologous Pair

Two chromosomes — one inherited from each parent — that are the same size, have the same banding pattern, and carry genes for the same characteristics at the same loci (though the alleles may differ). One homologous pair is not identical, since each member may carry different alleles.

📌 VCE: Homologous ≠ identical. They match in structure and gene content, not necessarily in allele content.

🔢 Autosomes

The 22 pairs of chromosomes (44 chromosomes total in humans) that are not involved in determining biological sex. They carry the vast majority of your genes — for everything from blood type to enzyme production.

📌 Autosomal inheritance patterns (like most monohybrid crosses) don't depend on whether the offspring is male or female.

⚥ Sex Chromosomes

The 23rd pair, which determines biological sex. Females typically have two X chromosomes (XX); males typically have one X and one Y (XY). The Y chromosome is much smaller than the X and carries far fewer genes — this is why X-linked conditions affect males and females differently.

📌 Since males have only one X, any recessive allele on their single X will be expressed — they can't be "carriers" the way females can.

📚 Genome

The complete set of genetic material (all the DNA) belonging to an organism, including every gene on every chromosome. The Human Genome Project (completed 2003) mapped the full sequence of human DNA.

📌 Every cell in your body (with a nucleus) contains a full copy of your genome — but different cells switch different genes on and off.

🖼️ Karyotype

A photograph or image of an individual's chromosomes, arranged in matching homologous pairs by size, from largest (pair 1) to smallest (pair 22), with the sex chromosomes shown last. Karyotyping is used to detect chromosomal abnormalities — such as an extra chromosome 21 (trisomy 21, Down syndrome).

📌 A karyotype shows chromosome number and structure — but not the specific alleles an individual carries.

7B · Meiosis

🔬 Meiosis — Making Gametes

Meiosis is a special type of cell division that produces gametes (sperm and egg cells) with half the number of chromosomes of a normal body cell — and it's the source of almost all genetic variation between siblings.

Body cells (produced by mitosis) are diploid (2n) — they have chromosomes in pairs. Gametes must be haploid (n) — just one of each pair — so that when a sperm (n) fertilises an egg (n), the resulting zygote is diploid (2n) again. Meiosis is how the chromosome number is halved, and it involves two divisions, not one.

➕ Interphase (Before Meiosis Begins)
The cell grows and each chromosome is replicated, so it now consists of two identical sister chromatids joined at a centromere. The cell still has 46 chromosomes (23 pairs) at this point — just each one is doubled.
↔️ Meiosis I — The Reduction Division
Homologous chromosomes pair up (synapsis) and crossing over can occur — sections of DNA are swapped between homologs, creating new allele combinations. The homologous pairs then line up randomly at the equator (independent assortment) before being pulled to opposite poles. The cell divides into two cells, each now haploid but with chromosomes still doubled (sister chromatids attached).
✂️ Meiosis II — Like Mitosis, But Haploid
Very similar to mitosis: the sister chromatids of each chromosome are pulled apart and separated into new cells. No further reduction in chromosome number happens here — this step just separates the sister chromatids that were created back in interphase.
🧫 Result — Four Genetically Unique Haploid Gametes
Meiosis produces four haploid daughter cells, each genetically different from the others and from the parent cell, thanks to crossing over and independent assortment. In males this produces four functional sperm; in females it typically produces one functional egg and smaller polar bodies that don't develop further.

🧫 Mitosis vs Meiosis

FeatureMitosisMeiosis
PurposeGrowth & repair — makes body cellsMakes gametes for sexual reproduction
Number of divisionsOneTwo (Meiosis I and II)
Daughter cells2 diploid (2n), genetically identical4 haploid (n), genetically unique
Genetic variationNone — clones of parent cellCrossing over + independent assortment
Where it occursThroughout the bodyOnly in reproductive organs (testes/ovaries)
Crossing Over Independent Assortment Non-Disjunction
Crossing Over — During prophase I, homologous chromosomes pair up closely and exchange sections of DNA at points called chiasmata. This creates chromosomes with new combinations of alleles that neither parent chromosome had — a major source of genetic variation.
Independent Assortment — During metaphase I, each homologous pair lines up at the equator independently of the others, so the combination of maternal and paternal chromosomes that ends up in each gamete is random. With 23 pairs, this alone creates over 8 million possible chromosome combinations per gamete.
Non-Disjunction — An error where homologous chromosomes (or sister chromatids) fail to separate properly during meiosis, producing gametes with an abnormal chromosome number. If a gamete with an extra chromosome 21 is fertilised, the result is trisomy 21 (Down syndrome).

7C · Genotypes & Phenotypes

🧪 Genotype, Phenotype & How Alleles Interact

Two individuals can carry different combinations of alleles (a different genotype) yet look the same (the same phenotype) — or vice versa. Understanding dominant, recessive, homozygous and heterozygous is essential before you can predict any cross.

Genotype Phenotype Dominant Allele Recessive Allele Homozygous & Heterozygous Incomplete Dominance Codominance
Genotype — The actual combination of alleles an individual carries for a gene, written using letters (e.g. Bb, TT, tt). The genotype is the genetic "recipe," but it isn't necessarily visible.
Phenotype — The observable physical, biochemical or behavioural characteristic that results from a genotype (and often the environment too), e.g. brown eyes, blood type A, tallness in pea plants.
Dominant Allele — An allele that is expressed in the phenotype whenever it is present, even with only one copy (heterozygous). Written with a capital letter, e.g. T for tall.
Recessive Allele — An allele that is only expressed in the phenotype when two copies are present (homozygous recessive) — its effect is "masked" by a dominant allele if one is present. Written with a lowercase letter, e.g. t for short.
Homozygous — Both alleles for a gene are the same (e.g. TT or tt). Heterozygous — the two alleles are different (e.g. Tt) — sometimes called a "carrier" for a recessive condition.
Incomplete Dominance — Neither allele is fully dominant; the heterozygous phenotype is a blend of both. E.g. red (RR) × white (R′R′) snapdragons → pink (RR′) offspring.
Codominance — Both alleles are fully expressed at the same time, with neither masking the other, so both phenotypes show simultaneously. E.g. the IA and IB alleles in the ABO blood group system — a person with both alleles has blood type AB, showing both A and B antigens.

7D · Nature vs Nurture

🌱 Nature vs Nurture — Genes & Environment

Not every characteristic is determined by genes alone. Most traits result from an interaction between an organism's genotype (nature) and the environment it experiences (nurture) — and this is a common source of exam confusion, so it's worth being precise.

Identical twins share 100% of their DNA. If a characteristic were purely genetic, identical twins would always match perfectly for it. But studies consistently show identical twins can differ in things like height, weight, intelligence test scores, and disease risk — evidence that the environment (diet, illness, upbringing, chance) also shapes the phenotype, even when the genotype is identical.

Nature Nurture Epigenetics Twin Studies
Nature — The genetic contribution to a trait; the influence of the genotype an individual inherited from their parents. Traits controlled almost entirely by genes (e.g. blood type, eye colour in simple models) are described as largely due to nature.
Nurture — The environmental contribution to a trait — including diet, exposure to toxins, sunlight, education, illness and social experiences. Traits like body weight, skin tone (from sun exposure), muscle development and academic performance are strongly shaped by nurture.
Epigenetics — Changes in gene expression (whether a gene is switched on or off) caused by the environment, without any change to the underlying DNA sequence. Epigenetic changes can sometimes be triggered by diet, stress or chemical exposure, and in some cases can even be passed on to offspring.
Twin Studies — Comparing identical twins (100% shared DNA) to fraternal twins (~50% shared DNA, like regular siblings), especially twins raised apart, lets researchers estimate how much of a trait's variation is due to genes versus environment. If identical twins are consistently more similar than fraternal twins for a trait, that trait has a stronger genetic component.

8A · Monohybrid Crosses

🟣 Monohybrid Crosses & Punnett Squares

A monohybrid cross tracks the inheritance of a single gene between two parents, using a Punnett square to predict the possible genotypes and phenotypes of the offspring.

📋 How to Set Up a Monohybrid Cross

1
Define the Alleles
Choose a letter for the gene, using the capital for the dominant allele and lowercase for the recessive allele. E.g. T = tall (dominant), t = short (recessive).
VCE: Always state your allele key explicitly in an exam answer — markers need to see you've defined which letter means what.
2
Write the Parental Genotypes
Determine or use the given genotype of each parent, e.g. Tt (tall, heterozygous) × Tt (tall, heterozygous). Each parent contributes one allele per gene to each gamete.
3
Set Up the Punnett Square
Write one parent's possible gametes (alleles) across the top, and the other parent's possible gametes down the side. Fill in each box by combining the row and column allele — this gives every possible offspring genotype.
4
Read Off the Ratios
Count the genotypes in the grid to get the genotypic ratio, then apply dominance rules to get the phenotypic ratio. For Tt × Tt, the classic result is a 1:2:1 genotypic ratio (TT:Tt:tt) and a 3:1 phenotypic ratio (tall:short).
VCE: Always give BOTH the genotypic and phenotypic ratio unless the question asks for only one — they are frequently marked separately.

🔲 Worked Example: Tt × Tt

Tt
TTTTt
tTttt

Genotypic ratio: 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt. Since T is dominant, both TT and Tt are tall — giving a phenotypic ratio of 3 tall : 1 short.

8B · Dihybrid Crosses

🔵 Dihybrid Crosses — Two Genes at Once

A dihybrid cross tracks the inheritance of two different genes at the same time — for example, seed shape and seed colour in pea plants. It relies on Mendel's Law of Independent Assortment: alleles for different genes are inherited independently of one another (assuming the genes are on different chromosomes, or far apart on the same one).

Because each parent is heterozygous for two genes (e.g. RrYy), each parent can produce four different types of gametes (RY, Ry, rY, ry) rather than just two. This means a dihybrid Punnett square needs a 4 × 4 grid — 16 boxes — instead of the 2 × 2 grid used for a monohybrid cross.

📋 Setting Up a Dihybrid Cross

1
Define Both Genes' Alleles
E.g. R = round seed (dominant), r = wrinkled seed (recessive); Y = yellow seed (dominant), y = green seed (recessive).
2
List All Possible Gametes for Each Parent
For a doubly heterozygous parent RrYy, the four possible gametes are RY, Ry, rY, ry — one allele from each gene, in every combination.
VCE: Listing gametes in this order (using the FOIL-style pattern) reduces careless errors when filling in the grid.
3
Build the 4×4 Grid
Write one parent's four gamete types across the top, the other's down the side, and fill in all 16 boxes by combining each row and column gamete.
4
Read Off the Classic 9:3:3:1 Ratio
For a cross between two double heterozygotes (RrYy × RrYy), the phenotypic ratio is always 9 round-yellow : 3 round-green : 3 wrinkled-yellow : 1 wrinkled-green — as long as the two genes assort independently.
VCE: The 9:3:3:1 ratio is worth memorising as the signature result of a dihybrid cross between two double heterozygotes — recognising it quickly saves time in exams.

🌳 Reading Pedigree Charts

A pedigree chart is a diagram showing how a trait or genetic condition is inherited across generations of a family. Being able to read one — and identify the inheritance pattern it shows — is a core VCE Biology skill.

Look for: the trait appears in every generation, and an affected individual usually has at least one affected parent.
