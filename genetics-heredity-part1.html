Phase 1 — Wonder

🌿 What Makes You, You?

Every cell in your body carries approximately 3.2 billion base pairs of DNA — a molecule so thin that if you stretched it out, the DNA from a single cell would reach about 2 metres. All of human heredity, from eye colour to disease risk, is written in that molecule.

✨ Two siblings can share the same parents but look and act completely differently. A mutation of just one single nucleotide can cause sickle cell anaemia. The DNA of a human and a chimpanzee differs by less than 1.2%. What does that tell us about how genetic information works?

This workshop covers B1.1.1–B1.1.9 of the IB DP Biology 2023 syllabus: nucleotide structure, DNA double helix, complementary base pairing, antiparallel strands, genes, alleles, the genome, and mutations. SL and HL content is labelled throughout.

Phase 2 · B1.1.1–B1.1.4 — DNA Structure

🐱 The DNA Double Helix SL

DNA is a double-stranded polynucleotide. Its structure — discovered by Watson, Crick, Franklin and Wilkins in 1953 — directly explains how genetic information is stored and copied. Every IB exam tests nucleotide structure, base pairing, and the antiparallel arrangement.

Key structural facts the IB tests:
• DNA is made of nucleotides: deoxyribose sugar + phosphate + nitrogenous base
• Purines (A, G) always pair with pyrimidines (T, C) — Chargaff's rules: A=T, G=C
• The two strands are antiparallel (one runs 5′→3′, the other 3′→5′)
• Strands are held together by hydrogen bonds between complementary bases
• A–T: 2 hydrogen bonds  |  G–C: 3 hydrogen bonds

Deoxyribose Sugar

A 5-carbon (pentose) sugar. The "deoxy" refers to the missing hydroxyl group at position 2' compared to ribose (RNA's sugar). Carbon 1' attaches to the base; carbon 3' and 5' attach to phosphate groups forming the backbone. The difference between 3' and 5' ends defines directionality.

IB link: You need to distinguish deoxyribose (DNA) from ribose (RNA). The 2'-OH group in ribose makes RNA less chemically stable than DNA.

Phosphate Group

Links adjacent deoxyribose sugars via phosphodiester bonds, forming the sugar-phosphate backbone on the outside of the helix. The negative charge of phosphate groups makes DNA hydrophilic on the outside and is why DNA binds to positively-charged histone proteins in eukaryotes.

IB link: Phosphodiester bonds form by condensation (releasing water). DNA replication breaks them using nucleases; DNA polymerase creates them using dNTPs.

Purines: Adenine (A) and Guanine (G)

Purines have a double-ring structure. A always pairs with T (2 H-bonds); G always pairs with C (3 H-bonds). Because a purine always pairs with a pyrimidine, the width of the helix stays constant at ~2 nm along its entire length — this is why X-ray diffraction showed a regular repeating pattern.

Chargaff's Rule: In any DNA sample, %A = %T and %G = %C. This is testable. If you know %G, you can calculate all four bases.

Pyrimidines: Thymine (T) and Cytosine (C)

Pyrimidines have a single-ring structure. Thymine is unique to DNA (RNA uses Uracil instead of Thymine). Cytosine pairs with Guanine via 3 hydrogen bonds, making G-C-rich regions more thermally stable — relevant to PCR annealing temperatures and to whether an organism adapted to heat has higher G+C content.

IB link: RNA uses Uracil (U) in place of Thymine (T). This is a key DNA vs RNA comparison tested in exams.

Hydrogen Bonds Between Base Pairs

The two strands are held together by hydrogen bonds: A–T forms 2 H-bonds; G–C forms 3 H-bonds. These bonds are individually weak but collectively strong enough to maintain double-helix structure, yet weak enough to be broken by helicase during replication. High G+C content = more H-bonds = higher melting temperature.

Exam tip: DNA helicase breaks H-bonds during replication. This is different from nucleases which break phosphodiester bonds.

Antiparallel Strands

The two strands run in opposite directions: one from 5′→3′ top to bottom, the other 3′→5′ top to bottom. The 5' end has a free phosphate group; the 3' end has a free hydroxyl group. DNA polymerase can only add nucleotides to the 3′ end, which is why one strand (leading) is synthesised continuously and the other (lagging) in fragments.

IB HL link: Antiparallel structure explains Okazaki fragments during replication — the lagging strand must be synthesised in short segments moving away from the replication fork.

🔐 Vocabulary Check — Tap Each Term

Nucleotide Polynucleotide Chargaff's Rules Double Helix Phosphodiester Bond
Nucleotide: The monomer of DNA. Each nucleotide consists of three components: a deoxyribose sugar, a phosphate group, and one of four nitrogenous bases (A, T, G, or C). Nucleotides are joined by condensation reactions to form a polynucleotide strand.
Polynucleotide: A chain of many nucleotides linked by phosphodiester bonds between the 3' carbon of one sugar and the 5' carbon of the next. DNA consists of two antiparallel polynucleotide strands. The sequence of bases along a polynucleotide encodes genetic information.
Chargaff's Rules: Erwin Chargaff found that in any DNA sample: %A = %T and %G = %C. This meant A always pairs with T and G always pairs with C — the principle of complementary base pairing. If you know any one base percentage, you can calculate all four.
Double Helix: The three-dimensional structure of DNA. Two antiparallel polynucleotide strands coil around each other. The sugar-phosphate backbones form the outside; the paired bases face inward. The helix makes one complete turn every 10 base pairs (3.4 nm).
Phosphodiester Bond: The covalent bond that joins adjacent nucleotides in a strand, linking the 3' carbon of one deoxyribose to the 5' carbon of the next via a phosphate group. Formed by condensation. Broken by nucleases.

Phase 3 · B1.1.5–B1.1.6 — Genes & Alleles

⚙️ Genes, Alleles & Loci SL

A gene is a heritable factor that controls a specific characteristic. It consists of a specific sequence of DNA nucleotides that codes for a functional product (usually a protein). Understanding how genes vary — through alleles — is the foundation of all inheritance genetics.

Every human has approximately 20,000–25,000 protein-coding genes occupying about 1.5% of the genome. The rest — once called "junk DNA" — includes regulatory sequences, introns, repeated sequences, and non-coding RNA genes. HL

Gene

A gene is a heritable factor that controls a specific characteristic. It consists of a specific sequence of DNA nucleotides at a particular locus on a chromosome. This sequence codes for a polypeptide (protein), or in some cases a functional RNA molecule (e.g. ribosomal RNA, tRNA). One gene = one polypeptide (with important exceptions).

IB definition (memorise exactly): "A heritable factor that consists of a length of DNA and influences a specific characteristic." Genes code for proteins; proteins control traits.

Locus (plural: loci)

The specific position of a gene on a chromosome. All members of a species have the same loci for the same genes on homologous chromosomes. Because humans are diploid, we have two copies of each autosomal locus — one on each homologue. The locus is fixed; what varies is the allele at that locus.

Analogy: The locus is the address on the chromosome; the allele is whoever lives at that address. Different people may have different "residents" (alleles) at the same address (locus).

Allele

An alternative form of a gene, occupying the same locus on homologous chromosomes. Alleles arise by mutation. A gene can have many alleles in a population (e.g. ABO blood group has three alleles: IÅ, Iᵇ, i), but a diploid individual can carry at most two alleles at any locus.

IB definition: "One specific form of a gene, differing from other alleles by one or a few bases only, and occupying the same gene locus as other alleles of the gene." Exam note: allele ≠ gene; allele ≠ trait.

Homozygous

An individual is homozygous at a locus when both alleles at that locus are identical. Homozygous dominant (AA) expresses the dominant phenotype; homozygous recessive (aa) expresses the recessive phenotype. A homozygous individual is "true-breeding" for that trait and produces only one type of gamete for that locus.

Example: A plant with genotype TT is homozygous dominant for height. All of its gametes will carry T.

Heterozygous

An individual is heterozygous at a locus when the two alleles are different (e.g. Aa). The phenotype expressed depends on dominance relationships. A heterozygous individual produces two types of gametes (A and a) in equal proportions. Heterozygous individuals that carry a recessive allele without showing its phenotype are called carriers.

Example: Aa individual for cystic fibrosis is a carrier — they express normal phenotype but can pass the recessive allele to offspring. Critical for genetics problems.

Dominant and Recessive Alleles

A dominant allele is expressed in the phenotype even when only one copy is present (in a heterozygote). A recessive allele is expressed only when two copies are present (homozygous recessive). Written as uppercase (dominant) and lowercase (recessive). In codominance, both alleles are expressed simultaneously — neither is dominant.

IB note: Not all traits follow simple dominance. Codominance (e.g. AB blood type), incomplete dominance (e.g. snapdragon flower colour), and multiple alleles (e.g. ABO) are all testable.

Phase 4 · B1.1.7 — The Genome

🆕 The Genome SL

The genome is the complete set of genetic information of an organism — including all genes and all non-coding DNA sequences. Understanding the genome is essential for modern genetics, and it links directly to your research project and to the growing field of genomic medicine.

The Human Genome Project (completed 2003) sequenced all ~3.2 billion base pairs of the human genome. Result: ~20,000–25,000 protein-coding genes — only about 1.5% of the genome. The rest includes regulatory DNA, introns, repeated sequences, transposons, and non-coding RNA genes. HL

The Genome

The IB defines the genome as "the complete set of genes and other DNA sequences of a cell, an organism, or a species." This includes both coding and non-coding DNA. Different species have wildly different genome sizes (the C-value paradox): a human genome is ~3.2 Gb; a wheat genome is ~17 Gb; the Paris japonica plant has ~150 Gb. Genome size does not correlate with organism complexity.

The term "genome" encompasses all DNA in the nucleus (and mitochondria/chloroplasts). Each human cell contains ~2 metres of DNA packed into a nucleus ~6 micrometres across.

Coding DNA (Exons)

Coding sequences are the exons — stretches of DNA that are transcribed into mRNA and translated into protein. In eukaryotes, exons are interrupted by introns and spliced together. Only about 1.5% of the human genome is exonic. Each exon encodes part of a protein; alternative splicing allows one gene to produce multiple different proteins.

IB HL: Alternative splicing is a key mechanism for proteome diversity — one gene (one sequence of exons) can produce many different proteins depending on which exons are included.

Non-coding DNA

The ~98.5% of the human genome that does not code for proteins includes: introns (interrupted sequences within genes, removed during RNA processing), regulatory sequences (promoters, enhancers — control gene expression), repetitive sequences (used in DNA fingerprinting), and transposons (mobile genetic elements). Non-coding is not the same as non-functional.

IB link: Repetitive sequences (STRs — short tandem repeats) are the basis of DNA profiling/fingerprinting, which appears on both genetics and forensics questions.

Prokaryotic vs Eukaryotic Genomes

Prokaryotic genomes: single circular chromosome in the nucleoid, no introns, no histones, smaller (E. coli ~4.6 Mb), plus plasmids. Eukaryotic genomes: multiple linear chromosomes in the nucleus, extensive non-coding DNA, introns, wrapped around histones. This distinction is heavily tested in IB exams.

Comparison table: Prokaryote = circular, no introns, no membrane, no histones. Eukaryote = linear, introns present, membrane-bound, histones. You will need this for genetics questions and for evolution (horizontal gene transfer).

Phase 5 · B1.1.8–B1.1.9 — Mutations

⚡ Gene Mutations SL

A gene mutation is a change in the base sequence of DNA. Mutations are the ultimate source of all genetic variation — they create new alleles. Some mutations have no effect, some are harmful, and some are beneficial. The IB expects you to classify and evaluate mutations with specific named examples.

The sickle cell allele is the result of a single base substitution: one nucleotide changed in the β-globin gene. This changes a single amino acid in haemoglobin, yet in homozygous form it causes a life-threatening blood disorder. In heterozygous form, it provides protection against malaria. One mutation — two opposite effects depending on allele dosage.

⚡ Types of Gene Mutation — Tap Each

Substitution Insertion Deletion Frameshift Silent Mutation
Substitution: One nucleotide is replaced by a different nucleotide. This changes only one codon and therefore potentially only one amino acid. Because the genetic code is degenerate (multiple codons can code for the same amino acid), some substitutions are silent. Others cause missense (different amino acid) or nonsense (stop codon) mutations. Example: sickle cell anaemia — GAG changed to GTG in HBB gene, changing glutamic acid to valine.
Insertion: One or more extra nucleotides are added into the DNA sequence. Unless inserted in multiples of three, this causes a frameshift — all codons downstream of the insertion are read in the wrong frame, usually producing a completely non-functional protein. Insertions are generally more damaging than substitutions.
Deletion: One or more nucleotides are removed from the DNA sequence. Like insertion, a deletion that is not a multiple of three causes a frameshift. Even a single nucleotide deletion can completely destroy a protein's function by shifting every codon after the deletion. Example: deltaF508 deletion in the CFTR gene causes ~70% of cystic fibrosis cases.
Frameshift Mutation: Caused by insertions or deletions that are not multiples of three. The reading frame shifts, so all codons from the mutation point onwards are misread. This typically produces a highly altered polypeptide or introduces an early stop codon. Frameshifts are almost always loss-of-function mutations and are very rarely beneficial.
Silent (Synonymous) Mutation: A nucleotide change that does NOT alter the amino acid sequence of the protein. This is possible because the genetic code is degenerate — 64 codons code for only 20 amino acids + stop. Example: changing CGA to CGG — both code for Arginine. Silent mutations still change allele frequency in populations and can occasionally affect RNA splicing.

📝 Causes and Effects of Mutation

Mutagens increase the rate of mutation: ionising radiation (UV light, X-rays, gamma rays), chemical mutagens (e.g. benzene, nitrous acid), certain viruses. Spontaneous mutations occur at a low background rate during DNA replication errors. The vast majority of mutations are: repaired by DNA repair enzymes, neutral in effect, or harmful. Beneficial mutations are rare but are the raw material for evolution by natural selection.

Understanding the mutation type helps you explain the mechanism in your research paper and ISEF abstract.

Phase 6 — IB-Style Exam Questions

📝 IB Exam Practice SL HL

Work through these exam-style questions, write your own answers first, then reveal the mark-scheme response. Focus on using precise IB terminology and meeting the command terms (state, describe, explain, evaluate).

B1.1.1 — SL — State (2 marks)
State two structural differences between a DNA nucleotide and an RNA nucleotide.
Award 1 mark each (max 2):
  • DNA nucleotide contains deoxyribose; RNA nucleotide contains ribose (with 2'-OH group)
  • DNA uses thymine; RNA uses uracil instead of thymine
  • DNA is double-stranded / RNA is single-stranded (accept if clearly referring to structural difference)

Examiner note: "Sugar is different" alone scores 0 — you must name the sugars. "Base is different" alone scores 0 — you must name which base differs.

B1.1.2 — SL — Describe (3 marks)
Describe the structure of the DNA double helix.
Award 1 mark each (max 3):
  • Two polynucleotide strands wound around each other in a double helix
  • Strands are antiparallel (one 5'→3', the other 3'→5')
  • Sugar-phosphate backbone on the outside; bases on the inside
  • Complementary base pairing via hydrogen bonds (A-T: 2 H-bonds; G-C: 3 H-bonds)
  • The helix makes one complete turn every 10 base pairs (3.4 nm)
B1.1.5 — SL — Distinguish (2 marks)
Distinguish between a gene and an allele.
Award 1 mark each:
  • A gene is a heritable factor consisting of a specific sequence of DNA that controls a specific characteristic / codes for a specific polypeptide
  • An allele is one specific form of a gene, occupying the same locus as other alleles of that gene, differing by one or a few bases

Examiner note: Many students lose marks by defining allele as "a variant gene" without specifying that it occupies the same locus and differs only in base sequence. Use the IB definition exactly.

B1.1.8 — SL — Explain (4 marks)
Explain why a frameshift mutation is usually more damaging than a substitution mutation.
Award 1 mark each (max 4):
  • A substitution changes only one codon; may result in one amino acid change
  • Due to degeneracy of the genetic code, substitution may be silent (no amino acid change)
  • A frameshift (insertion/deletion not in multiples of 3) shifts the reading frame
  • All codons downstream of the mutation are misread
  • Likely produces a completely non-functional / drastically altered polypeptide
  • May introduce a premature stop codon, truncating the protein
B1.1.3 — HL — Calculate (2 marks)
A DNA sample contains 18% adenine. Calculate the percentage of guanine in this sample. Show your working.
Working:
  • %A = %T = 18%, so A + T = 36% (1 mark)
  • G + C = 100 − 36 = 64%, therefore %G = 32% (1 mark)

Always show working — if you write 32% only, you may score 1/2. If your arithmetic is wrong but your method is correct, you still score the method mark.

B1.1.9 — SL — Evaluate (5 marks)
Evaluate the possible consequences of a point mutation in a protein-coding gene.
For full marks, cover: consequences, mechanisms, examples, and balanced evaluation
  • Silent: No amino acid change due to degenerate code; no phenotypic effect; still creates a new allele (1)
  • Missense: One amino acid substituted; effect depends on position and chemical nature change; example: sickle cell (GAG→GTG, Glu→Val in HBB) (1)
  • Nonsense: Creates a premature stop codon; truncated, non-functional protein (1)
  • Consequences range from no effect to lethal depending on: which protein, which domain, whether homozygous/heterozygous (1)
  • Some mutations are beneficial in certain environments, e.g. sickle cell heterozygote has malaria resistance; raw material for evolution by natural selection (1)
