A — Weathering

Sun, rain, ice and plant roots attack the exposed mountain rock, breaking it into smaller and smaller pieces without moving it anywhere.

B — Erosion

The river wears away the bottom and sides of its valley, picking up the loose broken rock fragments as it flows downhill.

C — Transportation

The river carries — transports — the fragments, now called sediment, further and further downstream toward the sea.

D — Deposition

As the river slows down and reaches the sea, it drops — deposits — its sediment, which settles in layers on the sea bed.

Correct answer: weathering. Weathering breaks rock apart where it stands — nothing is carried away.

Correct answer: deposits. The river transports sediment while it flows, then deposits (drops) it once it slows down near the sea.

Sedimentation, compaction and cementation

Layers of sediment build up on the sea floor in a process called sedimentation. As more layers pile on top, the weight above squeezes the water out of the older, deeper layers — this is compaction. Dissolved minerals then glue the squashed grains together — this is cementation. Together, these processes turn loose sediment into solid sedimentary rock.

Correct answer: bottom. Just like in your layer stack above, the deepest, oldest layers carry the most weight pressing down on them, so they compact and cement into rock first.

Reading the layers

Geologists can "read" these layers almost like the pages of a book. Because deposition happens over and over, the oldest layer of rock is always at the bottom and the youngest is at the top — unless huge earth movements have later tilted or folded the rock.

What are sedimentary rocks made of?

Meet the sedimentary rock family

Not all sedimentary rock looks the same — it depends on the size of the grains, and what they are made from. Explore three common types below.

Coarse grains you can see and feel

Sandstone forms from grains of sand — often from old deserts, beaches or river beds — that were buried, compacted and cemented together. The grains are large enough to see and feel, giving sandstone a gritty texture.

Coarse grainedFeels gritty
Sandstone — you can see individual grains of sand cemented together.

Fine grains of mud and clay

Shale forms from mud and clay that settled in very still water, such as a lake, lagoon or deep sea. The particles are much smaller than sand, so shale is fine grained, feels smooth, and often splits apart into thin, flat sheets.

Fine grainedSplits into layers
Shale — made of compacted mud, so fine grained that individual particles are hard to see.

Built from ancient sea life

Limestone is different — instead of forming from weathered rock fragments, it is usually made almost entirely of the crushed shells and skeletons of sea creatures, cemented together over millions of years.

Made of shellsOften pale grey/white
Limestone — formed largely from the calcium carbonate in shells and coral.

Detective work: is it sedimentary?

A close-up of a sedimentary rock sample.

a   How do you know that the rock in the picture is sedimentary rock?

It is made of small fragments or grains that have been cemented together, and it may show layering (bedding). This texture is very different from the interlocking crystals you would see in an igneous rock, so it must have formed from sediment that was compacted and cemented — a sedimentary rock.

b   How could you decide whether this is limestone or shale?

Test a small piece with a drop of dilute acid: limestone contains calcium carbonate from shells, so it fizzes; shale does not react. You could also look closely — limestone often shows pale colour and fragments of shell, while shale is very fine grained, feels smooth, and splits easily into thin flat sheets.
Nature's time capsules

How does a fossil form?

A fossil forms when a plant or animal is buried quickly by sediment before it can fully rot away. As layer after layer builds up above it, the sediment turns to rock around it. There are two main kinds of fossil — tap each button to see how they differ.

An empty imprint

The plant or animal's hard parts leave a hollow impression in the surrounding rock, but the original material itself has dissolved or rotted away completely. What is left behind is the shape and surface pattern — like a footprint pressed into wet sand that then hardens.

A solid replica

If minerals dissolved in water later seep into a mould and harden inside it, they fill the hollow space and form a solid, natural copy of the original shape — a cast. A cast is a bit like pouring plaster into a mould to make a model.

Workbook · 3.2 Focus, question 1h

h   Fossils are only found in ___ rock.

Correct answer: sedimentary. Fossils can form in any sedimentary rock (not just sandstone) because sediment settles gently around the remains instead of destroying them with heat, as igneous rock would.

A fossil leaf, preserved as a detailed imprint in fine-grained sedimentary rock.

a   How did this rock form?

Fine sediment such as mud or silt settled in still water and quickly buried the leaf. Over time, more layers piled on top, and compaction and cementation turned the sediment into solid sedimentary rock — with the leaf's imprint preserved inside.

b   Is this fossil a mould or a cast?

It is a mould. You can see the delicate outline and vein pattern of the leaf pressed into the rock, but the leaf's own material has decayed away — only the flat imprint remains.

c   Describe how the fossil formed.

The leaf fell into still water and was rapidly covered by fine sediment before it could rot away completely. Further layers of sediment built up above it, and over a long time pressure and cementation turned the sediment into rock. The leaf itself gradually decayed, leaving behind a detailed hollow imprint — its mould — preserved in the surrounding rock.
Workbook · 3.2 Challenge, question 4

The West Coast Fossil Park

Along South Africa's dry west coast, a huge collection of ancient bones lies buried in the sand — the West Coast Fossil Park. A phosphate mine once operated here for forty years, and miners kept finding strange fossilised bones that did not match any animal alive today. When the mine closed, the site became a heritage site and open-air museum.

Scientists have dated the fossils to about five million years ago, when a river once flowed through this now-dry landscape to the sea. Back then the climate was warm and wet, with tall trees, ferns and lush plants — nothing like the arid coastline of today.

Over 200 kinds of animal have been found here, including huge bears, three-toed horses, several species of elephant, and short-necked giraffes called sivatheres. Scientists think many of these animals drowned trying to cross a flooded river; their bodies were washed downstream, scavenged, and eventually buried under layer upon layer of phosphate-rich sand — slowly turning bone into stone.

Answer the questions — tap each one to reveal the model answer

a   How old are the fossils in the West Coast Fossil Park?
They are about five million years old, dating from a time when the West Coast had a much wetter, greener climate than it does today.
b   How were the bones of the animals "turned into stone"?
The bones were buried under layers of phosphate-rich sand. Over a very long time, minerals dissolved in water gradually seeped into the bones and replaced the original bone material, molecule by molecule — a process of fossilisation that turned soft bone into hard rock.
c   How do scientists know that the fossilised animals are now extinct?
None of the fossilised species — such as the short-necked sivathere, the giant bear, or the three-toed horse — match any animal alive today. Since no living examples of these species have ever been found anywhere on Earth, scientists conclude they are extinct.
d   How can scientists describe the climate, animal and plant life from five million years ago?
By studying which plants and animals are preserved as fossils, and comparing them with similar living species today, scientists can work out what the climate and habitat must have been like. The type of sediment the fossils were buried in (for example, river sand rather than desert dune sand) also gives clues about the ancient environment.
e   What type of sedimentary rock were the fossils found in?
A phosphate-rich sandstone — the bones were buried in phosphate-rich sand that was later compacted and cemented into rock.
