Phase 1 · Wonder

🧫 What's Inside a Living Cell?

Every organism you met in Topic 1 — animal, plant, fungus, prokaryote, protoctist — is built from cells. But not all cells look the same, and not all cells contain the same parts.

An onion cell and a cheek cell (from the inside of your mouth) look strikingly different under a microscope — one has a thick outer wall and a large empty-looking space in the middle; the other doesn't. Both are definitely cells. What do you think could explain the differences? What job might each extra structure be doing?

✏️ Your Thinking — Before We Begin

Plant, animal and bacterial cells all share some basic structures — but each also has its own special features suited to the job it does. By the end of this lesson you'll be able to compare all three, meet six specialised cells, and calculate how much bigger a microscope image is than the real thing.

🧱 Cell Wall

Found in PLANT cells only (not animal cells). A rigid layer surrounding the cell membrane, made of cellulose. It supports and gives shape to the cell, and prevents the cell from bursting when it takes in too much water.

📌 Exam tip: "cell wall = plant only" is one of the most tested facts on this topic.

🫧 Cell Membrane

Found in BOTH plant and animal cells (and bacteria). A thin, partially permeable layer surrounding the cytoplasm that controls which substances can enter and leave the cell.

🔵 Nucleus

Found in BOTH plant and animal cells. Contains the cell's genetic material (DNA, organised into chromosomes) and controls the cell's activities.

💧 Cytoplasm

Found in BOTH plant and animal cells. A jelly-like substance filling the cell, where most of the chemical reactions of metabolism take place and where organelles are suspended.

🌱 Chloroplasts

Found in PLANT cells only, mainly in green parts such as leaves (not in root cells, which don't photosynthesise). Contain chlorophyll and are the site of photosynthesis.

⚫ Ribosomes

Found in BOTH plant and animal cells (and bacteria — though bacterial ribosomes are smaller). The site of protein synthesis.

🔋 Mitochondria

Found in BOTH plant and animal cells. The site of (aerobic) respiration, releasing energy for the cell's activities. Cells with high energy demands (e.g. muscle cells) contain many mitochondria.

⭕ Vacuole

PLANT cells typically have one large, permanent vacuole filled with cell sap, which stores water and solutes and helps keep the cell turgid (firm). Animal cells do not have this large permanent vacuole.

Phase 3 · 2.1 Cell Structure — Bacterial Cells

🦠 Bacterial Cells

Bacteria are prokaryotes — their cells are built quite differently from plant and animal cells (which are eukaryotes).

A bacterial cell has: a cell wall, a cell membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes, a single strand of circular DNA (not organised into a nucleus), and often one or more small circular loops of extra DNA called plasmids.

🧠 Key Terms — Tap Each

Circular DNA Plasmid No Nucleus
Circular DNA — In a bacterial cell, the main genetic material is a single circular strand of DNA lying free in the cytoplasm — it is NOT enclosed in a nucleus.
Plasmid — A small, separate loop of DNA, additional to the main circular DNA, found in the cytoplasm of many bacterial cells. Plasmids often carry genes that give bacteria useful extra traits, such as antibiotic resistance.
No Nucleus — Unlike plant and animal cells, bacterial cells have no nucleus, no mitochondria, and no chloroplasts. This is the key difference between prokaryotic cells (bacteria) and eukaryotic cells (plants, animals).

🌬️ Ciliated Cell

Found lining the trachea and bronchi. Covered in tiny hair-like cilia that beat rhythmically to sweep mucus (which traps dust, bacteria and debris) up and away from the lungs.

📌 Function: movement of mucus in the trachea and bronchi.

🌱 Root Hair Cell

Found on the surface of plant roots. Has a long, thin extension that greatly increases surface area, allowing efficient absorption of water and mineral ions from the soil.

📌 Function: absorption.

🍃 Palisade Mesophyll Cell

Found packed near the upper surface of a leaf. Contains many chloroplasts, positioned to capture as much light as possible.

📌 Function: photosynthesis.

⚡ Neurone

A nerve cell with a long, thin fibre (axon) that can carry an electrical impulse over long distances in the body, connecting to other neurones or effectors.

📌 Function: conduction of electrical impulses.

🩸 Red Blood Cell

A small, flexible, disc-shaped cell packed with haemoglobin. It has no nucleus, leaving more space to carry oxygen.

📌 Function: transport of oxygen.

🧬 Sperm and Egg Cells (Gametes)

Sperm cells are small with a long tail for swimming towards the egg. Egg cells are large, with food reserves to support early development, and only one is released at a time (in humans).

📌 Function: reproduction.

🏗️ Levels of Organisation

New cells are produced by division of existing cells. As an organism develops, cells are organised into increasingly larger structures.

Cell Tissue Organ Organ System Organism
Cell — The basic structural and functional unit of all living organisms.
Tissue — A group of cells with similar structures, working together to perform a shared function. Example: muscle tissue.
Organ — A structure made of a group of different tissues, working together to perform specific functions. Example: the heart (made of muscle tissue, nervous tissue, and more).
Organ System — A group of organs with related functions, working together to perform body functions. Example: the circulatory system (heart, blood vessels, blood).
Organism — An individual living thing, made up of one or more organ systems (or, in unicellular organisms, a single cell that carries out all life processes).

Phase 5 · 2.2 Size of Specimens

📏 Magnification Calculations

Cells are far too small to see with the naked eye, so microscope images are magnified. IGCSE requires you to calculate magnification, image size, or actual size — and to convert between millimetres and micrometres.

Formula (learn this): magnification = image size ÷ actual size. Rearranged: actual size = image size ÷ magnification, and image size = actual size × magnification.

Unit conversion: 1 millimetre (mm) = 1000 micrometres (μm). To convert mm → μm, multiply by 1000. To convert μm → mm, divide by 1000.

📝 IGCSE Past Paper Questions

CIE IGCSE Biology — Plant vs Animal Cells · 2 marks
State TWO structures found in a plant cell but not in an animal cell. [2]
Tip: Think about support, photosynthesis, and water storage.
Mark Scheme Answer (2 marks — any 2 of):
  • Cell wall (made of cellulose) [1]
  • Chloroplasts [1]
  • Large, permanent (sap-filled) vacuole [1]
CIE IGCSE Biology — Mitochondria · 2 marks
State the function of mitochondria, and explain why a muscle cell would contain many mitochondria. [2]
Tip: Link the structure to the energy needs of the cell.
Mark Scheme Answer (2 marks):
  • Mitochondria are the site of (aerobic) respiration, releasing energy [1]
  • Muscle cells need a lot of energy for contraction, so contain many mitochondria to provide this energy [1]
CIE IGCSE Biology — Bacterial Cells · 2 marks
Give TWO differences in structure between a bacterial cell and a plant cell. [2]
Tip: Think about the nucleus and extra loops of DNA.
Mark Scheme Answer (2 marks — any 2 of):
  • Bacterial cell has no nucleus (DNA is a single circular strand free in the cytoplasm); plant cell has a nucleus [1]
  • Bacterial cell may contain plasmids; plant cells do not [1]
  • Bacterial cell has no chloroplasts or mitochondria; plant cell has both [1]
CIE IGCSE Biology — Root Hair Cells · 2 marks
A root hair cell has a long, thin extension. Explain how this feature adapts the cell to its function. [2]
Tip: Think about surface area.
Mark Scheme Answer (2 marks):
  • The long, thin extension increases the surface area of the cell [1]
  • This increases the rate of absorption of water and mineral ions from the soil [1]
CIE IGCSE Biology — Levels of Organisation · 2 marks
Define the term 'tissue', and give a named example, stating where it is found in the human body. [2]
Tip: The definition needs both "group of cells" and "shared function".
Mark Scheme Answer (2 marks):
  • A tissue is a group of cells with similar structures, working together to perform a shared function [1]
  • Named example with location, e.g. muscle tissue found in the heart, or ciliated epithelial tissue lining the trachea [1]
CIE IGCSE Biology — Magnification · 2 marks
An image of a cell measures 45 mm across. The actual diameter of the cell is 0.03 mm. Calculate the magnification of the image. Show your working. [2]
Tip: magnification = image size ÷ actual size.
Mark Scheme Answer (2 marks):
  • magnification = image size ÷ actual size = 45 ÷ 0.03 [1]
  • = ×1500 [1]
CIE IGCSE Biology — Unit Conversion · 2 marks
An organelle has an actual diameter of 2 μm. Convert this measurement to millimetres, and name one organelle that could plausibly be this size. [2]
Tip: 1000 μm = 1 mm.
Mark Scheme Answer (2 marks):
  • 2 μm = 0.002 mm [1]
  • Named organelle of plausible micrometre-scale size, e.g. mitochondrion or ribosome [1]
