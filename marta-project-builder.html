🌟 Welcome, Marta!

This interactive will guide you through every stage of building a science competition project — from finding a topic you love, to designing your experiment, to writing your abstract for Explory in Poland and preparing for the ISEF pathway.

🎉 About Explory & ISEF

Explory is an international science competition held in Poland, open to students aged 12–20. It showcases original student research across all scientific fields. Top projects from Explory can qualify for the Intel ISEF (International Science and Engineering Fair) — the world's largest pre-college science competition, held annually in the USA. ISEF winners receive scholarships, prizes, and recognition from Nobel laureates.

✨ The best science fair projects come from genuine curiosity. They are not about impressing judges with complexity — they are about asking a clear question, designing a fair test, collecting real data, and interpreting what it means honestly. A simple, well-designed study on tongue-rolling in your school could beat a complicated experiment done sloppily.

💡 Before We Start — What Sparks Your Curiosity?

Write freely — no wrong answers. What topics do you find yourself looking up on your own? What problems do you wish science could solve? What do you notice around you that makes you ask "why?"

Tutor tip: The most common mistake is picking a topic that sounds impressive but doesn't interest you. Science fair projects take months. You need to still care about your question at 11pm when the data isn't cooperating. Start with what you love.

Step 1 — Explore Topic Areas

🔎 What Should My Project Be About?

Good science competition projects come from many fields. The most important thing is that you can collect real data yourself. Tap each topic area to see what kinds of projects are possible and what makes them work at Explory and ISEF level.

🔬 The golden rule for topic selection: Ask yourself three questions. (1) Can I collect data on this myself, in my school or community? (2) Has someone else already studied this exact question? (3) Would the answer actually tell us something new or interesting? If yes, yes (literature exists to learn from), and yes — you have a great topic.

⚡ What Makes a Strong Topic?

Testable Original Feasible Relevant Ethical
Testable: A good research question can be answered by collecting data. "Is climate change bad?" is not testable as stated. "Does increased CO₂ concentration affect the growth rate of duckweed?" is testable. Every question must have a measurable dependent variable and at least one controllable independent variable.
Original: Your project doesn't have to discover something completely new, but it should add something — a new population, a new condition, a new comparison, a new time period. Search Google Scholar and PubMed to see what has been done. If the exact question has been answered, refine it. Judges at ISEF specifically look for originality.
Feasible: Can you actually do this in a school setting within your time frame (usually 3–6 months)? You need access to participants/organisms, safe materials, and equipment you can realistically use. A project studying deep-sea fish or requiring a particle accelerator is not feasible. Tongue-rolling surveys, plant growth experiments, and psychological studies are.
Relevant: Why does your question matter? Who would benefit from knowing the answer? The best projects connect to a real-world problem or question — health, environment, behaviour, technology. You should be able to say in one sentence why your research is important.
Ethical: Any research involving human subjects requires informed consent. Research involving animals requires welfare consideration. You must not collect sensitive personal data without proper permissions. ISEF has strict rules on human subjects research (IRB approval required). Survey-based genetics studies are generally low-risk if anonymised.

Step 2 — Choose Your Project Type

🧪 What Kind of Project Will It Be?

Science fair projects come in several types. Choosing the right type for your topic makes the difference between a project that's manageable and one that collapses under its own complexity. Each type has strengths — the best choice depends on your topic and what you can realistically do.

🎯 What Type Fits Genetics Projects?

Genetics-based projects are almost always Survey / Observational studies at school level (phenotype surveys, inheritance pattern studies in families, allele frequency estimation in populations). They can also be Lab experiments if you use model organisms like fruit flies (Drosophila) or fast plants. Survey-based genetics projects are ideal for ISEF because they generate real population data and can be done safely with appropriate ethics consent.

Tutor tip: Judges at ISEF and Explory actually prefer a simple, well-executed project over a complicated one done poorly. A survey of 150 people with clean data analysis beats a complex lab experiment with errors and missing controls.

Step 3 — Build Your Research Structure

🔬 The Science Project Structure

Every strong science project follows the same fundamental structure. Work through each section below, filling in your own ideas as you go. This becomes the backbone of your research report and competition presentation.

📝 ISEF & Explory Report Sections

Both competitions require a written report with these sections (in order): Abstract, Introduction/Background, Research Question, Hypothesis, Materials & Methods, Results, Discussion, Conclusion, References, Acknowledgements. You will build each section here.

1
🔎 Research Question

A single, clear, testable question. Format: "How does [independent variable] affect [dependent variable] in [organism/population/system]?" It must be specific enough that someone could design an experiment to answer it. Avoid "what is" or "which is better" — these don't specify a measurable relationship.

Aim: 1–2 sentences — precise and specific
2
📚 Background / Introduction

Summarise what is already known about your topic from published sources. This shows judges you understand the scientific context. It should explain: (a) the key biology/science of your topic, (b) why this question is worth studying, (c) what previous researchers have found, and (d) the gap in knowledge your project addresses.

Aim: 300–500 words in final report — draft the key points here
3
💡 Hypothesis

A specific, falsifiable prediction. Format: "If [IV], then [DV] will [increase/decrease/show pattern], because [scientific reason]." The "because" is crucial — it shows your prediction is based on scientific understanding, not guesswork. Your hypothesis must be answerable by your planned experiment or survey.

Aim: 1–3 sentences
4
⚙️ Variables

Clearly identify your independent variable (what you change or compare), dependent variable (what you measure), and at least 3 controlled variables (what you keep constant). For a genetics survey: IV might be year group or sex; DV might be frequency of a trait; CVs might be survey method, criteria for trait classification, etc.

List format
5
📋 Materials & Methods

A numbered, step-by-step procedure detailed enough that another scientist could exactly replicate your study. Include: participant/sample description, sampling method, all materials, data collection procedure, any controls, and how you handled ethics (consent forms, anonymisation). This is where most students lose marks — be specific.

Aim: numbered steps, each a single clear action
6
📊 Data & Analysis Plan

Before collecting data, plan exactly how you will record and analyse it. What will your data table look like? How many participants/samples? What statistical test will you use and why? For genetics surveys: chi-squared test (observed vs expected Mendelian ratios) or descriptive statistics (frequency tables, bar charts, percentages).

Plan your data table structure and analysis method
7
💬 Discussion Plan

This is where most marks are won or lost. Plan what you will discuss after you collect results. Even before you have data, you can anticipate: What would it mean if results support your hypothesis? What if they don't? What are the limitations of your study? How do your expected results connect to existing literature? What would you do differently next time?

Bullet-point plan

Step 4 — Explory & ISEF Criteria

🌏 How Judges Score Your Project

Knowing exactly how judges evaluate projects is one of the most powerful advantages you can have. Work backwards from the scoring rubric: make sure every part of your project directly addresses the judging criteria.

🔬 Explory Competition Overview

Explory is held annually in Lodz, Poland. Students submit a project report and present their work to an international jury. Projects are judged on scientific quality, originality, presentation, and impact. There are categories by scientific field and age group. Top projects from Explory gain direct ISEF qualification or affiliated fair status. The competition is conducted in English.

🏆 ISEF Judging Criteria — Tap Each

🔬 Scientific Thought

Does the student understand the scientific method? Is the question well-defined? Does the hypothesis follow logically from background research? Is the experimental design controlled and fair? Can the student explain how the data answers the research question? This is usually the highest-weighted criterion.

HIGHEST WEIGHT ~30%
🌟 Creative Ability

Is the research question original? Did the student design their own study (not just replicate a kit experiment)? Does the approach show ingenuity? Did they come up with creative solutions to methodological challenges? Originality does NOT mean unprecedented — a new angle on a known question counts.

HIGH WEIGHT ~25%
📊 Thoroughness

Was the study completed fully? Is the data collection adequate (sufficient sample size, appropriate number of trials)? Does the discussion address limitations honestly? Are references cited properly? Did the student acknowledge what they don't know? Judges respect intellectual honesty about limitations far more than pretending a project is perfect.

HIGH WEIGHT ~20%
📋 Skill

Does the student demonstrate competence in the techniques they used? Are data collection methods appropriate? Is statistical analysis correctly applied? Did they make good use of available resources? This does not mean expensive equipment — a student who correctly applies chi-squared to survey data demonstrates appropriate skill.

MEDIUM WEIGHT ~15%
🌍 Clarity & Presentation

Is the project clearly communicated in the report and poster/presentation? Does the student explain their work to judges in a clear, confident, engaging way? Can they answer questions beyond their own results (background science, implications)? Good presentation amplifies excellent science; bad presentation hides it.

MEDIUM WEIGHT ~10%

🕐 Project Timeline — Tap Each Phase

Phase 1: Topic & Question (Weeks 1–2) Phase 2: Background Research (Weeks 3–4) Phase 3: Data Collection (Weeks 5–10) Phase 4: Analysis & Report (Weeks 11–14) Phase 5: Presentation Prep (Weeks 15–16)
Weeks 1–2 (Topic & Question): Choose your topic area, narrow to a specific research question, identify what type of project you will do. Review 5–10 relevant papers on Google Scholar. Write a first draft of your introduction and hypothesis. Get ethics approval if needed (consent forms for human participants). Submit your research question to your teacher for feedback before proceeding.
Weeks 3–4 (Background Research): Deep literature review — aim for 8–15 credible sources (peer-reviewed papers, textbooks, reputable science websites). Write your full introduction section. Finalise your hypothesis and variables. Design your data collection tools (survey form, data table). Have your methodology reviewed before you begin collecting data.
Weeks 5–10 (Data Collection): Execute your methodology exactly as planned. Record all raw data carefully and honestly. If something goes wrong, document it — don't hide errors. Collect more data than you think you need (aim for 50–150+ participants for survey studies). Keep a research journal noting any deviations from your plan.
Weeks 11–14 (Analysis & Report): Clean and organise your data. Perform statistical analysis. Create clear graphs/tables. Write results section (present data objectively — no interpretation yet). Write discussion (interpret, connect to literature, address limitations). Write conclusion. Write abstract (last). Compile references. Get teacher feedback.
Weeks 15–16 (Presentation Prep): Prepare your poster or presentation slides. Practise your 3–5 minute oral summary. Practise answering questions from a "judge" (your teacher or parent). Common judge questions: "What would you do differently?", "What was your biggest challenge?", "What are the limitations?", "What are the next steps?" Knowing your work deeply matters more than memorising a script.

Step 6 — Write Your Abstract

📄 The Abstract

The abstract is the most important 250 words you will write. It is the first (and sometimes only) thing judges, reviewers, and the public read. A perfect abstract covers five things: what you studied, why it matters, what you did, what you found, and what it means.

⚠️ ISEF Abstract Rules: Maximum 250 words. Must include: purpose, procedure, data, and conclusions. Cannot include acknowledgements, names of schools, or references. Should be written in past tense (for completed projects) or future tense (for proposals). Must be written by the student, not the mentor or teacher. A copied or AI-generated abstract is disqualification grounds.

📄 Build Your Abstract Sentence by Sentence

Sentence 1–2: Purpose / Background (~40 words)
Why does this topic matter? State the biological/scientific context in 1–2 sentences. Do not start with "This study..." — start with the scientific context.
Sentence 3: Research Question (~15 words)
State your research question clearly.
Sentences 4–6: Methods (~60 words)
Who/what was studied? How many? What did you do? What did you measure? What statistical test did you use?
Sentences 7–8: Results (~50 words)
What did you find? State key numbers/ratios. For a proposal, state your expected findings.
Sentences 9–10: Conclusion & Implications (~40 words)
What do your results mean? How do they connect to broader biology? What might future research do?
YOUR ABSTRACT DRAFT
Tutor tip: Read your abstract out loud when you're done. If you stumble or have to re-read any sentence, it needs to be rewritten. Every sentence should be clear on the first read. Avoid passive voice where possible. "I measured" is clearer than "measurements were taken."
